The research map

Hundreds of GraphRAG papers have appeared since 2023, and more arrive every week. This chapter gives you a map to place each one on, a timeline of the milestones, the directions the 2026 papers are pushing, and a checklist for deciding whether a result is worth building on.

You will be able to

  • Place any GraphRAG paper in a three-stage taxonomy: knowledge organization, knowledge retrieval and knowledge integration
  • Trace the field's milestones from Think-on-Graph and From Local to Global to the 2026 work on cost, time, provenance and modality
  • Summarize what the 2026 papers claim, using only what their abstracts actually say
  • Read a GraphRAG paper critically with a checklist covering baselines, cost, leakage, judges and statistics
  • Set up a weekly routine to keep up with the literature without drowning in it

Why you need a map

By now you have built every major piece of GraphRAG yourself: extraction and resolution (Chapters 8 and 9), communities and reports (11 and 12), local, global, PPR and path retrieval (14 to 19), evaluation (25) and agents with memory (30). The research literature is the same set of ideas recombined, pushed further, and occasionally renamed.

Without a map, every new paper looks like a new system you have to learn from scratch. With one, most papers reduce to a sentence: “a new way to build the index,” “a new policy for walking the graph,” or “a new way to feed the retrieved subgraph to the model.” That sentence tells you which chapter's code it would change, which baseline it should beat, and whether it matters for your use case.

Three surveys from late 2024 and early 2025 are the standard entry points. They slice the same field differently, and it helps to know all three because papers borrow their vocabulary.

One paper, several boxes

Many papers span categories. The curated list files Think-on-Graph under “GraphRAG with existing KGs,” “logical reasoning-based retriever,” “LLM-based retriever” and “graph-enhanced chain of thought,” because it does all four. When you need a single place for a paper, use its main contribution: the component its ablation shows matters.

Knowledge organization: what the graph is for

The first question to ask about any system is what role the graph plays. The distinction is subtle and it predicts almost everything else about the design.

Graphs for knowledge indexing

Here the graph is a navigation structure over the original text. RAPTOR builds a tree of recursive summaries over chunks; HippoRAG 2 links phrase nodes to passage nodes so a PageRank walk ends on passages; LinearRAG argues that relation extraction is the unstable, expensive step and builds its graph without it, for large corpora. The retrieved unit is still a passage, so answers inherit the passages' fidelity and citations come for free. The weakness is that the graph can only be as good as the co-occurrence or similarity signals used to link nodes.

Graphs as knowledge carriers

Here the graph is the knowledge. The list splits this in two. Graphs built from a corpus is Microsoft's From Local to Global (entity graph, communities, reports), GraphReader, Medical Graph RAG, PathRAG and Youtu-GraphRAG; this is what you built in Part 2. GraphRAG with existing knowledge graphs is the older KGQA tradition: Think-on-Graph, Think-on-Graph 2.0 and Reasoning on Graphs walk Freebase or Wikidata. The strength is compact, composable evidence. The weakness is extraction error: a wrong edge is a wrong fact, and nothing in the retrieved context reveals it.

A newer strand questions whether you should build the graph ahead of time at all. You Don't Need Pre-built Graphs for RAG (2508.06105, the method is called LogicRAG) argues that corpus-to-graph conversion costs tokens and update latency, and that a fixed graph may not match the reasoning structure a particular query needs. Instead it decomposes each query into sub-problems, builds a directed acyclic graph of their dependencies at inference time, and retrieves for them in topological order. AutoGraph-R1 (2510.15339) goes the other way: it keeps the pre-built graph but trains the constructor with reinforcement learning, rewarding graphs by how well they serve a downstream RAG pipeline, with separate rewards for the carrier and index roles.

Hybrid GraphRAG

Hybrids treat the graph and the text as complementary. KG²RAG retrieves seed chunks semantically, then expands and organizes them using the knowledge graph. HybGRAG answers questions that need both textual and relational knowledge bases. CodexGraph puts a code repository into a graph database and lets the model query it. Capstones 2 and 4 are hybrids in exactly this sense.

Knowledge retrieval: how the graph is searched

Given a graph, the second question is how a query becomes a subgraph, a path set or a list of passages. The curated list uses seven retriever families. Knowledge integration: how evidence reaches the model

The third stage is how retrieved graph knowledge changes what the model generates. There are two broad routes.

Fine-tuning

Fine-tuning methods change the model's weights so it can consume graph structure directly. The list groups them by granularity. Node-level methods such as GraphGPT tune the model on instructions about individual nodes and their attributes. Path-level methods train on sequences: Reasoning on Graphs tunes the model to generate relation paths that are then instantiated in the KG, and MuseGraph turns graphs into compact descriptions for instruction tuning. Subgraph-level methods such as LLaGA encode whole neighborhoods into the model's input space. Since 2025 a related line trains the retrieval policy rather than the reader with reinforcement learning: Graph-R1 frames retrieval as a multi-turn agent-environment interaction with an end-to-end reward, and AutoGraph-R1 trains the graph constructor. Chapter 24 covers these.

In-context learning

Most production systems never fine-tune. They linearize graph evidence into the prompt (Chapter 22) and structure the reasoning. Graph-enhanced chain of thought interleaves reasoning steps with graph lookups (Think-on-Graph, Graph Chain-of-Thought). Collaborative KG refinement lets the model detect and repair problems in the retrieved graph or its own plan, as Plan-on-Graph does with self-correction. Everything you built in minigraphrag is in-context integration: the [Data: Entities (3)] citation format is a lightweight form of grounding that keeps the model tied to specific records.

A three-question classifier

For any paper, answer: (1) What does a graph node stand for, text or fact? (2) Who decides what to retrieve: similarity, rules, an LLM, a GNN or a trained policy? (3) Are model weights changed? Those three answers place most papers in the explorer above. The rest are benchmarks, surveys, systems papers or critiques, which you should tag as such.

A timeline of milestones, 2023 to 2026

The field did not start in 2024. Knowledge-graph question answering and graph neural networks have long histories. What changed in 2023 is that LLMs became good enough to both build graphs from text and walk them with language. The timeline below lists the papers and releases that each changed what practitioners built. Dates are the month of first arXiv submission.

Read the timeline as three waves. In 2023 the work came from the knowledge-graph QA community: the graph already existed and the question was how an LLM should walk it. In 2024 From Local to Global and its open-source release shifted attention to graphs built from your own documents, and a burst of retrieval ideas followed (PPR, keywords, paths, subgraphs). In 2025 the field turned on itself: unified comparisons such as DIGIMON and GraphRAG-Bench asked when graphs help, while RL-trained constructors and agents tried to make every stage learnable. The 2026 papers, next, mostly attack the costs and blind spots that those comparisons exposed.

What 2026 is pushing on

This section summarizes a sample of 2026 arXiv papers. Every description below restates what the paper's own abstract says, including the numbers, which are the authors' self-reported results. None of them has been independently reproduced in this course. Treat them as claims to test, which is exactly what the checklist later in the chapter is for.

Cost-efficient GraphRAG

LiteRAG (2609.10239) targets query-time cost. Its abstract describes replacing expensive retrieval-time LLM control with query-conditioned algorithmic exploration and reasoning-chain context construction. On DistComp, a multi-hop benchmark over distributed-systems papers, it reports the highest overall quality among the evaluated methods (0.798) while cutting per-query latency by over 100× and cost by over 99% relative to GraphRAG Global and DRIFT; on UltraDomain it reports matching LinearRAG's quality with about 14× fewer tokens. The ablation credits query-adaptive thresholding and community-aware hub penalization. If you have run global search from Chapter 15, the direction makes sense: map-reduce calls the LLM once per batch of reports on every query.

The Commercial Tax (2608.16096) is a critique of how benchmarks report multi-hop retrieval. It audits four leading MuSiQue systems (HippoRAG-2, PropRAG, SAG, KET-RAG) and reports that three depend on NV-Embed-v2, which is licensed cc-by-nc-4.0, for their best numbers without saying so. It measures thirteen embedders on one MuSiQue harness with bootstrap confidence intervals, and reports that a commercially licensed gap to that anchor existed until mid-2026 and was closed by a model released in July 2026. On cost, it reports that three of five audited systems, including Microsoft's GraphRAG, do not disclose indexing cost, and that the only published GraphRAG dollar figures it found span 11× inside a single third-party paper. Its cost model separates one-time embedding from graph construction and recurring answering. The lesson for you: a leaderboard number without a license and a cost is not yet a decision.

Query-adaptive exploration

MOSAIC (2609.11065) starts from an observation you may have made in Chapter 21: direct facts need compact neighborhoods, comparisons need balanced coverage of several targets, and mediated questions need deeper paths. It is training-free: an LLM analyzer turns each query's evidence requirements into a bounded policy over seed selection, traversal, stopping and evidence selection, while the graph, indexes, scoring, grounding and generator stay shared. On GraphRAG-Bench it reports query-weighted answer correctness of 76.97 (Medical) and 64.33 (Novel), and in controlled comparisons on the same graph and generator it reports that no fixed narrow, medium or wide policy was consistently best, with MOSAIC 9.96 points above the strongest fixed policy while evaluating 81.9% fewer paths than the wide one. That controlled comparison, same graph and same generator, is the kind of experiment the checklist below asks for.

Provenance and lineage

LineageRAG (2608.16004) argues that existing GraphRAG methods leave the link between finding evidence and grounding it in source text implicit. It derives “evidence demands” from the query, builds one evidence lineage per demand through demand-conditioned retrieval over the corpus graph, keeps the demand attached to every candidate, and completes a lineage with a verbatim source span when the evidence supports the demand. On HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue it reports average gains of 3.51 R@5, 5.96 EM and 5.22 F1 over leading GraphRAG baselines. For Kestrel, a lineage for “Why did Kestrel start Project Tern?” would carry two demands (why the supply ended, what Kestrel did about it), each ending in a quoted sentence.

Time: bi-temporal and spatio-temporal graphs

post-graph-rag (2608.24921) is an open-source engine that puts chunks with embeddings, a canonical entity graph and community summaries in one PostgreSQL database (pgvector plus edge tables). It validates extraction output before writing, rejecting vague predicates, pronominal names and bare quantities, and it adds a bi-temporal layer that records when a relation held and when the system believed it, superseding incompatible earlier assertions. The abstract reports 94.0% on LongMemEval and attributes the largest single gain to carrying each relation's validity period into the synthesis prompt, which moved temporal reasoning from 0.496 to 0.881 in its ablation. It also reports 0.807 on ECT-QA, an earnings-call benchmark in which every metric is restated each quarter, against published figures of 0.599, 0.406 and 0.405 for TG-RAG, LightRAG and GraphRAG. That is the design you built in Chapter 27 and will extend in Capstone 3. Notice one detail when you read it: the abstract names gemini-3.6-flash for its own 94.0% and gpt-4o for Zep's 71.2, and does not name a generator for the full-context 60.2.

NS-ST-GraphRAG (2609.05139) applies temporal and spatial graphs to long literary narratives, where relations evolve over narrative time. It combines ontology-guided extraction, deterministic constraint checking, dual temporal coordinates, spatial scene attributes and dynamic subgraph retrieval that selects the graph state valid for a query's scope, and introduces Red-Chamber-QA for classical Chinese literature. Its abstract is unusually candid: on a 120-question held-out split it reports 0.733 against 0.675 for the baseline with McNemar exact p = 0.092, described as “directionally favorable but not significant,” notes a closed-book model scores 0.083, and says a pre-specified condition of one of its hypotheses was not supported. Reward that kind of reporting when you see it.

Hypergraphs

HyCE-RAG (2607.22597) notes that pairwise edges cannot represent associations among several entities and contexts at once. It organizes entities, relations and contextual evidence into hyperedges, builds a query-aware evidence hypergraph, propagates confidence over entity-hyperedge incidences, and assembles ranked evidence chains before generation, scoring semantic relevance, connectivity, coverage, relation reliability, extraction confidence and propagated confidence. It reports consistent gains over standard and graph-based RAG on HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue and two GraphRAG-Bench subsets in accuracy, context relevance and faithfulness, and frames hypergraphs as a direction for post-retrieval reasoning. Chapter 31 covers the representation.

Multimodal documents

HVM-GraphRAG (2607.24861) addresses question answering over complex documents where evidence spans regions and modalities. It identifies two problems in existing multimodal GraphRAG, unreliable cross-modal evidence indexing and expensive traversal, and responds with a holistic view that guides graph construction, a compact concept-level graph indexed to supporting multimodal chunks (so retrieval avoids walking a dense entity graph), and modality-specific grouping of retrieved chunks. It reports the best answer performance in most evaluated settings on three datasets with better online retrieval efficiency.

ColGraphRAG (2607.16208) is a narrow, well-controlled study: in a graph-grounded multimodal QA pipeline it replaces only the ranking operator for graph-linked image nodes, swapping single-vector similarity for late-interaction MaxSim scoring in the ColBERT/ColPali lineage, and keeps everything else unchanged. On MultimodalQA it reports improved retrieval-stage estimates and downstream QA gains where visual evidence matters most, mixed trends on text-dominant questions, and explicitly calls for broader validation. Single-operator swaps like this are the easiest papers to reproduce and trust.

Agentic GraphRAG

Two 2025 papers anchor the agentic direction that 2026 work builds on. GraphSearch (2509.22009) names two limitations, shallow retrieval that misses important evidence and inefficient use of the pre-built graph, and proposes a six-module agentic workflow with dual-channel retrieval: semantic queries over chunks and relational queries over the graph, reporting consistent gains on six multi-hop benchmarks. Youtu-GraphRAG (2508.19855) unifies construction and retrieval around one schema: a seed schema bounds an extraction agent and expands to new domains, “dually-perceived” community detection fuses topology with subgraph semantics into a hierarchical knowledge tree, and an agentic retriever uses the same schema to split queries into parallel sub-queries with reflection. It also introduces an anonymized dataset and an “Anonymity Reversion” task to reduce knowledge leakage from pre-training. Its arXiv abstract reports up to 90.71% token-cost savings and 16.62% higher accuracy over the strongest baselines it compares against; the later ICLR listing reports 33.60% lower token cost with the same accuracy gain, so cite the version you evaluate.

Open problems

The same problems recur across surveys, critiques and your own experiments in this course. Each is a good place to contribute.

  • When do graphs actually help? GraphRAG-Bench was motivated by reports that GraphRAG often underperforms vanilla RAG on real tasks. Your own Kestrel results probably showed the same shape: graphs win on multi-hop and global questions and can lose on single-hop lookups. A reliable way to predict, per query or per corpus, whether to use the graph is still open. Routers in Capstone 1 are a practical but crude answer.
  • Cost that scales with the corpus. LLM extraction and per-community summarization dominate indexing (Chapter 26). Relation-free graphs (LinearRAG), lazy indexing and trained small extractors all help, but systems still rarely publish cost next to quality, as The Commercial Tax documents.
  • Graph quality you cannot see. A wrong edge looks exactly like a right one in the prompt. Validating extraction before writing (post-graph-rag), training constructors against downstream utility (AutoGraph-R1) and verbatim grounding (LineageRAG) are three different answers to the same problem.
  • Change over time. Most benchmarks are static snapshots. Real corpora supersede facts, as the Kestrel CTO change shows. Evaluations of incremental updates and temporal correctness are still thin.
  • Evaluation validity. LLM judges have position and length bias (Chapter 25), public benchmarks leak into pre-training (the motivation for Youtu-GraphRAG's anonymized dataset), and comparisons often change the generator or embedder along with the method.
  • Security and access control. Community summaries mix text from documents with different permissions, and injected instructions can ride along with retrieved evidence (Chapter 29). There is little published work on ACL-correct GraphRAG; Capstone 4 makes you confront it.
  • Beyond binary text graphs. Hyperedges, images, tables, code and time all strain the entity-relation-entity model, and each extension currently comes with its own pipeline.

Reading a GraphRAG paper critically

A paper's abstract is an advertisement for its best result. Your job is to find out whether that result will survive on your data, with your models, at your budget. Use this checklist on every paper you are considering building on. Most items can be checked in ten minutes with the paper's tables and repository.

The checklist applied to one abstract

Take post-graph-rag's LongMemEval claim: 94.0% for its engine versus 71.2% for Zep and 60.2% for full context. Item 2 flags it immediately: the abstract names gemini-3.6-flash for its own run and gpt-4o for the Zep number, so part of the gap may come from the generator. Item 9 is answered well: the abstract reports a paired ablation on the same graph for its largest single contribution (temporal grounding in the prompt, 0.496 to 0.881 on temporal reasoning). Item 10 is answered well too: code links are given. A fair next step is to rerun the Zep comparison with the same generator before concluding the architecture is the cause.

Read beyond the abstract in this order: the experimental setup table (models, datasets, sizes), the main results table with per-type breakdowns, the ablation table, the cost or efficiency section, the limitations section, and only then the method section. You will skip the method section of most papers, and that is the point.

Curated lists have errors too

Lists are edited by hand and papers change after they are listed. arXiv 2412.12612, for example, first appeared as SynthCypher and is now titled Auto-Cypher, so a list entry and the paper can disagree. Before you cite or classify a paper, open its arXiv page and check the current title and authors.

How to keep up

A query that finds the new papers

arXiv's export API is free, needs no key, and supports field queries and sorting by submission date. The script below fetches the latest matches, proposes taxonomy tags from keyword rules, and flags two checklist signals visible in the abstract alone: whether cost is reported and whether code is linked. It uses only the standard library. Be gentle with the API; one request per run with a few dozen results is plenty.

"""Weekly GraphRAG radar: fetch new arXiv papers, tag them with the taxonomy, write a triage list."""
from __future__ import annotations

import re
import sys
import urllib.parse
import urllib.request
import xml.etree.ElementTree as ET

API = "https://export.arxiv.org/api/query"
QUERY = ('abs:GraphRAG OR ti:GraphRAG OR abs:"graph retrieval-augmented generation" '
         'OR (abs:"knowledge graph" AND abs:"retrieval-augmented")')
ATOM = {"a": "http://www.w3.org/2005/Atom"}

# Keyword rules that propose a place in the taxonomy. A human confirms it after reading the abstract.
TAGS = {
    "organization/indexing": r"\bindex(ing)?\b|tree|hierarch|passage graph",
    "organization/kg-construction": r"construct|extraction|schema|ontolog",
    "retrieval/llm-agent": r"agent|agentic|iterative|multi-turn|reflection",
    "retrieval/gnn": r"\bGNN\b|graph neural",
    "retrieval/ppr-paths": r"pagerank|path|random walk|traversal",
    "integration/fine-tuning": r"fine-tun|reinforcement|\bRL\b|instruction tun",
    "cross-cutting/cost": r"cost|latency|token|efficien",
    "cross-cutting/temporal": r"temporal|time-aware|bi-temporal",
    "cross-cutting/multimodal": r"multimodal|image|visual|table",
    "cross-cutting/hypergraph": r"hypergraph|hyperedge|n-ary",
    "cross-cutting/evaluation": r"benchmark|evaluat",
    "cross-cutting/provenance": r"provenance|lineage|grounding|citation",
}


def fetch(max_results: int = 50) -> list[dict]:
    params = urllib.parse.urlencode({"search_query": QUERY, "sortBy": "submittedDate",
                                     "sortOrder": "descending", "max_results": max_results})
    with urllib.request.urlopen(f"{API}?{params}", timeout=30) as resp:
        root = ET.fromstring(resp.read())
    papers = []
    for e in root.findall("a:entry", ATOM):
        abstract = " ".join(e.find("a:summary", ATOM).text.split())
        papers.append({
            "id": e.find("a:id", ATOM).text.rsplit("/", 1)[-1],
            "date": e.find("a:published", ATOM).text[:10],
            "title": " ".join(e.find("a:title", ATOM).text.split()),
            "abstract": abstract,
            "tags": [t for t, pat in TAGS.items() if re.search(pat, abstract, re.I)],
            # Checklist signals visible in the abstract alone.
            "reports_cost": bool(re.search(r"cost|token|latency|\$|USD", abstract)),
            "has_code": bool(re.search(r"github\.com|code (is )?available|open-source", abstract, re.I)),
        })
    return papers


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for p in fetch(int(sys.argv[1]) if len(sys.argv) > 1 else 25):
        flags = ("cost " if p["reports_cost"] else "") + ("code" if p["has_code"] else "")
        print(f"{p['date']}  {p['id']:<14} {p['title'][:80]}\n    tags: {', '.join(p['tags']) or '-'}  {flags}")

Run it once a week (python research/radar.py 50). The tags are suggestions, not truth: in a sample run, MOSAIC was tagged “ppr-paths” because its abstract mentions paths, while a human would file it under query-adaptive LLM-based retrieval. That gap is why the triage step below exists.

  3. Vanilla RAG baseline. Needs the paper: not mentioned in the abstract.
  4. Cost. Answered, strongly: latency over 100× lower and cost over 99% lower than GraphRAG Global and DRIFT, 14× fewer tokens than LinearRAG. Check whether indexing cost is also reported, since the abstract talks about query time.
  5. Question types. Needs the paper: DistComp is multi-hop; UltraDomain is closer to sensemaking. Look for a per-type breakdown.
  6. Leakage. Partly reassuring: a benchmark over distributed-systems papers is less likely to be memorized as QA pairs than Wikipedia, but the papers themselves may be in pre-training.
  7. Judge bias. Needs the paper: “overall quality 0.798” suggests a judged or composite score; check how it was produced.
  8. Significance. Needs the paper: no intervals in the abstract. “Matches LinearRAG” in particular needs an interval to mean anything.
  9. Ablation. Answered: query-adaptive thresholding and community-aware hub penalization drive the token efficiency.
  10. Reproducibility. Needs the paper: no code link in the abstract.

Two questions for Kestrel: (1) Global questions such as “What are the main themes?” have no seed entities. How does query-conditioned exploration choose where to start, and how does quality on UltraDomain's broad questions compare with map-reduce global search per question type? (2) Is the 99% cost reduction measured against global search at a fixed community level or with dynamic community selection, which already cuts map calls substantially?

Exercise 33.3 Core

Design an experiment, runnable with minigraphrag on the Kestrel corpus, that tests MOSAIC's central claim at small scale: that no single fixed exploration policy is best for every question type. Specify the policies, the fixed components, the question sets, the metrics, how you control for cost, and what result would falsify the claim on this corpus.

Show answer

Fixed components. One index built once with build_index; one embedder; one generator model for every answer; the same response type and a fixed context budget (say 4,000 tokens) so a wider policy cannot win by reading more.

Policies. Three fixed policies over hipporag_search, differing in how many passages reach the context: narrow (3), medium (5) and wide (10, truncated to the budget). Seed selection stays fixed at the five seeds link_query_to_nodes returns, because hipporag_search does not expose that parameter; varying seeds as well means calling link_query_to_nodes and personalized_pagerank yourself. Add an oracle-routed policy that picks, per question, the best fixed policy in hindsight (an upper bound), and a simple rule-based router that picks narrow for single-hop, wide for multi-hop.

Questions. The 23 questions in data/questions.jsonl sliced by type (single-hop, multi-hop, temporal, global), plus 20 new ones you write so the router is not tuned on its test set.

Metrics. Context recall against supporting_docs (retrieval quality, no judge needed), token F1 against the gold answers, and LLM calls and context tokens per question.

from minigraphrag.evaluation import context_recall, load_questions
from minigraphrag.indexer import load_index
from minigraphrag.llm import get_llm
from minigraphrag.search.ppr import hipporag_search

POLICIES = {"narrow": 3, "medium": 5, "wide": 10}
index, llm = load_index(".index"), get_llm("openai", cache_dir=".cache/llm")
rows = []
for q in load_questions("data/questions.jsonl"):
    for name, k in POLICIES.items():
        r = hipporag_search(index, q["question"], llm, top_k_passages=k, max_context_tokens=4000)
        rows.append((q["type"], name, context_recall(r.sources, q["supporting_docs"])))
for qtype in sorted({t for t, _, _ in rows}):
    print(qtype, {p: round(sum(s for t, n, s in rows if t == qtype and n == p) /
                           sum(1 for t, n, _ in rows if t == qtype and n == p), 2) for p in POLICIES})

Analysis. Compute per-type means with bootstrap intervals over questions. The claim is supported on Kestrel if the best fixed policy differs between types with non-overlapping (or paired-significant) intervals, and if the oracle-routed policy beats the best single fixed policy overall. It is falsified here if one fixed policy is within the interval of the best on every type. With about 5 to 8 questions per type, expect wide intervals; say so, and treat the result as a pilot that motivates a larger question set rather than a verdict.

Extend radar.py so that it (a) remembers which arXiv IDs it has already shown, (b) writes new papers to a triage.md file with empty fields for your three-question classification and checklist items 2, 4, 5 and 8, and (c) never shows a paper twice across versions (2609.11065v1 and v2 are the same paper).

import json
import re
from pathlib import Path

from radar import fetch

SEEN = Path("research/seen.json")


def base_id(arxiv_id: str) -> str:
    return re.sub(r"v\d+$", "", arxiv_id)          # 2609.11065v2 -> 2609.11065


def triage(max_results: int = 50) -> int:
    SEEN.parent.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    seen = set(json.loads(SEEN.read_text())) if SEEN.exists() else set()
    new = [p for p in fetch(max_results) if base_id(p["id"]) not in seen]
    lines = []
    for p in new:
        lines += [f"## {p['title']} ({base_id(p['id'])}, {p['date']})",
                  f"https://arxiv.org/abs/{base_id(p['id'])}",
                  f"- Suggested tags: {', '.join(p['tags']) or 'none'}",
                  "- Node meaning (text or fact):",
                  "- Who decides retrieval:",
                  "- Weights trained (yes/no):",
                  f"- [2] same models/graph as baselines: ",
                  f"- [4] cost reported: {'abstract mentions cost' if p['reports_cost'] else 'check paper'}",
                  "- [5] per-type results: ",
                  "- [8] intervals or tests: ",
                  "- Decision (skip / read / reproduce):", ""]
        seen.add(base_id(p["id"]))
    if lines:
        with Path("research/triage.md").open("a") as f:
            f.write("\n".join(lines) + "\n")
    SEEN.write_text(json.dumps(sorted(seen)))
    return len(new)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"{triage()} new papers added to research/triage.md")

Storing base IDs handles version bumps. Appending rather than overwriting keeps your notes. If you run this in a scheduled job, commit seen.json and triage.md so your notes have history.

def ci(xs, n=2000, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    means = sorted(sum(rng.choice(xs) for _ in xs) / len(xs) for _ in range(n))
    return means[int(0.025 * n)], means[int(0.975 * n) - 1]


groups = defaultdict(list)
for row in result["rows"]:

print("| type | method | recall [95% CI] | F1 [95% CI] |")

Check your understanding

In HippoRAG 2, PageRank mass ends on passage nodes, and the LLM reads those passages. Where does it belong in the organization taxonomy?

  1. Graph as knowledge carrier, because it has entity nodes
  2. Graphs for knowledge indexing, because the graph navigates to text that the LLM reads
  3. Hybrid GraphRAG, because it uses embeddings
  4. Knowledge integration, because it changes the prompt
The deciding question is what the LLM consumes as evidence. HippoRAG 2 uses the graph to rank passages; the passages are the evidence. The curated list files it under graphs for indexing.

  3. Ablation
  4. Same generator, embedder and graph for all systems
With different generators, part or all of the 6 points may come from the model rather than the method. Copied baseline numbers are one of the most common ways this happens.

Questions learners often ask

Should I read the surveys cover to cover?

No. Read the framework section of one survey (the knowledge organization, retrieval and integration sections of 2501.13958 map directly to this course), then use the surveys as indexes: when a paper cites an idea you don't recognize, look it up in the survey's table of methods. Surveys date quickly; use the curated lists for anything after early 2025.

How do I know whether a 2026 result is worth building on when nobody has reproduced it?

Look for three things: a controlled comparison (same graph and generator), an ablation that isolates one component, and released code. If all three are present, reimplement just that component inside minigraphrag and test it on Kestrel and on your own data. ColGraphRAG-style single-operator papers are the easiest to verify this way. If none are present, wait.

Is GraphRAG research converging, or fragmenting?

Both. The pipeline stages have converged: nearly every paper fits the organization, retrieval, integration frame, and the same baselines (GraphRAG, LightRAG, HippoRAG) recur. The extensions are fragmenting into time, modality, hypergraphs, code and agents, each with its own benchmarks. For practitioners the convergence matters more: a small set of patterns, which you have now built, covers most systems.

Microsoft GraphRAG is in maintenance mode. Does that mean the approach is dead?

No. It means one reference implementation is no longer being actively extended. The ideas it popularized (entity graphs from text, community reports, local and global search) are in LightRAG, neo4j-graphrag, LlamaIndex and many 2026 papers, and several of those papers still use GraphRAG Global and DRIFT as baselines. Chapter 28 discusses what to use instead.

Where can I contribute as an engineer rather than a researcher?

Reproductions with honest cost numbers, evaluations on new domains with per-type breakdowns, and security work (ACL-correct summaries, injection through documents) are all underserved and valued. A well-documented reproduction that shows a published gain shrinking under a fair baseline is a real contribution.

Key takeaways

  • Place papers with three questions: what a node stands for, who decides what to retrieve, and whether weights are trained.
  • Knowledge organization distinguishes graphs that index text from graphs that carry facts; hybrids keep both.
  • Retrieval families trade query-time LLM calls against flexibility; integration is either fine-tuning at node, path or subgraph level or in-context prompting.
  • The 2026 papers push on cost, query-adaptive exploration, provenance, time, hyperedges, modality and agents; their numbers are self-reported claims until reproduced.
  • Read results critically: same models across systems, fair vanilla baselines, cost, per-type results, leakage, judge bias, significance, ablations and licenses.
  • Keep up with a small weekly routine and reproduce one component a month rather than reading everything.
