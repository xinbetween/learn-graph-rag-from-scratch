Community detection: Louvain and Leiden

Global questions like “what are the main themes in this corpus?” have no single chunk that answers them. GraphRAG's answer is to split the entity graph into communities and summarize each one. In this chapter you compute modularity by hand, implement Louvain from scratch, and learn why GraphRAG uses hierarchical Leiden instead.

You will be able to

  • Explain why communities are the unit of summarization for global questions and why a hierarchy helps
  • Compute modularity Q and the modularity gain ΔQ of moving a node, by hand and in code
  • Implement Louvain's local-moving and aggregation phases and trace them on the Kestrel graph
  • Use the resolution parameter deliberately and recognize the resolution limit
  • Explain Louvain's badly connected communities and how Leiden's refinement phase fixes them
  • Build a hierarchical partition with a maximum cluster size, control randomness with seeds, and evaluate the result

Why communities

By now the Kestrel corpus is a resolved graph (Chapter 9) of 17 core entities and their relationships. Local search (Chapter 14) will answer “What connects Priya Nair to Deepcast?” by starting at those two nodes and walking outwards. But canonical question 4, “What are the main themes in this corpus?”, has no starting node. Vector RAG fails at it too: the top-k chunks for “main themes” are whichever chunks happen to use theme-like words (Chapter 3).

The GraphRAG paper's idea is to precompute answers to “what is going on in this part of the corpus?” before anyone asks. To do that, it needs parts. A community is a set of nodes that are more densely connected to each other than to the rest of the graph. In Kestrel you can almost see them: the reef science cluster around Project Sentinel, Tidewater Institute and Halden Reef; the supply-chain cluster of Marlow Dynamics, Deepcast and Project Tern; the funding pair Brightwater Capital and Lena Park; the regulation cluster of the Ocean Health Act, the city council and Port Avalon.

Communities serve three purposes in GraphRAG:

  • Summarization units. Each community gets an LLM-written report (Chapter 12). A community is small enough for its entities and relationships to fit in a prompt, and coherent enough that the report has a topic.
  • Global search. Global search (Chapter 15) maps the question over community reports and reduces the partial answers. The themes in question 4 are, roughly, the communities.
  • A hierarchy of zoom levels. Big communities are split into sub-communities. Coarse levels give broad themes (“Kestrel's business”), fine levels give specific ones (“the Deepcast supply shock”), and a query can pick the level that fits.

Community detection is unsupervised: nobody labels the themes. So you need an objective that says what a good partition is. The one GraphRAG uses is modularity.

Modularity

A partition puts every node in exactly one community. A good partition has many edges inside communities and few between them. “Many” needs a baseline, because a community of hubs will have internal edges by chance. Modularity (Newman and Girvan, 2004) compares the fraction of edge weight inside communities with the fraction you would expect if edges were rewired at random while keeping every node's degree.

The formula

Let m be the total edge weight, Aij the weight between nodes i and j, ki the (weighted) degree of i, and ci its community. Then

Q = (1 / 2m) Σi,j [ Aij − γ · ki kj / 2m ] · δ(ci, cj)

The term ki kj/2m is the expected weight between i and j in a random graph with the same degrees; δ is 1 when both nodes share a community. γ is the resolution, 1 in the original definition. Summing over pairs inside each community gives the form you will actually compute:

Q = Σc [ Lc / m − γ · ( dc / 2m )² ]

where Lc is the edge weight inside community c and dc is the sum of degrees of its nodes. The first term is “share of edges inside c”; the second is “share you would expect inside c by chance”. Q is always less than 1; it is 0 when everything is in one community, negative when communities have fewer internal edges than chance, and values above roughly 0.3 usually indicate real structure.

A fully worked example

Take six Kestrel entities and seven unweighted edges: a funding triangle (Kestrel Labs, Brightwater Capital, Lena Park), a reef triangle (Project Sentinel, Halden Reef, the bleaching event), and the bridge “Kestrel Labs runs Project Sentinel”.

Step by step for the two-triangle partition. Degrees: K and S have 3, the other four have 2, so 2m = 14 and m = 7.

  • Community {K, B, P}: internal edges K–B, K–P, B–P, so L = 3. Degree sum d = 3 + 2 + 2 = 7. Contribution 3/7 − (7/14)² = 0.4286 − 0.25 = 0.1786.
  • Community {S, H, E}: the same by symmetry, 0.1786.
  • Q = 0.357. The bridge K–S is the only edge not counted in any Lc.

Now a worse partition: move Kestrel Labs to the reef side, giving {B, P} and {K, S, H, E}. The first has L = 1 (B–P) and d = 4, contributing 1/7 − (4/14)² = 0.1429 − 0.0816 = 0.0612. The second has L = 4 (K–S, S–H, S–E, H–E) and d = 10, contributing 4/7 − (10/14)² = 0.5714 − 0.5102 = 0.0612. Q = 0.122, far below 0.357: Kestrel Labs brought two internal edges' worth of degree into a community where it has only one edge.

The gain of moving one node

Louvain never recomputes Q from scratch. It asks a cheaper question: how much does Q change if I move node i into community C? Take i out of its community first, so it sits alone. The gain of inserting an isolated node i into C is

ΔQ(i → C) = ki,in(C) / m − γ · Σtot(C) · ki / 2m²

where ki,in(C) is the edge weight from i into C and Σtot(C) is the total degree of C's nodes (without i). Everything is local: you need i's edges and one running total per community. The gain of moving i from A to B is ΔQ(i → B) − ΔQ(i → A without i).

Check it on the move above: Kestrel Labs from {K, B, P} to {S, H, E}, with kK = 3, m = 7 and 2m² = 98.

  • Back into its own community without it, {B, P}: kin = 2 (edges to B and P), Σtot = 4. ΔQ = 2/7 − 4 · 3/98 = 0.2857 − 0.1224 = 0.1633.
  • Into {S, H, E}: kin = 1 (edge to S), Σtot = 7. ΔQ = 1/7 − 7 · 3/98 = 0.1429 − 0.2143 = −0.0714.
  • Net change: −0.0714 − 0.1633 = −0.2347. And indeed 0.1224 − 0.3571 = −0.2347.

A positive example from the start of Louvain, where every node is alone: move Lena Park into Brightwater's singleton community. Removing her from her own singleton gains nothing (kin = 0, Σtot = 0), and inserting her next to Brightwater gives 1/7 − 2 · 2/98 = 0.1429 − 0.0408 = +0.1020. You can verify with full recomputation: Q goes from −34/196 to −14/196, a change of 20/196 = 0.1020.

def modularity(G: nx.Graph, partition: list[set], resolution: float = 1.0, weight: str = "weight") -> float:
    """Newman modularity of `partition` (a list of node sets covering G)."""
    m = G.size(weight=weight)
    if m == 0:
        return 0.0
    q = 0.0
    for community in partition:
        inside = G.subgraph(community).size(weight=weight)
        degree = sum(d for _, d in G.degree(community, weight=weight))
        q += inside / m - resolution * (degree / (2 * m)) ** 2
    return q

Louvain from scratch

Maximizing modularity exactly is NP-hard. The Louvain method (Blondel, Guillaume, Lambiotte and Lefebvre, 2008) is a greedy heuristic that is fast enough for graphs with millions of nodes and finds high-modularity partitions in practice. It alternates two phases until nothing changes.

Phase 1: local moving

  1. Put every node in its own community.
  2. Visit nodes in some order (random, controlled by a seed). For node i, compute the weight from i into each neighboring community, remove i from its community, and compute ΔQ for inserting it into each neighboring community and back into its own.
  3. Move i to the community with the largest gain (stay if no gain is positive relative to staying).
  4. Repeat passes over all nodes until a full pass moves nothing.

Only neighboring communities are considered, because moving a node into a community it has no edges to always has negative gain (ki,in = 0).

Phase 2: aggregation

Build a new graph whose nodes are the communities found in phase 1. The weight between two super-nodes is the total weight of edges between their communities; the weight inside a community becomes a self-loop. Modularity of the aggregated graph with singleton communities equals modularity of the phase-1 partition, so no progress is lost. Run phase 1 again on the smaller graph. Stop when phase 1 makes no move. The result maps every original node to its final super-node.

Phase 2 would collapse those pairs into super-nodes, and the next round of local moving would merge them into one supply-chain community {Priya Nair, Marlow Dynamics, Project Tern, Deepcast}. That is exactly what the full reference implementation returns with seed=1: four communities with Q = 0.338. The pairs illustrate why aggregation matters: a single node moving alone can't see that two pairs belong together.

The implementation

minigraphrag implements Louvain in about 80 lines on plain adjacency dictionaries. Phase 1 is _one_level; phase 2 is _aggregate.

def _one_level(adj: dict, resolution: float, rng: random.Random) -> tuple[dict, bool]:
    """Phase 1 (local moving) on an adjacency dict {node: {nbr: weight}} that may contain self-loops."""
    # Self-loops count twice toward degree (both "ends" are on the node).
    k = {u: sum(w for v, w in nbrs.items() if v != u) + 2 * nbrs.get(u, 0.0) for u, nbrs in adj.items()}
    m = sum(k.values()) / 2
    node2com = {u: u for u in adj}
    sigma_tot = dict(k)  # total degree of each community
    improved = False
    nodes = list(adj)
    rng.shuffle(nodes)  # visiting order changes the result; the seed makes it reproducible

    moved = True
    while moved:
        moved = False
        for u in nodes:
            ku, current = k[u], node2com[u]
            # Weight from u into each neighbouring community.
            w2c: dict = defaultdict(float)
            for v, w in adj[u].items():
                if v != u:
                    w2c[node2com[v]] += w
            # Take u out of its community first...
            sigma_tot[current] -= ku
            # ...then the gain of inserting u into community C is
            #   k_{u,in}(C) / m  -  gamma * sigma_tot(C) * k_u / (2 m^2)
            best, best_gain = current, w2c[current] / m - resolution * sigma_tot[current] * ku / (2 * m * m)
            for com, w in sorted(w2c.items(), key=lambda x: str(x[0])):
                gain = w / m - resolution * sigma_tot[com] * ku / (2 * m * m)
                if gain > best_gain + 1e-12:
                    best, best_gain = com, gain
            sigma_tot[best] += ku
            if best != current:
                node2com[u] = best
                moved = improved = True
    return node2com, improved


def _aggregate(adj: dict, node2com: dict) -> dict:
    """Phase 2: build the graph whose nodes are communities. Internal weight becomes a self-loop."""
    new: dict = defaultdict(lambda: defaultdict(float))
    for u, nbrs in adj.items():
        cu = node2com[u]
        new[cu]  # make sure isolated communities survive
        for v, w in nbrs.items():
            cv = node2com[v]
            if u == v:
                new[cu][cu] += w
            elif cu == cv:
                new[cu][cu] += w / 2  # each internal edge is seen from both ends
            else:
                new[cu][cv] += w
    return {u: dict(nbrs) for u, nbrs in new.items()}


def louvain_communities(G: nx.Graph, resolution: float = 1.0, seed: int = 42, weight: str = "weight") -> list[set]:
    """Partition G with the Louvain method. Returns a list of node sets, largest first."""
    if G.number_of_edges() == 0:
        return [{n} for n in G.nodes()]
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    adj = {u: {} for u in G.nodes()}
    for u, v, d in G.edges(data=True):
        w = float(d.get(weight, 1.0))
        adj[u][v] = adj[u].get(v, 0.0) + w
        if u != v:
            adj[v][u] = adj[v].get(u, 0.0) + w
    membership = {n: n for n in G.nodes()}  # original node -> current super-node

    while True:
        node2com, improved = _one_level(adj, resolution, rng)
        if not improved:
            break
        membership = {n: node2com[c] for n, c in membership.items()}
        adj = _aggregate(adj, node2com)

    groups: dict = defaultdict(set)
    for n, c in membership.items():
        groups[c].add(n)
    return sorted(groups.values(), key=lambda s: (-len(s), sorted(map(str, s))))

Three details are easy to get wrong when you write this yourself. The self-loop weight counts twice toward a super-node's degree, because both ends of an internal edge are inside it. An internal edge between two different nodes of the same community is visited from both endpoints during aggregation, hence w / 2. And the candidate list is sorted before comparing gains, so that ties break the same way on every run, independent of dictionary insertion order.

Cost

Each pass of phase 1 touches every edge a constant number of times, so one pass is O(m). The number of passes per level is small in practice, and each aggregation shrinks the graph dramatically, so Louvain behaves close to linearly in the number of edges on real graphs. For GraphRAG, community detection is never the bottleneck: clustering a graph of 100,000 entities is a matter of seconds to minutes, while writing reports for its communities takes thousands of LLM calls.

Resolution and its limit

The resolution γ scales the “expected by chance” term. Larger γ penalizes big communities more, so partitions get finer; smaller γ favors fewer, larger communities. On the six-node example the two triangles score 6/7 − γ/2 and one big community scores 1 − γ, so merging wins exactly when γ < 2/7 ≈ 0.286. On the Kestrel graph, running the reference implementation with three seeds per setting gives:

Two lessons. At γ = 1 the result depends on the seed, because this small graph has several partitions with nearly equal modularity (0.338, 0.335, 0.324, 0.320). And no single γ recovers the “expected communities” listed in the running example; their modularity is only 0.270. Modularity is one reasonable definition of a good partition, not a ground truth about themes.

The resolution limit

Fortunato and Barthélemy (2007) showed that modularity optimization cannot resolve communities below a scale that depends on the size of the whole graph. The null model's expected weight between two groups shrinks as the graph grows, so in a large graph even a single edge between two small, dense, otherwise separate groups can make merging them increase Q. Roughly, groups whose internal edge count is small compared with √(2m) are at risk. The classic demonstration is a ring of many small cliques joined by single edges: modularity prefers merging neighboring cliques in pairs.

For GraphRAG this has a practical consequence. Index 10,000 documents and a small, tight cluster like {Brightwater Capital, Lena Park} can be swallowed into a bigger community, and its report never gets written. Two remedies: raise γ, or do what GraphRAG does and split large communities recursively, so every community is re-examined inside its own subgraph where the “whole graph” is small.

From Louvain to Leiden

Louvain has a defect that went largely unnoticed for a decade: it can produce communities that are badly connected, and even communities that are internally disconnected.

How a community falls apart

Suppose phase 1 builds {Kestrel Labs, Dr. Mira Okafor, Jonas Vehl, Brightwater Capital, Lena Park}. Kestrel Labs is the hub that holds it together: Okafor and Vehl connect to the others only through it. Later in the same pass, or after an aggregation that changes the neighborhood, Kestrel Labs gets a better gain from joining a different community and moves. What happens to the nodes it left behind? On a graph this small, Okafor and Vehl now have no edges inside the community and would drift away on the next sweep. The damaging case is the one Traag and colleagues describe: the hub bridged two groups that are each dense inside. Every member of each group still has most of its weight inside the old community, so no single-node move improves modularity, and Louvain only ever moves one node at a time. The community stays whole although nothing connects its two halves. Worse, if this happens after aggregation, the community is already a single super-node, and no later step can split it.

Traag, Waltman and van Eck, From Louvain to Leiden: guaranteeing well-connected communities (2019)

Published in Scientific Reports, this paper shows that Louvain may yield arbitrarily badly connected communities, and in the worst case disconnected ones, especially when run iteratively. In the authors' experiments, up to 25% of communities were badly connected and up to 16% were disconnected. The Leiden algorithm they introduce is proven to yield connected communities, converges (when iterated) to partitions in which all subsets of all communities are locally optimally assigned, and in their benchmarks runs faster than Louvain while finding better partitions.

The refinement phase

Leiden keeps Louvain's structure and changes two things.

Fast local moving. Instead of sweeping every node repeatedly, Leiden keeps a queue. When a node moves, only its neighbors that are not in the new community are added back to the queue. Most work concentrates where the partition is still changing, which is where the speedup comes from.

Refinement. After local moving produces a partition P, Leiden builds a refined partition Pref inside each community of P. Every node starts as its own sub-community. A node is considered for merging only if it is well connected to the rest of its community, and it may merge only into a sub-community that is itself well connected to the community. Among the candidate sub-communities with non-negative gain, one is chosen at random, with probability increasing in the gain (the randomness parameter controls how greedy this is). Because merges only happen along edges, every refined sub-community is connected.

Aggregation on the refined partition. The aggregated graph has one super-node per refined sub-community, not per community. But the initial partition of the aggregated graph is taken from P, so super-nodes that came from the same community start together. If a community of P was disconnected, its pieces are separate super-nodes and the next local-moving phase can separate them. That is the mechanism behind the connectivity guarantee.

LouvainLeiden
Local movingFull sweeps until no moveQueue of nodes whose neighborhood changed

GraphRAG cares because a disconnected community produces a report about entities that have nothing to do with each other. “Okafor, Vehl, Brightwater and Park” without Kestrel Labs is not a theme. Leiden's guarantee is cheap insurance for summary quality.

Hierarchical Leiden in GraphRAG

GraphRAG does not stop at one partition. It runs hierarchical Leiden: cluster the whole graph (level 0); for every community larger than max_cluster_size, run Leiden again on that community's subgraph to produce level 1; repeat until every community is small enough. Communities that are already small are final and do not appear at deeper levels. Microsoft GraphRAG's defaults are max_cluster_size = 10, use_lcc = true and a fixed seed (0xDEADBEEF), with the clustering done by graspologic's native Leiden implementation.

from graspologic.partition import hierarchical_leiden

clusters = hierarchical_leiden(G, max_cluster_size=10, random_seed=0xDEADBEEF)
for c in clusters[:5]:
    # HierarchicalCluster(node, cluster, parent_cluster, level, is_final_cluster)
    print(c.node, c.cluster, c.parent_cluster, c.level, c.is_final_cluster)

first = clusters.first_level_hierarchical_clustering()   # {node: cluster} at level 0
final = clusters.final_level_hierarchical_clustering()   # {node: deepest cluster}

Two defaults deserve attention. use_lcc keeps only the largest connected component, so entities in small disconnected islands (an extracted person mentioned once alongside one company) get no community and never appear in any report. That is usually noise, but check what you drop. And max_cluster_size is the real knob for report granularity: it caps how many entities a leaf report must cover.

The minigraphrag version

hierarchical_communities uses graspologic when it is installed and falls back to recursive Louvain otherwise. The recursion is the same idea: partition, then split any part above max_cluster_size inside its own subgraph.

@dataclass
class Community:
    id: int
    level: int
    parent: int | None
    nodes: list[str]
    edges: list[tuple[str, str]]
    children: list[int] = field(default_factory=list)


def hierarchical_communities(
    G: nx.Graph, max_cluster_size: int = 10, seed: int = 42, use_leiden: bool | None = None
) -> list[Community]:
    """Recursive partition: any community larger than `max_cluster_size` is split again one level down.

    Level 0 is the coarsest partition. Uses graspologic's hierarchical Leiden
    if installed (or `use_leiden=True`), otherwise recursive Louvain.
    """
    if use_leiden is not False:
        result = _leiden_hierarchy(G, max_cluster_size, seed)
        if result is not None:
            return result
        if use_leiden:
            raise ImportError("graspologic is not installed (pip install minigraphrag[leiden])")

    communities: list[Community] = []

    def recurse(nodes: set, level: int, parent: int | None) -> None:
        parts = louvain_communities(G.subgraph(nodes), seed=seed)
        if parent is not None and len(parts) <= 1:
            return  # Louvain can't split it further; stop here
        for part in parts:
            c = Community(len(communities), level, parent, sorted(part),
                          sorted(tuple(sorted(e)) for e in G.subgraph(part).edges()))
            communities.append(c)
            if parent is not None:
                communities[parent].children.append(c.id)
            if len(part) > max_cluster_size:
                recurse(set(part), level + 1, c.id)

    recurse(set(G.nodes()), 0, None)
    return communities

Determinism and seeds

Louvain's visiting order and Leiden's randomized refinement both make results depend on a random seed. For GraphRAG this is not academic: community ids change, reports get regenerated, cached answers go stale, and your evaluation numbers wobble between index builds. On the Kestrel graph, ten seeds of the reference Louvain give Q values of 0.3384 (six seeds), 0.3345, 0.3237 (two seeds) and 0.3203, with three or four communities.

Practical rules: always pass a fixed seed and record it with the index; sort node and edge lists before clustering so that input order does not act as a hidden second seed (GraphRAG sorts its edge list before clustering for the same reason); and if stability matters more than the last bit of modularity, run several seeds and keep the partition that agrees most with the others (a consensus choice) rather than the one with the highest Q.

Evaluating communities

There is rarely ground truth, so combine structural checks with a look at what the reports will be about.

  • Modularity at each level, as a sanity check (well above 0), not as the goal.
  • Connectivity: every community's induced subgraph should be connected. With Louvain, check and split; with Leiden it is guaranteed.
  • Size distribution: a single giant community or hundreds of singletons both mean poor report units. Count communities above max_cluster_size at the leaf level (should be none) and below 3 nodes.
  • Conductance: cut weight divided by the smaller of the community's volume and the rest's; low is good. It flags communities that are mostly boundary.
  • Stability: adjusted Rand index or normalized mutual information between partitions from different seeds. Low agreement means the structure is weak and reports will churn.
  • Semantic coherence: read the entity lists of a sample of communities, or have an LLM rate whether a short title fits them. For Kestrel, check that the canonical themes (reef science, supply chain, funding, regulation) each dominate at least one community.

Exercise 11.4 Core

Write split_disconnected(G, partition) that replaces every community whose induced subgraph is disconnected with its connected components, and badly_connected(G, partition, gamma=1.0) that flags communities containing a node whose edge weight into the rest of its community is less than γ · ki · (dc − ki) / 2m (it would be better off alone by the modularity gain formula). Test split_disconnected on the Kestrel graph with the artificial partition {Okafor, Vehl, Brightwater, Park} plus all other nodes in one community. Does splitting always increase modularity?

Show answer 
import networkx as nx
from minigraphrag.communities import modularity

def split_disconnected(G, partition):
    out = []
    for c in partition:
        out.extend(set(cc) for cc in nx.connected_components(G.subgraph(c)))
    return out

def badly_connected(G, partition, gamma=1.0, weight="weight"):
    two_m = 2 * G.size(weight=weight)
    flagged = []
    for c in partition:
        d_c = sum(d for _, d in G.degree(c, weight=weight))
        for i in c:
            k_i = G.degree(i, weight=weight)
            k_in = sum(G.edges[i, j].get(weight, 1.0) for j in G.neighbors(i) if j in c)
            if len(c) > 1 and k_in < gamma * k_i * (d_c - k_i) / two_m:
                flagged.append((sorted(c)[:3], i))
    return flagged

bad = [{"okafor", "vehl", "brightwater", "park"}, set(G) - {"okafor", "vehl", "brightwater", "park"}]
print(round(modularity(G, bad), 3))                  # before
fixed = split_disconnected(G, bad)
print([sorted(c) for c in fixed][:3], round(modularity(G, fixed), 3))
print(badly_connected(G, bad))

The first community splits into {okafor}, {vehl} and {brightwater, park}; the second is connected and stays. Modularity rises from 0.003 to 0.022. It has to rise: modularity decomposes over communities, and when a community has no edges between two parts X and Y, splitting it leaves L unchanged while the penalty drops from (dX + dY)² to dX² + dY², a decrease of 2dXdY / (2m)² > 0. So splitting a disconnected community always increases Q (for γ > 0), which is why a disconnected community can never be modularity-optimal and why Leiden's guarantee costs nothing in quality. badly_connected flags Okafor and Vehl in the first community (zero internal edge weight) and, in the 13-node second community, Kestrel Labs, Project Tern and Tidewater Institute: most of their weight goes to the four nodes that were split off, so their ties inside a community with such a large total degree fall below what chance predicts. Being flagged does not mean a node is disconnected, only that modularity would prefer it elsewhere.

from itertools import combinations
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score
from minigraphrag.communities import louvain_communities, modularity

nodes = sorted(G)

for seed in range(20):
    parts = louvain_communities(G, seed=seed)
    label = {n: i for i, p in enumerate(parts) for n in p}
    runs.append((seed, parts, [label[n] for n in nodes]))

ari = {(a[0], b[0]): adjusted_rand_score(a[2], b[2]) for a, b in combinations(runs, 2)}
def mean_ari(seed):
    vals = [v for (x, y), v in ari.items() if seed in (x, y)]
    return sum(vals) / len(vals)

print("mean pairwise ARI:", round(sum(ari.values()) / len(ari), 3))
consensus = max(runs, key=lambda r: mean_ari(r[0]))
best_q = max(runs, key=lambda r: modularity(G, r[1]))
print("consensus seed", consensus[0], len(consensus[1]), round(modularity(G, consensus[1]), 4))
print("best-Q seed", best_q[0], len(best_q[1]), round(modularity(G, best_q[1]), 4))

What you should find: 14 of the 20 seeds land on the four-community partition with Q = 0.3384, and the other six on three-community partitions with Q of 0.3345, 0.3237 or 0.3203 (two seeds each). The mean pairwise ARI is about 0.81. Because the Q = 0.3384 partition is also the most common, it has the highest mean ARI (seed 0 is the first such run), so here consensus and best modularity agree. Why does GraphRAG partition the entity graph into communities at all?

  2. The number of nodes in the community

It is the null model: under random rewiring that preserves degrees, the expected share of edge ends inside c is (dc/2m)². Modularity rewards communities that beat it.

After aggregation, a super-node moving is a whole community moving. The Kestrel pairs {Nair, Marlow} and {Tern, Deepcast} only merge into one supply-chain community at the second level. Aggregation does not guarantee connectivity; that is Leiden's refinement.

Which statement about Leiden's refinement phase is correct?

  3. It removes the need for a random seed
  4. It merges small communities to avoid the resolution limit
Refinement merges nodes only along edges within each community, and the aggregated graph has one super-node per refined sub-community. Leiden is still randomized, and with modularity it still has the resolution limit (CPM avoids it).

Questions learners often ask

Should community detection use edge weights?

Usually yes. Summed relationship strengths (Chapter 9) encode how often and how strongly a relation was stated, so weighted clustering keeps strongly related entities together. Watch for one hub whose edges are heavy simply because it appears in every document, like Kestrel Labs: it can pull everything into its community. Capping per-chunk contributions or log-scaling weights helps.

Can an entity belong to more than one community?

Not in Louvain or Leiden: they produce partitions. Priya Nair is plausibly part of both the supply-chain and the reef-monitoring story, but she lands in one. Overlapping methods exist (for example clique percolation, or soft clustering as in RAPTOR), and a practical middle ground is to include a community's strongest boundary relationships in its report context, so the report can mention neighbors that live elsewhere.

Why not cluster entity embeddings instead of the graph?

Embedding clusters group entities that are described similarly: all the venture firms together, all the reefs together. Graph communities group entities that interact: Brightwater with the company it funds. For “what is going on” summaries, interaction is what you want, since a report about all venture firms in the corpus is a list, not a story. Embedding clustering is a reasonable choice for chunks, which is what RAPTOR does (Chapter 12).

How big should max_cluster_size be?

Work backwards from the report prompt. A leaf community's entities, relationships and claims should fit the report's input budget (GraphRAG's default is 8,000 input tokens) with room to spare, and there should be enough entities for a report to say something. GraphRAG's default of 10 is a sensible start; raise it if leaf reports feel thin, lower it if reports are generic or their context is truncated.

My communities change every time I add a few documents. Is that normal?

Yes. Modularity has many near-optimal partitions, and a small change to the graph can tip the optimizer from one to another, renumbering everything. Fix the seed, sort inputs, and match new communities to old ones by member overlap so unchanged reports can be reused. Chapter 27 covers incremental strategies that avoid re-clustering the whole graph.

Key takeaways

  • Communities are GraphRAG's summarization units: coherent, prompt-sized, and arranged in a hierarchy of zoom levels for global questions.
  • Modularity compares edge weight inside communities with what degree-preserving chance predicts; Q = Σ [Lc/m − γ(dc/2m)²].
  • Louvain greedily moves nodes by the local gain ΔQ = kin/m − γ Σtot ki/2m², then aggregates communities into super-nodes and repeats.
  • Resolution γ sets granularity, and the resolution limit hides small communities in large graphs; recursive splitting counters it.
  • Louvain can leave communities badly connected or disconnected; Leiden's refinement phase guarantees connected communities at no cost in quality.
  • GraphRAG uses hierarchical Leiden with a max cluster size, largest connected component and a fixed seed; record and control all three.
