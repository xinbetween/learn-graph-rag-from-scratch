Why LLMs need retrieval

A language model answers from what it absorbed during training, and it will answer confidently even when that is not enough. This chapter explains what a model can and cannot know, what it costs to paste your documents into its context, and why “retrieve, then generate” became the default way to ground answers in your own data.

You will be able to

  • Explain next-token prediction and why it produces fluent answers whether or not the model knows the facts
  • Distinguish parametric knowledge (in the weights) from non-parametric knowledge (in documents you supply)
  • Estimate the token count, cost and fit of a prompt, and decide when “stuff the whole corpus” stops working
  • Describe the lost-in-the-middle effect and what it implies for how you order context
  • Call an LLM through the minigraphrag interface and build a stuffed-context baseline with citations
  • Argue the trade-off between long-context prompting and retrieval-augmented generation for a given workload

What a language model does

Every chat model you will use in this course is, at its core, a function that takes a sequence of tokens and returns a probability distribution over the next token. A token is a chunk of text from a fixed vocabulary: often a whole common word, sometimes a word piece, a space plus a word, or punctuation. Generation is a loop: pick a token from the distribution, append it, run the function again.

Formally, a model with parameters θ defines

P(xt | x1, …, xt−1; θ)

and the probability of a whole answer is the product of those per-step probabilities. Training adjusts θ so that real text from the training corpus gets high probability. Nothing in that objective says “only emit true statements”. It says “emit what text like this usually continues with”. Instruction tuning and preference training make the model more helpful and more willing to say “I don't know”, but they sit on top of the same next-token machinery.

The walk-through above is illustrative, but the shape is real: the tokens that carry facts are often the ones with the flattest distributions, and the output text does not show you that. The correct answer, from the corpus, is Priya Nair (since March 2026), with Jonas Vehl before her. No amount of sampling tricks can recover a fact the model never saw.

Parametric and non-parametric knowledge

Researchers call what a model absorbed during training its parametric knowledge: facts stored implicitly in the weights. Petroni et al. showed in 2019 that pretrained language models can answer many fill-in-the-blank factual queries without any retrieval, which is why chat models seem to “know” things. The same line of work also showed the limits: recall is uneven, rare facts are unreliable, and you cannot tell which facts the model has from the outside.

Non-parametric knowledge is knowledge you supply at inference time: documents, database rows, graph facts, pasted into the prompt. It can be updated by editing a file, it can be private, and it can be cited.

Three things the weights cannot give you

  • Anything after the knowledge cutoff. Training data stops at some date. The Kestrel leadership change happened in March 2026; a model trained before that would, at best, name Jonas Vehl.
  • Private data. Kestrel's confidential board memo about the sonar supply disruption was never on the public web. No model has it in its weights, however large.
  • Provenance. Even when the weights hold a fact, the model cannot point to the document it came from. In most business settings an answer without a source is not usable.
The Kestrel test

The running example is fictional so that parametric knowledge cannot help. When a model answers a Kestrel question correctly in this course, it is because your retrieval put the evidence in front of it. When it answers incorrectly, you can trace the failure to what was or was not retrieved.

Hallucination and grounding

A hallucination is generated content that is fluent but not supported by the source or by reality. The survey by Ji et al. distinguishes intrinsic hallucinations, which contradict the provided source, from extrinsic ones, which add claims the source cannot verify. Both matter for RAG:

  • Extrinsic: the context mentions that Kestrel lost its sonar supplier, and the model adds “because Deepcast went bankrupt”. Nothing in the context says that. The real reason, Marlow Dynamics' acquisition of Deepcast, is in a different document.
  • Intrinsic: the context says Brightwater Capital led a $12 million Series A, and the answer says $40 million, mixing it up with the Series B figure from another sentence.

Grounding means constraining the answer to evidence that is present in the prompt and making that link checkable. In practice it has three parts, and you will build all three:

  1. Put the relevant evidence in the context (retrieval; most of this course).
  2. Instruct the model to answer only from that evidence and to say when it does not know.
  3. Require citations to evidence ids, so a person or a program can check each claim.

Grounding reduces hallucination; it does not eliminate it. Models still misread context, merge two facts into one, or cite the wrong id. That is why evaluation (Chapter 25) checks faithfulness as well as correctness.

Missing evidence is the most common cause

When a RAG answer is wrong, the first question to ask is “was the evidence in the context?” If it was not, no prompt wording will fix it. Most of the failures in Chapter 3 are of this kind, and they are what graph-based retrieval is designed to address.

Context windows, tokens and cost

If the model needs evidence in its prompt, the obvious move is to paste in everything. Whether that works depends on three budgets: the context window (the maximum number of tokens a model can take as input plus output), money (providers bill per input and output token), and latency (the model must process every input token before producing the first output token).

Token math you can do in your head

For English prose with common tokenizers, a useful rule of thumb is that one token is about four characters, or about three quarters of a word. minigraphrag uses the same idea when tiktoken is not installed: it estimates 4 tokens for every 3 words.

ItemWordsApproximate tokens
A canonical question (“Who supplied Kestrel's sonar in 2024?”)68
One Kestrel document (the Series B press release)453≈ 600
The whole Kestrel corpus (12 documents)5,427≈ 7,200
A 50,000-document company wiki at 600 tokens per page≈ 22.5 million≈ 30 million

The Kestrel corpus fits comfortably in any modern context window. A company wiki does not fit in any of them: current long-context models accept inputs on the order of hundreds of thousands to about a million tokens, which is still 30 times too small for 30 million tokens.

What a prompt costs

Cost per question is roughly

cost = Tin × pin + Tout × pout

where T are token counts and p are per-token prices. Output tokens are usually priced several times higher than input tokens, but in RAG the input dominates because the context is long and the answer is short. Prices change constantly, so this course uses an illustrative input price of $3 per million tokens. Plug in your provider's current numbers.

Stuffing the Kestrel corpus: 7,236 corpus tokens plus about 150 tokens of instructions and question gives about 7,390 input tokens per question. At 1,000 questions a day that is 7.39 million input tokens, about $22 a day. Retrieving five chunks of about 400 tokens each instead gives about 2,150 input tokens per question and about $6.45 a day. For a 12-document corpus both numbers are small. The gap is what grows: the stuffed prompt scales with the corpus, the retrieved prompt scales with k.

Two more costs do not show up on the invoice. Latency: the model processes the whole prompt before the first answer token, so time-to-first-token grows with prompt length. In a standard transformer, self-attention compares every token with every other token, so that prefill compute grows faster than linearly with length. Prompt caching, which most providers now offer, cuts the price and latency of a repeated prompt prefix. It helps when the same long corpus prefix is reused across many questions, and it does nothing when every question needs different documents.

Budgeting the window

Even when you retrieve, the context window is a shared budget. Instructions, conversation history, retrieved evidence, the question and room for the answer all compete for it. Every GraphRAG method in this course ends with a budgeting step that decides how many tokens each kind of evidence gets. Local search, for example, splits its budget between text chunks, community reports and entity and relationship tables.

More context is not always better: lost in the middle

Suppose money were no object and your corpus fit in the window. Pasting everything in would still not reliably give the best answers. Liu et al. (“Lost in the Middle”, 2023) placed the one document that contained the answer at different positions among many distractor documents. Models did best when the relevant document was at the beginning or the end of the context, and noticeably worse when it sat in the middle, producing a U-shaped curve. Adding more distractor documents also tended to hurt.

Three practical consequences follow, and they recur throughout the course:

  • Rank, then order. Put the strongest evidence first (or first and last), not in retrieval-index order.
  • Precision matters. A retriever that returns the answer plus forty distractors can do worse than one that returns the answer plus four.
  • Structure helps. Tables with ids, short summaries and explicit relationships, which GraphRAG produces, are easier for a model to use than a wall of raw chunks.
Liu et al., Lost in the Middle (2023)

Using multi-document question answering and a synthetic key-value retrieval task, the authors found that performance was highest when relevant information appeared at the start or end of the input and degraded when it appeared in the middle, including for models built for long contexts. The effect motivates careful ordering and aggressive filtering of retrieved context.

Retrieve, then generate

The pattern that fixes the knowledge problem without paying for the whole corpus on every question is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). Lewis et al. introduced the name in 2020: a neural retriever (Dense Passage Retrieval over Wikipedia) selects passages for a question, and a sequence-to-sequence generator (BART) conditions on them to write the answer. They trained the retriever's query encoder and the generator together, and showed strong results on open-domain question answering and more specific, more factual generations than a comparable model without retrieval. The same year, REALM (Guu et al.) built retrieval into language model pre-training.

Modern RAG systems rarely train anything end to end. They keep the idea and swap the parts: an off-the-shelf embedding model, a vector index, and a general-purpose LLM steered by a prompt. The loop is retrieve, augment, generate, with an optional check at the end.

What goes in the “Retrieve” box is the subject of the rest of this course. Chapter 2 builds the classic version: chunks, embeddings, nearest-neighbor search. Chapter 3 shows the questions it cannot answer. Parts 2 and 3 replace the box with a knowledge graph, community summaries and graph traversal.

Calling an LLM through minigraphrag

Everything in minigraphrag talks to language models through one method. That is the whole interface:

from typing import Protocol

class LLM(Protocol):
    def complete(self, prompt: str, system: str | None = None, json: bool = False) -> str: ...

Any object with that method works: OpenAICompatibleLLM (OpenAI, Ollama, vLLM and other OpenAI-compatible servers), AnthropicLLM, MockLLM for offline runs, and CachedLLM, which wraps any of them with a disk cache. get_llm builds one from a short name and environment variables.

import os
from minigraphrag.llm import get_llm

# "mock" runs offline. Set OPENAI_API_KEY and use "openai", ANTHROPIC_API_KEY and
# "anthropic", or run Ollama locally and use "ollama" (OLLAMA_MODEL picks the model).
llm = get_llm(os.getenv("LLM", "mock"), cache_dir=".cache/llm")

closed_book = llm.complete(
    "Who is the CTO of Kestrel Labs?",
    system="Answer in one sentence. If you do not know, say so.",
)
print(closed_book)

With a real model, the closed-book answer should be some form of “I don't know”, or a made-up name. With MockLLM you will see OK: the mock recognizes each of the course's prompt templates by a marker sentence and returns a deterministic answer for it, and a free-form prompt matches none of them. That is intentional. The mock has no world knowledge to fall back on, so everything it says in later chapters comes from context you retrieved.

A “stuff the whole corpus” baseline

Before building retrieval, build the thing retrieval must beat. The baseline below puts every document into the same grounded prompt that later search methods use, as a table of sources with ids, and refuses to run when the prompt would not fit.

from minigraphrag.chunking import load_documents
from minigraphrag.context import format_table
from minigraphrag.llm import get_llm
from minigraphrag.search.common import generate_answer
from minigraphrag.tokens import count_tokens

class ContextTooLarge(Exception):
    pass

def stuffed_answer(llm, docs: dict[str, str], question: str,
                   max_context_tokens: int = 100_000, price_per_million: float = 3.0):
    rows = [[doc_id, text] for doc_id, text in docs.items()]
    context = format_table("Sources", ["id", "text"], rows)   # -----Sources----- / id|text / ...
    n = count_tokens(context)
    if n > max_context_tokens:
        raise ContextTooLarge(f"{n} tokens > budget {max_context_tokens}; you need retrieval")
    answer = generate_answer(llm, question, context)          # context goes in the system prompt
    cost = n / 1_000_000 * price_per_million                  # input side only, illustrative price
    return answer, n, cost

docs = load_documents("data/corpus")
llm = get_llm("mock")
answer, n, cost = stuffed_answer(llm, docs, "Who supplied Kestrel's sonar in 2024?")
print(f"{n} context tokens, about ${cost:.4f} per question")
print(answer)

generate_answer formats LOCAL_SEARCH_SYSTEM_PROMPT from minigraphrag/prompts.py. It tells the model to answer from the data tables, say when it does not know, and cite records as [Data: Sources (id)]. The mock answers by picking sentences that overlap the question and citing their rows, so the output is extractive but has the same citation format a real model will use.

Keep the baseline around

For a corpus this small, a strong model with the whole corpus in context is a hard baseline to beat on many questions. That is useful information, not an embarrassment. Every later chapter compares against it, and Capstone 1 asks you to show where GraphRAG wins and at what cost.

Long context or retrieval?

Context windows grew dramatically between 2023 and 2026, and every increase prompted the question “is RAG dead?” The answer so far has been that the two are complementary. Xu et al. (2023) compared the approaches directly and found that a model with a 4K window plus simple retrieval could perform comparably to a version fine-tuned for a 16K window, and that retrieval also improved the long-context model. The trade-offs are practical:

ConcernStuff everything into a long contextRetrieve, then generate
Corpus sizeMust fit in the window; hard ceilingUnbounded; the index grows, the prompt does not
Cost per questionGrows with the corpus (prompt caching helps for a fixed corpus)Grows with k and chunk size
LatencyLong prefill on every uncached callRetrieval step plus a short prompt
RecallEverything is present, though the model may not use it (lost in the middle)Only what the retriever finds; misses are silent
Cross-document reasoningPossible in principle, since all documents are visibleOnly across what was retrieved together
Freshness and access controlRebuild the prompt; hard to filter per userUpdate the index; filter results per user
CitationsPossible, with ids on every documentNatural: every retrieved piece has an id
Engineering effortMinimalChunking, embeddings, index, tuning, evaluation

A reasonable rule in 2026: if your corpus fits well within the window, changes rarely, serves few questions and does not need per-user filtering, try long context first and measure. Otherwise retrieve. Most real systems do both: retrieve to narrow down, then use a generous context for the survivors.

Where graphs come in

Retrieval's weakness sits in the row labeled “cross-document reasoning”. A retriever that scores chunks one at a time cannot see that the chunk about Deepcast's acquisition explains the chunk about Project Tern. GraphRAG keeps the retrieve-then-generate loop and changes what is indexed and retrieved: entities, relationships, paths and community summaries instead of isolated chunks.

Exercise 1.1 Warm-up

By hand, using 4 tokens per 3 words and an input price of $3 per million tokens: (a) estimate the input tokens and daily input cost of answering 1,000 questions a day by stuffing the 5,427-word Kestrel corpus plus 150 tokens of instructions and question; (b) do the same for retrieving five chunks of 300 words each plus the same 150 tokens; (c) repeat (a) for a 50,000-document wiki with 600 tokens per document, and say whether it fits in a 1,000,000-token window.

Show answer

(a) 5,427 × 4/3 = 7,236 tokens. Plus 150 gives 7,386 tokens per question. Per day: 7,386 × 1,000 = 7.386 million tokens, times $3 = $22.16 a day.

(b) Five chunks of 300 words = 1,500 words = 2,000 tokens. Plus 150 gives 2,150 tokens per question, 2.15 million a day, $6.45 a day. That is about 3.4 times cheaper.

(c) 50,000 × 600 = 30,000,000 tokens per question. It does not fit in a 1,000,000-token window: it is 30 times too large. Even if it did, one question would cost 30 × $3 = $90 in input alone, and 1,000 a day would cost $90,000. Retrieval (b) stays at $6.45 a day regardless of corpus size, as long as k and chunk size stay fixed.

Exercise 1.2 Core

Answers that cite sources are only checkable if the citations are real. Write check_citations(answer: str, context: str) -> dict that extracts every id cited as [Data: Sources (a, b)] in the answer, compares them to the ids present in the Sources table of the context, and returns the cited ids, the ids that do not exist in the context, and whether the answer cites anything at all. Test it on the output of the stuffed baseline.

Hint

minigraphrag.context.parse_tables turns the context back into {"Sources": [{"id": ..., "text": ...}]}. A single [Data: ...] block can cite several datasets separated by semicolons.

Show answer 
import re
from minigraphrag.context import parse_tables

CITE = re.compile(r"\[Data:([^\]]*)\]")
DATASET = re.compile(r"(\w+)\s*\(([^)]*)\)")

def check_citations(answer: str, context: str) -> dict:
    known = {row["id"] for row in parse_tables(context).get("Sources", [])}
    cited = []
    for block in CITE.findall(answer):
        for dataset, ids in DATASET.findall(block):
            if dataset != "Sources":
                continue
            cited += [i.strip() for i in ids.split(",") if i.strip() and i.strip() != "+more"]
    return {
        "cited": sorted(set(cited)),
        "unknown": sorted(set(cited) - known),
        "has_citations": bool(cited),
    }

# Tests
ctx = "-----Sources-----\nid|text\n03_deepcast_supply_agreement|Deepcast signs...\n08_kestrel_board_memo_supply|Memo..."
ok = "Deepcast supplied Kestrel. [Data: Sources (03_deepcast_supply_agreement, 08_kestrel_board_memo_supply)]"
bad = "Deepcast supplied Kestrel. [Data: Sources (99_made_up)]"
assert check_citations(ok, ctx)["unknown"] == []
assert check_citations(bad, ctx)["unknown"] == ["99_made_up"]
assert check_citations("No citations here.", ctx)["has_citations"] is False

Microsoft GraphRAG's prompts let the model write +more when a claim has more sources than it lists; minigraphrag's prompt does not ask for it, but the parser skips it so the checker works on either output. A check like this catches invented ids cheaply. It cannot tell whether a real cited source actually supports the claim; that needs an entailment check or an LLM judge (Chapter 25).

import os
from minigraphrag.chunking import load_documents
from minigraphrag.context import format_table
from minigraphrag.llm import get_llm

docs = load_documents("data/corpus")
target_id = "12_interview_leadership_change_2026"

question = "Who is the CTO of Kestrel Labs as of April 2026? Answer with a name."

def run(distractors, trials=5):
    results = {}
    for pos in range(len(distractors) + 1):
        ordered = distractors[:pos] + [(target_id, docs[target_id])] + distractors[pos:]
        context = format_table("Sources", ["id", "text"], [list(p) for p in ordered])
        hits = sum("priya" in generate_answer(llm, question, context).lower() for _ in range(trials))
        results[pos] = hits / trials

print(" 3 distractors:", run(others[:3]))

What to expect: with only about 7,000 tokens of context, many 2026 models will answer correctly at every position, so you may see no dip at all. A more telling failure here is the stale answer “Jonas Vehl”, because documents 01, 03 and 08 call him CTO. Exercise 1.5 Core

A team runs an internal assistant over 4,000 policy documents averaging 1,500 tokens each. Documents change weekly, answers must respect each employee's access rights, and about 20,000 questions arrive per day. A colleague proposes a 1-million-token-context model with the whole corpus in the prompt. Write a short recommendation with numbers.

Show answer

Corpus size: 4,000 × 1,500 = 6,000,000 tokens. It does not fit in a 1M-token window: it is six times too large, so the proposal fails before cost is even considered. Suppose you split it into six 1M-token shards and queried each: that is 6M input tokens per question, 120 billion tokens a day at 20,000 questions, $360,000 a day at $3 per million before any caching discount. Access control is the second blocker: one shared prompt cannot hide documents from users who are not allowed to see them, so you would need a different prompt per permission group, which breaks prompt caching.

Recommendation: retrieval with per-user filtering at query time. With, say, eight 500-token chunks plus 500 tokens of overhead, that is 4,500 tokens per question, 90 million tokens a day, about $270 a day at the same price. Weekly updates become index upserts. Keep a long-context model for the final generation step so you can afford generous k for hard questions.

Why does next-token prediction produce confident-sounding hallucinations?

  1. The objective rewards plausible continuations; a flat distribution at a fact-bearing token still yields a fluent sentence once a token is sampled
  2. Models are trained to avoid saying “I don't know”
  3. Sampling temperature is always too high
  4. Hallucinations only happen when the context is too long
Uncertainty lives in individual token distributions, which the final text hides. Temperature 0 makes the choice deterministic, not correct.

Questions learners often ask

If context windows keep growing, will RAG become unnecessary?

For small, static corpora it already is sometimes unnecessary. For large, changing or access-controlled data it is not, for three reasons that bigger windows do not remove: cost and latency grow with prompt length, models do not use every part of a long context equally well, and access control and freshness are easier to enforce in an index than in a giant prompt. What is changing is the balance: retrieval can afford to be less precise because the generator can read more.

Why not fine-tune the model on our documents instead?

Fine-tuning changes parametric knowledge, which brings back the problems retrieval avoids: you cannot cite where an answer came from, updating a fact means training again, deleting a document's influence is hard, and small fine-tunes are an unreliable way to inject new facts. Fine-tuning is better for style, format and task behavior, such as teaching a small model to follow your extraction format. Chapter 24 covers training with graphs.

Does temperature 0 stop hallucinations?

No. Temperature 0 makes the model pick the most likely token at each step, which makes outputs more reproducible. If the most likely name is wrong because the evidence is missing, you get the same wrong name every time. Use low temperature for reproducibility and caching, and grounding for correctness. The reference code's AnthropicLLM sends no temperature at all, because the Claude models it was written against reject sampling parameters; providers change these rules, so check the current API documentation rather than assuming the knob exists.

Why does the course put the context in the system prompt and the question in the user message?

It mirrors Microsoft GraphRAG's search prompts, which minigraphrag follows: the system prompt carries the role, rules and data tables, and the user turn carries only the question. That keeps the question easy to find for logging and caching. Putting everything in one user message also works. What matters is that instructions, evidence with ids, and the question are clearly separated.

Why does the mock LLM answer “OK” to my own prompt?

MockLLM is not a language model. It recognizes the course's prompt templates by a marker sentence in each (listed in prompts.MARKERS) and runs a deterministic heuristic for that task, such as extracting entities with a gazetteer or picking overlapping sentences to answer from data tables. A free-form prompt matches no marker, so it returns OK. Use it to run pipelines offline and in tests; use a real model to judge answer quality.

Project: a closed-book versus stuffed-context baseline report

Measure the starting point every later chapter will be compared against, with a real model if you have access to one and the mock otherwise.

  • A script that answers all questions in data/questions.jsonl twice: closed-book, and with the whole corpus stuffed into the grounded prompt
  • For each run, the input tokens per question, total LLM calls and an estimated cost at a price you state
  • The citation checker from Exercise 1.2 applied to every stuffed answer, with the count of answers that have no citations or invalid ids
  • A hand-graded accuracy column (correct, partial, wrong) against the reference answers, broken down by question type
  • A short paragraph naming the question types where the stuffed baseline struggles most, and your guess at why

Done when you have a table of accuracy and cost by question type that you would be willing to show a colleague as “the number to beat”.

Key takeaways

  • A language model predicts plausible next tokens. Fluency does not signal that a fact is known.
  • Parametric knowledge is frozen, public and uncitable. Private, recent and checkable facts must be supplied in the prompt.
  • Grounding means evidence in context, instructions to use only that evidence, and citations you can verify.
  • Stuffing the corpus works for small, static data; cost, latency and window limits grow with the corpus, while retrieval grows with k.
  • More context is not automatically better: models use evidence in the middle of long contexts less reliably, so rank, filter and order.
  • Retrieve-then-generate is the backbone of the course; GraphRAG changes what is retrieved, not the loop.
