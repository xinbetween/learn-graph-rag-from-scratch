Claims, covariates and time

Entity descriptions tell you what something is; they are bad at saying what happened, when, and whether it is even true. This chapter adds claims with status and dates, normalizes “last month” into real calendar ranges, and puts validity intervals on edges so the graph can answer “who was CTO in 2024?”.

You will be able to

  • Explain what a GraphRAG claim (covariate) is and which facts it preserves that summarized descriptions lose
  • Write and parse a claim extraction prompt with subject, object, type, status, start and end dates, description and source text
  • Normalize absolute and relative time expressions into date ranges anchored on the document date
  • Attach valid_from and valid_to to relationships and filter the graph to any point in time
  • Tell a genuine contradiction from a change over time, and model point events differently from interval facts

What descriptions blur

By the end of Chapter 9 each entity has one summarized description. Here is the kind of profile a summarizer writes for Jonas Vehl from four chunks spanning 2021 to 2026:

Jonas Vehl is a co-founder of Kestrel Labs who has served as its CTO and Chief Scientist. He designed the acoustic array used in Sentinel drones and co-leads Project Tern with Priya Nair.

Every sentence is supported, and the paragraph is still misleading. It does not say that Vehl stopped being CTO in March 2026, or that Priya Nair replaced him. Ask “Who is the CTO of Kestrel Labs?” (canonical question 6) and a model reading this profile, plus a similar one for Nair that says she “became CTO”, can easily answer “Jonas Vehl and Priya Nair”. The summarizer did its job: it merged descriptions. Merging is exactly what destroys sequence.

The graph edges don't help either. After Chapter 9, {JONAS VEHL, KESTREL LABS} is one undirected edge with a summed weight and a merged description. It has no dates. Three kinds of information need a better home:

  • Events: things that happened at a time. Marlow Dynamics acquired Deepcast in April 2025. Sentinel drones detected bleaching on 11 August 2024.
  • Dated states: facts that were true over an interval. Deepcast supplied Kestrel's sonar from 2022 until mid-2025. Vehl was CTO from 2021 to March 2026.
  • Assertions with uncertain truth: allegations, rumors, estimates and denials. People familiar with the deal put the price at $180 to $220 million. A councillor asked whether Kestrel's contract created a conflict of interest, and the City Attorney said it did not.

Microsoft GraphRAG calls these records claims and stores them in a table of covariates: extra data attached to entities that is not itself a node or an edge. This chapter builds that table, then goes one step further and puts time directly on relationships.

Anatomy of a claim

A claim is a statement about an entity, extracted from one text unit, with enough metadata to judge it later. GraphRAG's claim extractor asks for eight fields, and minigraphrag mirrors them in a dataclass.

The three statuses

Status is what makes claims more than dated relationships. GraphRAG's prompt defines them precisely: TRUE means the claim is confirmed, FALSE means it was found to be false, and SUSPECTED means it is not verified. The Kestrel corpus has natural examples of each.

  • TRUE: “Kestrel Labs today announced a $40 million Series B financing led by Brightwater Capital” (press release, 23 September 2025).
  • SUSPECTED: “Terms were not disclosed, but two people familiar with the transaction put the price at between $180 million and $220 million” (trade journal, 17 April 2025). Also: “The acquisition is expected to close in May, subject to regulatory approval.” A forecast is not yet a fact.
  • FALSE: Councillor Brandt asked whether the contract created a conflict of interest because Kestrel's CEO formerly worked at Tidewater Institute. “The City Attorney advised that no conflict exists” (council minutes, 18 November 2025). The allegation was raised and refuted in the same chunk.
Status is relative to the chunk, not to the world

The extractor only sees one text unit. In the council minutes, Councillor Adeyemi refers to “press reports of a sonar supply problem”: from that chunk alone, a supply disruption is at best SUSPECTED. The CEO's board memo from May 2025 states it as fact. The same real-world claim can therefore arrive as SUSPECTED from one chunk and TRUE from another. Keep both records; decide how to reconcile them at query time or in the community report, where all claims about an entity sit side by side.

Extracting claims

Claim extraction is a second LLM pass over each text unit, with its own prompt. It is separate from entity extraction for two reasons: the instructions are long enough that combining them hurts both tasks, and many deployments don't need claims at all. In Microsoft GraphRAG, claim extraction is off by default (extract_claims.enabled: false), and the default claim description is deliberately broad: “Any claims or facts that could be relevant to information discovery.” Narrow it for your domain; for a risk-analysis corpus it might be “financial transactions, supply agreements, leadership changes, legal and regulatory findings”.

The prompt

Here is the claim prompt in minigraphrag/prompts.py. It is a condensed version of GraphRAG's, which has the same steps and field list plus two worked examples.

CLAIM_EXTRACTION_PROMPT = """-Target activity-
You are an intelligent assistant that helps a human analyst to analyze claims against certain entities presented in a text document.

-Goal-
Given a text document, an entity specification, and a claim description, extract all entities that match the entity specification and all claims against those entities.

-Steps-
1. Extract all named entities that match the entity specification: {entity_specs}
2. For each entity, extract all claims matching the claim description: {claim_description}
For each claim extract: subject, object (or NONE), claim type, claim status (TRUE, FALSE or SUSPECTED), claim start and end dates in ISO-8601 (or NONE), a description with evidence, and the source text quote.
Format each claim as (<subject>{tuple_delimiter}<object>{tuple_delimiter}<claim_type>{tuple_delimiter}<claim_status>{tuple_delimiter}<start_date>{tuple_delimiter}<end_date>{tuple_delimiter}<description>{tuple_delimiter}<source_text>)
Use **{record_delimiter}** as the list delimiter. When finished, output {completion_delimiter}

-Real Data-
Text:
{input_text}
######################
Output:
"""

A few details in GraphRAG's full prompt are worth copying when you tune this for your own domain:

  • The subject is the actor. “The subject entity is one that committed the action described in the claim.” This keeps claims about an acquisition on the acquirer, not scattered across both parties.
  • Types must be reusable. “Name it in a way that can be repeated across multiple text inputs, so that similar claims share the same claim type.” Otherwise you get ACQUISITION, ACQUIRED, BUYOUT and PURCHASE for one concept.
  • Single dates repeat. “If the claim was made on a single date rather than a date range, set the same date for both start_date and end_date.” The prompt's own example dates a claim about “corruption activities in 2015” to the whole year, 2015-01-01 to 2015-12-30. (Yes, the example stops a day short of the year's end. Don't copy that part.)
  • Source text is plural. The model should list all relevant quotes, which matters when the evidence is split across sentences.

What the prompt does not have is the document date. Add it. Put a line such as Document date: 2025-04-17 right before Text:, and say in the instructions that relative expressions (“on Tuesday”, “last month”, “earlier this year”) must be resolved against that date. Of everything in this chapter, that one line does the most for temporal accuracy.

Look at the judgment calls. The requirement claim has an open end date, because a law that starts applying has no stated end. The supply claim's subject is DEEPCAST, which is not named in the chunk at all: a model that knows the rest of the corpus might write that, but from this chunk alone a stricter extractor should use KESTREL LABS as subject and NONE as object. That is precisely the kind of world-knowledge leak you should test for with the source_text field.

Parsing

The parser reuses Chapter 8's record splitter and is equally forgiving. Three normalizations matter for claims: uppercase the subject, object, type and status so they group; map NONE or empty to None; and refuse records with fewer than eight fields rather than guessing which field is missing.

def parse_claims_output(text: str, source_id: str = "") -> list[Claim]:
    """Parse claim tuples: (subject<|>object<|>type<|>status<|>start<|>end<|>description<|>source)."""
    claims = []
    for record in _split_records(text, P.RECORD_DELIMITER, P.COMPLETION_DELIMITER):
        fields = [_clean_field(f) for f in record.strip().strip("()").split(P.TUPLE_DELIMITER)]
        if len(fields) < 8:
            continue
        none = lambda v: None if v.upper() in ("NONE", "") else v  # noqa: E731
        claims.append(Claim(fields[0].upper(), fields[1].upper(), fields[2].upper(), fields[3].upper(),
                            none(fields[4]), none(fields[5]), fields[6], fields[7], source_id))
    return claims


def extract_claims(
    llm: LLM,
    unit: TextUnit,
    entity_specs: list[str] | None = None,
    claim_description: str = "Any claims or facts about the entity, especially dated events, roles and transactions",
) -> list[Claim]:
    """Extract dated, attributable claims ("covariates" in GraphRAG) from one chunk."""
    prompt = P.CLAIM_EXTRACTION_PROMPT.format(
        entity_specs=", ".join(entity_specs or P.DEFAULT_ENTITY_TYPES), claim_description=claim_description,
        input_text=unit.text, tuple_delimiter=P.TUPLE_DELIMITER, record_delimiter=P.RECORD_DELIMITER,
        completion_delimiter=P.COMPLETION_DELIMITER,
    )
    return parse_claims_output(llm.complete(prompt), source_id=unit.id)

After parsing, validate more than the shape. Status should be one of the three allowed values; anything else (“CONFIRMED”, “LIKELY”) maps to the closest one or to SUSPECTED. Dates should parse as ISO-8601, and start must not be after end. And source_text should actually occur in the chunk: a fuzzy substring check (for example a partial ratio above 90 against the chunk text) catches invented quotes, which almost always accompany invented claims.

from datetime import date
from rapidfuzz import fuzz

STATUSES = {"TRUE", "FALSE", "SUSPECTED"}
STATUS_ALIASES = {"CONFIRMED": "TRUE", "VERIFIED": "TRUE", "REFUTED": "FALSE", "DENIED": "FALSE",
                  "ALLEGED": "SUSPECTED", "LIKELY": "SUSPECTED", "UNVERIFIED": "SUSPECTED"}

def _iso(value):
    if value is None:
        return None
    try:
        return date.fromisoformat(value[:10])     # accepts 2025-04-15 and 2025-04-15T00:00:00
    except ValueError:
        return "invalid"

def validate_claim(claim, unit_text: str) -> list[str]:
    """Return a list of problems; an empty list means the claim passed."""
    problems = []
    claim.status = STATUS_ALIASES.get(claim.status, claim.status)
    if claim.status not in STATUSES:
        problems.append(f"unknown status {claim.status}")
    start, end = _iso(claim.start_date), _iso(claim.end_date)
    if "invalid" in (start, end):
        problems.append("unparseable date")
    elif start and end and start > end:
        problems.append("start after end")
    if fuzz.partial_ratio(claim.source_text.lower(), unit_text.lower()) < 90:
        problems.append("source_text not found in chunk")
    return problems
What claims cost

Claim extraction is another full pass over every chunk with a long prompt, plus gleaning if you enable it (GraphRAG's default for claims is one gleaning round). As a planning number, turning claims on roughly doubles the LLM calls of the extraction phase. Pay for it when your questions are about events, risks, allegations or change over time. For a static encyclopedia-style corpus, skip it.

Normalizing time

An LLM asked for ISO dates will produce ISO-looking strings, and a surprising fraction of them will be wrong in a systematic way: relative expressions resolved against the model's idea of “now” rather than the document's date, a month guessed as January when only a year was given, or a day invented where the text only gave a season. Dates deserve their own normalization step, with the document date as the anchor.

Temporal expressions, anchored

Linguists call a phrase like “last month” a temporal expression. Some are absolute (“11 August 2024”), some are relative to the time of writing (“last year”), and some are relative to another event (“within weeks” of the acquisition). The TimeML annotation standard marks such expressions with a TIMEX3 tag whose value attribute holds the normalized date, and rule-based tools such as HeidelTime resolve them using the document creation time as the reference. You don't need the full standard, but you need its central idea: every relative expression is resolved against a reference time, and for news and memos that reference is the document date.

Two rows deserve comment. “This summer” assumes the northern hemisphere; Port Avalon is fictional, so a real system would need a locale. And “bought Deepcast last year” resolves to all of 2025, a range that contains the more precise April 2025 from the trade journal. They are consistent, not conflicting: a coarser date is a wider interval, and the more precise claim should win when you merge.

import calendar
import re
from datetime import date, timedelta

MONTHS = {m.lower(): i for i, m in enumerate(calendar.month_name) if m}
WEEKDAYS = {d.lower(): i for i, d in enumerate(calendar.day_name)}
SEASONS = {"spring": (3, 5), "summer": (6, 8), "autumn": (9, 11), "fall": (9, 11)}  # northern hemisphere

def month_end(y: int, m: int) -> date:
    return date(y, m, calendar.monthrange(y, m)[1])

def quarter(y: int, q: int) -> tuple[date, date]:
    return date(y, 3 * q - 2, 1), month_end(y, 3 * q)

def normalize_time_expression(expr: str, doc_date: date) -> tuple[date, date, str] | None:
    """Map a time expression to an inclusive (start, end, granularity) anchored on the document date."""
    e = expr.lower().strip()
    parts, y = e.split(), doc_date.year
    if re.fullmatch(r"\d{4}-\d{2}-\d{2}", e):                           # 2024-08-11
        d = date.fromisoformat(e)
        return d, d, "day"
    if len(parts) == 3 and parts[1] in MONTHS and parts[0].isdigit():    # 11 august 2024
        d = date(int(parts[2]), MONTHS[parts[1]], int(parts[0]))
        return d, d, "day"
    if len(parts) == 2 and parts[0] in MONTHS and parts[1].isdigit():    # march 2026
        yy, mm = int(parts[1]), MONTHS[parts[0]]
        return date(yy, mm, 1), month_end(yy, mm), "month"
    if m := re.fullmatch(r"q([1-4]) (\d{4})", e):                         # q1 2026
        return (*quarter(int(m[2]), int(m[1])), "quarter")
    if re.fullmatch(r"\d{4}", e):                                         # 2021
        return date(int(e), 1, 1), date(int(e), 12, 31), "year"
    if e in ("this month", "earlier this month"):
        return date(y, doc_date.month, 1), doc_date, "month"
    if e == "last month":
        prev = doc_date.replace(day=1) - timedelta(days=1)
        return prev.replace(day=1), prev, "month"
    if e in ("this year", "earlier this year"):
        return date(y, 1, 1), doc_date, "year"
    if e == "later this year":
        return doc_date, date(y, 12, 31), "year"
    if e == "last year":
        return date(y - 1, 1, 1), date(y - 1, 12, 31), "year"
    if e == "next year":
        return date(y + 1, 1, 1), date(y + 1, 12, 31), "year"
    if e == "last quarter":
        q = (doc_date.month - 1) // 3 + 1
        return (*(quarter(y, q - 1) if q > 1 else quarter(y - 1, 4)), "quarter")
    if m := re.fullmatch(r"(?:the )?end of the (first|second|third|fourth) quarter", e):
        end = quarter(y, ["first", "second", "third", "fourth"].index(m[1]) + 1)[1]
        return end, end, "day"
    if m := re.fullmatch(r"(this|last) (spring|summer|autumn|fall)", e):
        a, b = SEASONS[m[2]]
        yy = y if (m[1] == "this" or doc_date.month > b) else y - 1
        end = month_end(yy, b)
        return date(yy, a, 1), (min(end, doc_date) if yy == y else end), "season"
    if parts and parts[-1] in WEEKDAYS and len(parts) <= 2:              # "on tuesday" in past-tense news
        back = (doc_date.weekday() - WEEKDAYS[parts[-1]]) % 7 or 7
        d = doc_date - timedelta(days=back)
        return d, d, "day"
    return None                                                           # hand it to dateparser or the LLM

Rules like these cover most expressions in news, memos and reports. Return None for the rest and fall back to a library such as dateparser (which accepts a relative base date) or to the LLM with the document date in the prompt. Whatever resolves the expression, keep three things: the normalized range, the granularity, and the original phrase. The phrase is your audit trail when someone asks why the graph believes Nair joined Kestrel in June 2023.

Event-relative expressions

A sentence such as “Within weeks, Deepcast told outside customers it would stop taking new orders” is relative to an event (the acquisition), not to the document date. Resolve it in two steps: find the anchor event's date (2025-04-15) and apply a fuzzy offset (“within weeks” → up to about two months), giving the range 2025-04-15 to roughly 2025-06-10 with granularity fuzzy. If you cannot find the anchor, leave the dates as NONE and keep the phrase. A wrong precise date is worse than an honest missing one, because it will be used for filtering.

Planned, reported and actual time

Documents mix three different times, and a claim record should know which one it has:

  • Event time: when the thing happened. Bleaching was first detected on 11 August 2024.
  • Report time: when the document said it. The research note was issued on 3 September 2024.
  • Planned or expected time: when something is supposed to happen. “The acquisition is expected to close in May.” “By the end of 2026 we want every drone in the fleet running a Tern module.”

A planned date is not a fact. Store it as a SUSPECTED claim, or add an explicit modality field (PLANNED, EXPECTED), and never use it to set a relationship's validity interval until a later document confirms it. The board memo is a good test: it says Project Tern would start in June, and the engineering blog of 30 June 2025 confirms that it did.

Putting time on relationships

Claims live in a side table. For questions like “Who is the CTO?” you want time on the graph itself, so that traversal and retrieval can filter by date. The standard way is to give each relationship a validity interval: valid_from and valid_to, the period during which the fact was true in the world.

Intervals and points

Two kinds of temporal fact need different shapes:

  • Interval facts (states) hold over a period: CTO_OF, SUPPLIED, BOARD_MEMBER_OF, FORMERLY_WORKED_AT. Represent them as half-open intervals [valid_from, valid_to), with valid_to = None for “still true”. Half-open intervals make handovers clean: Vehl's CTO_OF interval ends on 2026-03-01 and Nair's starts on 2026-03-01, and on that day exactly one of them is valid.
  • Point facts (events) happen at a time: ACQUIRED, DETECTED, INVESTED_IN for a single round. Represent them as a single date, or as a short interval when the date is only known to a month or year. An event is not “valid” later; it has either happened by a given date or not.

An as-of filter in a few lines

With validity on edges, answering a temporal question is a filter followed by an ordinary lookup. minigraphrag/temporal.py uses a networkx.MultiDiGraph, so the same pair of nodes can carry several time-stamped edges (Vehl CTO_OF Kestrel and, later, Vehl CHIEF_SCIENTIST_OF Kestrel). Each edge also carries a second timeline, recorded_at and invalidated_at, which this chapter ignores and Chapter 27 puts to work.

from __future__ import annotations

from datetime import date

import networkx as nx

# Relations where a target can have only one source at a time (one CTO per company).
EXCLUSIVE_RELATIONS: set[str] = {"CTO_OF", "CEO_OF"}


def parse_date(value: str | date | None) -> date | None:
    """Accept date objects or ISO-ish strings: '2026', '2026-03', '2026-03-26'."""
    if value is None or isinstance(value, date):
        return value
    parts = [int(p) for p in str(value).split("-")]
    return date(parts[0], parts[1] if len(parts) > 1 else 1, parts[2] if len(parts) > 2 else 1)


def new_temporal_graph() -> nx.MultiDiGraph:
    """Directed multigraph: the same pair can hold many facts over time."""
    return nx.MultiDiGraph()


def add_fact(
    G: nx.MultiDiGraph,
    source: str,
    relation: str,
    target: str,
    valid_from: str | date,
    valid_to: str | date | None = None,
    recorded_at: str | date | None = None,
    description: str = "",
    exclusive: bool | None = None,
) -> int:
    """Add a fact edge, invalidating contradicting facts. Returns the new edge key."""
    vf, vt = parse_date(valid_from), parse_date(valid_to)
    rec = parse_date(recorded_at) or vf
    exclusive = relation in EXCLUSIVE_RELATIONS if exclusive is None else exclusive

    if exclusive and target in G:
        for old_source, _, key, d in list(G.in_edges(target, keys=True, data=True)):
            if d["relation"] != relation or old_source == source or d["invalidated_at"] is not None:
                continue
            if d["valid_to"] is None or d["valid_to"] > vf:
                # Close the old interval where the new one begins; remember what we believed before.
                d["prior_valid_to"] = d["valid_to"]
                d["valid_to"] = vf
                d["invalidated_at"] = rec

    return G.add_edge(source, target, relation=relation, description=description, valid_from=vf, valid_to=vt,
                      recorded_at=rec, invalidated_at=None, prior_valid_to=None)


def is_valid(d: dict, at: date, known_at: date | None = None) -> bool:
    """Was this fact true at `at`, according to what was recorded by `known_at` (default: now)?"""
    valid_to = d["valid_to"]
    if known_at is not None:
        if d["recorded_at"] > known_at:
            return False  # we hadn't learned this fact yet
        if d["invalidated_at"] is not None and d["invalidated_at"] > known_at:
            valid_to = d["prior_valid_to"]  # the invalidation hadn't happened yet
    return d["valid_from"] <= at and (valid_to is None or at < valid_to)


def as_of(G: nx.MultiDiGraph, at: str | date, known_at: str | date | None = None) -> nx.MultiDiGraph:
    """Snapshot of the facts valid at `at` (optionally as believed at `known_at`)."""
    at_d, known_d = parse_date(at), parse_date(known_at)
    snap = nx.MultiDiGraph()
    snap.add_nodes_from(G.nodes(data=True))
    for u, v, k, d in G.edges(keys=True, data=True):
        if is_valid(d, at_d, known_d):
            snap.add_edge(u, v, key=k, **d)
    return snap


def holders(G: nx.MultiDiGraph, relation: str, target: str, at: str | date, known_at: str | date | None = None) -> list[str]:
    """Sources holding `relation` to `target` at a date, e.g. holders(G, 'CTO_OF', 'Kestrel Labs', '2024-08')."""
    snap = as_of(G, at, known_at)
    if target not in snap:
        return []
    return sorted({u for u, _, d in snap.in_edges(target, data=True) if d["relation"] == relation})

One thing to watch: parse_date maps “2026-03” to 2026-03-01, so month granularity is silently turned into a day. The section after next deals with that.

The exclusive-relation rule is a small preview of Chapter 27: when a new fact arrives that cannot coexist with an old one, the old one is not deleted but closed, and invalidated_at records when that happened. That keeps history queryable. The rule is only safe for relations you have declared exclusive, and it trusts the newer fact: it closes the old interval even if the new fact starts earlier than a date the old source stated explicitly. It also assumes facts arrive in time order. Load Nair's 2026 fact first and Vehl's 2021 to 2026 fact second, and the rule sets Nair's valid_to to 2021-01-01, an interval that ends before it begins; holders then reports no CTO at all for September 2026. Backfilling history is common, so a production version should compare the two intervals before closing anything and refuse to close an interval at a date before it starts.

Granularity at the boundaries

“Nair became CTO in March 2026” gives a month, not a day. Store valid_from = 2026-03-01 with granularity month, and be honest at query time: on 2026-03-15, both “Vehl” and “Nair” are possible answers. A careful answer generator says “Priya Nair has been CTO since March 2026; Jonas Vehl held the role before that”, which is correct at any granularity. A system that stores 2026-03-01 as if it were exact will answer confidently and sometimes wrongly for dates inside the uncertain month.

Contradictions or changes over time?

Once facts carry time, you can finally tell apart two situations that look identical in a timeless graph.

A decision procedure

When two facts about the same subject and relation disagree, walk through these questions in order:

  1. Do their validity intervals overlap? If not, it is a change. “Kestrel's CTO Jonas Vehl” in a 2022 article and “Priya Nair, CTO” in a 2026 podcast are both right.
  2. Is the relation functional? Two board members at the same time is fine; two CTOs at the same time is not (unless the company says “co-CTO”). Only functional relations can contradict through overlap alone.
  3. Is one fact just less precise? A date inside another claim's range, or a rounded number (“about 140 employees” in 2022, “roughly 150” in 2025) is not a contradiction. The Deepcast headcount is in fact a change, since the two numbers are three years apart.
  4. Is one statement a plan and the other an outcome? “Expected to close in May” (SUSPECTED, planned) does not contradict a later “closed in May” or even “closed in June”: the plan was just revised.
  5. Only then is it a real contradiction. Keep both claims with their sources, prefer the more authoritative or more recent report for display, and surface the disagreement in the answer rather than hiding it.

This is where the summarization prompt's instruction from Chapter 9, “prefer the most recent dated information”, is a blunt instrument: it gets changes over time right and silently discards genuine contradictions. Structured validity intervals let you do better, because they make the difference computable.

Valid time is only half the story

So far every date has been valid time: when a fact was true in the world. There is a second timeline, transaction time: when your system learned or recorded the fact. If a 2026 article reveals that a deal actually closed in 2024, the valid time is 2024 and the transaction time is 2026. Graphs that track both are called bi-temporal, and they can answer “what did we believe on 1 January 2025 about who supplied Kestrel?”. Zep and its open-source Graphiti engine put both timelines on every edge. You will build one in Chapter 27 and use it for agent memory in Capstone 3.

Rasmussen et al., Zep: A Temporal Knowledge Graph Architecture for Agent Memory (2025)

Zep's Graphiti engine stores episodes (raw inputs) and extracted entity edges with a bi-temporal model: each fact has a valid interval in the world and a separate record of when the system created and expired it. When new information contradicts an existing edge, the old edge is invalidated by setting its end time rather than being deleted, so historical queries still work. The exclusive-relation rule in add_fact above, with its recorded_at and invalidated_at fields, is a minimal version of that idea.

Using claims and time downstream

Claims and temporal edges pay off in three places later in the course:

  • Community reports (Chapter 12). GraphRAG's report context can include a Claims table next to the Entities and Relationships tables, and the report prompt cites claim ids in its data references. Events and allegations are exactly what makes a report about “supply chain risk” specific rather than generic.
  • Local search (Chapter 14). Claims whose subject is a retrieved entity go into the context as their own table, with status and dates visible to the model.
  • Temporal retrieval (Chapter 21). A “temporal window” pattern restricts traversal to edges valid in the time range the question asks about, which is as_of generalized from a point to a range.

def overlaps(d, start, end):
    """Half-open validity [from, to) overlaps the inclusive query range [start, end]."""
    lo, hi = d.get("valid_from"), d.get("valid_to")
    return (lo is None or lo <= end) and (hi is None or hi > start)

def holders_between(G, relation, target, start, end):
    return sorted({u for u, _, d in G.in_edges(target, data=True)
                   if d["relation"] == relation and overlaps(d, start, end)})

from datetime import date
from itertools import combinations
import networkx as nx

def overlap(a, b):
    a0, a1 = a["valid_from"] or date.min, a["valid_to"] or date.max
    b0, b1 = b["valid_from"] or date.min, b["valid_to"] or date.max
    return a0 < b1 and b0 < a1                      # half-open intervals

def find_conflicts(G, functional):
    by_key = {}
    for u, v, k, d in G.edges(keys=True, data=True):
        if d["relation"] in functional:
            by_key.setdefault((d["relation"], v), []).append((u, k, d))
    conflicts = []
    for (rel, target), edges in by_key.items():
        for (u1, k1, d1), (u2, k2, d2) in combinations(edges, 2):
            if u1 != u2 and overlap(d1, d2):
                conflicts.append((rel, target, (u1, d1["source_id"]), (u2, d2["source_id"])))
    return conflicts

G = nx.MultiDiGraph()
G.add_edge("JONAS VEHL", "KESTREL LABS", key="a", relation="CTO_OF",
           valid_from=date(2021, 1, 1), valid_to=date(2026, 3, 1), source_id="12-0")
G.add_edge("PRIYA NAIR", "KESTREL LABS", key="b", relation="CTO_OF",
           valid_from=date(2025, 1, 1), valid_to=None, source_id="bad-doc")
print(find_conflicts(G, {"CTO_OF"}))
# [('CTO_OF', 'KESTREL LABS', ('JONAS VEHL', '12-0'), ('PRIYA NAIR', 'bad-doc'))]

With the default exclusive=None, add_fact treats CTO_OF as exclusive and resolves every overlap in favor of the fact added last: adding Nair from 2025-01 would close Vehl's interval at 2025-01, stamp invalidated_at, and the conflict would vanish. That is the right behavior when a newer source reports a change, and the wrong behavior when a source is simply mistaken. A production pipeline runs conflict detection on the raw claims first, and only lets the closing rule apply when the new fact is not contradicted by a closed, sourced interval like Vehl's.

Exercise 10.5 Stretch

Claims about the same event arrive from many chunks: “Marlow acquired Deepcast” appears in the trade journal (April 2025), the board memo (as “change of ownership last month”), the Tern blog (implicitly), the Series B release and the podcast (“last year”). Design and implement merge_claims(claims) that groups claims into events and produces one merged record per event with: the tightest consistent date range, a status, all source ids, and a flag when the dates are inconsistent. State your grouping key and your status rule.

Show answer

Grouping key. (resolved subject, resolved object, claim type), then split a group into separate events if date ranges are disjoint by more than a tolerance (two funding rounds by the same investor are two events). Normalize types with a small synonym map first (ACQUIRED, BUYOUT → ACQUISITION).

Dates. Intersect ranges: the event must lie in every source's range if all are right. If the intersection is empty, keep the range from the most precise source (narrowest range) and flag an inconsistency.

Status. TRUE if any source asserts TRUE and none asserts FALSE; FALSE if any source asserts FALSE and none TRUE; otherwise SUSPECTED, and a TRUE/FALSE split is flagged as a contradiction.

from collections import defaultdict
from datetime import date

TYPE_SYNONYMS = {"ACQUIRED": "ACQUISITION", "BUYOUT": "ACQUISITION", "PURCHASE": "ACQUISITION"}

def _d(s, default):
    return date.fromisoformat(s[:10]) if s else default

def merge_claims(claims, tolerance_days=45):
    groups = defaultdict(list)
    for c in claims:
        groups[(c.subject, c.object, TYPE_SYNONYMS.get(c.type, c.type))].append(c)
    merged = []
    for key, items in groups.items():
        items.sort(key=lambda c: _d(c.start_date, date.min))
        events, current = [], [items[0]]
        for c in items[1:]:
            prev_end = max(_d(x.end_date, date.max) for x in current)
            if (_d(c.start_date, date.min) - prev_end).days > tolerance_days:
                events.append(current)
                current = [c]
            else:
                current.append(c)
        events.append(current)
        for ev in events:
            lo = max(_d(c.start_date, date.min) for c in ev)
            hi = min(_d(c.end_date, date.max) for c in ev)
            inconsistent = lo > hi
            if inconsistent:                     # fall back to the most precise source
                best = min(ev, key=lambda c: (_d(c.end_date, date.max) - _d(c.start_date, date.min)).days)
                lo, hi = _d(best.start_date, date.min), _d(best.end_date, date.max)
            statuses = {c.status for c in ev}
            status = ("TRUE" if "TRUE" in statuses and "FALSE" not in statuses else
                      "FALSE" if "FALSE" in statuses and "TRUE" not in statuses else "SUSPECTED")
            merged.append({"subject": key[0], "object": key[1], "type": key[2], "status": status,
                           "start": lo, "end": hi, "sources": sorted({c.source_id for c in ev}),
                           "inconsistent_dates": inconsistent,
                           "contradiction": {"TRUE", "FALSE"} <= statuses})
    return merged

On the acquisition: the journal gives 2025-04-15, the memo gives April 2025 (2025-04-01 to 04-30) and the podcast gives 2025. The intersection is 2025-04-15, status TRUE, with three sources. The SUSPECTED closing claim (“expected to close in May”) has a different type (ACQUISITION_CLOSING) and stays separate, which is correct: it is a forecast about a different moment. One weakness remains: the gap-based event splitting sorts by start date, so a wide range like “2025” can swallow two nearby events. Splitting on precise sources first, then attaching coarse ones to the event they contain, is more reliable.

Questions learners often ask

Can I put dates into relationship descriptions and let the LLM read them?

Do that too, since the extraction prompt already asks for “dates when stated”. But text dates only help once an edge is in the context window. They don't help retrieval choose edges, they get mangled by summarization, and they can't be filtered or compared. Structured valid_from and valid_to make time usable before generation as well as during it.

Should claims become edges in the graph?

Dated state claims with two entities (SUPPLIED, CTO_OF) are good candidates to promote into relationships with validity intervals. Allegations, estimates and single-entity claims (“Kestrel faces execution risk on Tern”) usually fit better as covariates attached to the subject node: turning a SUSPECTED price estimate into an edge makes it look as solid as a confirmed fact to any traversal that ignores attributes. GraphRAG keeps claims in their own table for exactly that reason.

What if a document has no date?

Try metadata first (file timestamps, URL paths, headers), then the latest absolute date mentioned in the document as a lower bound. If nothing works, leave relative expressions unresolved and keep the phrase. Never silently anchor on today's date: a 2021 article read in 2026 would place “next year” in 2027.

Does claim extraction replace entity extraction?

No. Claims hang off entities, so you need the entity graph first, and GraphRAG runs claims as an optional extra workflow. The claim prompt does extract entity names as subjects and objects, and those names must pass through the same resolution map from Chapter 9, or claims will attach to unresolved aliases.

How do I evaluate claim extraction?

Hand-label claims for a dozen chunks, then score three things separately: whether each gold claim was found (matching on subject, object and type), whether its status matches, and whether its date range contains the gold date and is not wildly wider. Status and date accuracy are usually much lower than detection, and they are what temporal questions depend on. Keep the canonical temporal questions 6 and 7 as end-to-end tests.

Key takeaways

  • Summarized descriptions merge facts across time and lose order; claims keep events, dated states and uncertain assertions separate and attributable.
  • A GraphRAG claim has subject, object, type, status (TRUE, FALSE, SUSPECTED), start and end dates, description and source text. Status is relative to the chunk.
  • Resolve relative time expressions against the document date, and store range, granularity and original phrase. Plans and forecasts are not facts.
  • Put half-open validity intervals on state relationships and single dates on events; an as-of query is then a filter.
  • Non-overlapping intervals are change, overlapping intervals on a functional relation are contradiction, and a precise date inside a coarse one is just precision.
  • Valid time is when a fact was true; transaction time is when you learned it. Bi-temporal graphs (Chapter 27) track both.
