Personalized PageRank retrieval (HippoRAG)

Local search expands a fixed number of hops around the question's entities. Personalized PageRank does something smarter: it spreads relevance from those entities through the whole graph and lets nodes that sit between several of them light up. In this chapter you compute it by hand, implement it, and use it to rank passages the way HippoRAG does.

You will be able to

  • Explain Personalized PageRank as a random walk with restart, and state its equation with an explicit convention for α
  • Run power iteration by hand on a small Kestrel subgraph and reason about convergence speed
  • Implement personalized_pagerank from scratch and check it against networkx.pagerank
  • Describe HippoRAG's offline index (OpenIE triples, synonym edges) and online steps (query NER, linking, node specificity, PPR, passage scores)
  • Explain what HippoRAG 2 changes and why one PPR run can answer multi-hop questions
  • Diagnose the failure modes: hub domination, bad entity linking and passage-length bias

Spreading activation from the question

Take canonical question 3: “What connects Priya Nair to Deepcast?” In Chapter 14 you would find the two seed entities, pull their 1-hop neighborhoods, and hope the connection shows up in the tables. It does, but so does everything else the two entities touch: Priya's project Sentinel, her CTO role, Deepcast's supply contract. A 2-hop expansion would include most of the Kestrel graph, because every path through the hub Kestrel Labs reaches almost everything.

What you actually want is a score for every node that answers: how strongly is this node connected to the question's entities, taking all paths into account, with short paths counting more than long ones and paths through hubs counting less? Nodes that sit between both seeds, such as Marlow Dynamics (where Priya used to work and which acquired Deepcast) and Project Tern (which Priya co-leads and which replaces Deepcast's sonar), should score high. A node that touches only one seed through a hub should score low.

That score has a name: Personalized PageRank (PPR). The personalization vector was already in the 1998 PageRank paper, and Haveliwala's topic-sensitive PageRank (2002) made it a ranking tool in its own right. In 2024 HippoRAG showed it makes a strong multi-hop retriever. The HippoRAG paper frames it with a neuroscience analogy, the hippocampal memory indexing theory: the LLM plays the neocortex that processes language, and the knowledge graph plays the hippocampus, an index of associations. A cue (the question) activates a few index entries, and activation spreads to associated memories. PPR is the spreading.

Where this sits in the retrieval toolbox

Local search (Chapter 14) and LightRAG (Chapter 16) pick entities and read their neighborhoods. PPR ranks all nodes by proximity to the seeds and then ranks passages from node scores. Path retrieval (Chapter 18) returns explicit chains instead of scores. The three are complementary, and Chapter 21 fuses them.

Random walk with restart

The walker

Put a walker on one of the question's entities. At every step it flips a biased coin:

  • With probability α it follows an edge: it moves to a neighbor chosen in proportion to edge weight (uniformly if the graph is unweighted).
  • With probability 1 − α it restarts: it jumps back to a seed entity, chosen according to a personalization vector p (also called the reset, restart or teleport distribution).

Run the walk forever and count the fraction of time spent at each node. That long-run distribution is the PPR vector r. Nodes close to the seeds get visited often, because the walker keeps coming back to the seeds and rarely wanders far before the next restart. Nodes reachable by many short paths from the seeds get visited more than nodes reachable by one. That is the whole idea; everything else is linear algebra to compute it without simulating a walker.

Classic PageRank is the special case where p is uniform over all nodes: the walker teleports anywhere, and the score measures global importance. Personalizing p turns “important” into “important to this question”.

The equation, and the convention this course uses

Let n be the number of nodes. Build the transition matrix M so that column j holds the probabilities of stepping from node j to each node i:

Mij = wji / ∑k wjk   (for an unweighted graph, Mij = 1 / deg(j) if i and j are adjacent, else 0)

Each column sums to 1 (the matrix is column-stochastic). If r is a probability distribution over nodes, M r is the distribution after one edge-following step. The PPR vector is the fixed point of “restart with probability 1 − α, otherwise step”:

r = (1 − α) p + α · M r

In this course, α is the probability of following an edge (the damping factor), and 1 − α is the restart probability. That matches NetworkX, the pagerank visualizations on this site, Chapter 4 and the minigraphrag code.

The damping and teleport naming trap

Papers and libraries disagree about which probability gets the Greek letter. NetworkX's alpha and igraph's damping are the follow-an-edge probability (default 0.85). Much of the random-walk-with-restart literature writes c or α for the restart probability (often 0.15). Some papers call the restart probability the “damping factor”. Before copying a hyperparameter, find the equation, not the name. HippoRAG sets its damping factor to 0.5, which happens to mean the same thing under both readings, but most values do not survive a convention swap: 0.85 in one is 0.15 in the other, and those produce very different rankings.

What α controls

Unroll the equation by substituting it into itself:

r = (1 − α) ∑k≥0 αk Mk p = (1 − α)(I − αM)−1 p

Read the sum as a mixture of walks. Mk p is where a walker ends up after exactly k steps from the seeds, and (1 − α)αk is the probability that a walk lasts exactly k steps before restarting. Walk lengths are geometrically distributed with mean α / (1 − α):

For retrieval you usually want small α. A question's answer is rarely more than two or three hops from its entities, and long walks drift toward whatever the whole corpus talks about most. On the Kestrel graph below, with seeds Marlow Dynamics and Project Tern, α = 0.5 ranks the seeds first and then Kestrel Labs (0.131), Priya Nair (0.093) and Deepcast (0.091). At α = 0.85, Kestrel Labs (0.198) overtakes both seeds: the hub wins because long walks keep passing through it.

Power iteration by hand

The fixed-point equation suggests an algorithm: start anywhere, apply the right-hand side, repeat. This is power iteration:

r(0) = p,    r(t+1) = (1 − α) p + α · M r(t)

One pass by hand on a small graph makes everything that follows concrete.

A five-node Kestrel subgraph

Seeds M and T get equal weight, so p = (M 0.5, D 0, K 0, P 0, T 0.5). Use α = 0.5. Each node sends its mass equally to its neighbors: M sends a third of its mass to each of D, K and P; D sends half to M and half to K; K sends a third each to M, D and T; P sends half each to M and T; T sends half each to K and P.

Iteration 1

Start with r(0) = p. Only M and T hold mass.

  • M's 0.5 splits three ways: 0.1667 each to D, K, P.
  • T's 0.5 splits two ways: 0.25 each to K and P.

So M r(0) = (M 0, D 0.1667, K 0.4167, P 0.4167, T 0). Now mix: r(1) = 0.5 · p + 0.5 · M r(0).

Why it converges, and how fast

Subtract the fixed point from the iteration: r(t+1) − r = α M (r(t) − r). A column-stochastic matrix never increases the L1 norm of a vector, so the error shrinks by at least a factor α per iteration:

‖r(t) − r‖1 ≤ αt ‖p − r‖1 ≤ 2αt

To reach an L1 error of ε you need at most t = log(ε/2) / log(α) iterations: 21 for α = 0.5 and ε = 10−6, 90 for α = 0.85. That is a worst-case bound; real graphs usually mix faster (Exercise 17.3 measures it). Each iteration is one sparse matrix-vector product, O(|E|) work, so PPR on a graph with a million edges and α = 0.5 costs a few tens of millions of floating-point operations per query: milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, far cheaper than an LLM call.

Two practical details:

  • Dangling nodes. A node with no outgoing edges (an isolated entity in an undirected graph, or a sink in a directed one) has an all-zero column, and the mass that reaches it would leak out of the distribution. The standard fix is to send that mass back to the seeds, which keeps r a probability distribution. NetworkX does the same by default.
  • Direction. Extracted knowledge graphs have directed relations (“Marlow acquired Deepcast”), but for retrieval the association matters in both directions. HippoRAG and minigraphrag run PPR on the undirected graph. Directed PPR would make sinks like “Halden Reef” unreachable from anything they point to.

From scratch in NumPy

This is the implementation in minigraphrag. It builds the dense transition matrix for readability; for large graphs swap in scipy.sparse with the same arithmetic.

import networkx as nx
import numpy as np

def personalized_pagerank(G: nx.Graph, seeds: dict[str, float], alpha: float = 0.85, iters: int = 50,
                          tol: float = 1e-10, weight: str = "weight") -> dict[str, float]:
    """Power iteration: p <- (1 - alpha) * s + alpha * (M p + dangling mass * s)."""
    nodes = list(G.nodes())
    if not nodes:
        return {}
    idx = {n: i for i, n in enumerate(nodes)}
    n = len(nodes)
    s = np.zeros(n)
    for node, w in seeds.items():
        if node in idx:
            s[idx[node]] += max(w, 0.0)
    s = s / s.sum() if s.sum() > 0 else np.full(n, 1.0 / n)  # no valid seeds: plain PageRank

    # Column-stochastic transition matrix: M[i, j] = P(step j -> i).
    W = np.zeros((n, n))
    for u, v, d in G.edges(data=True):
        w = float(d.get(weight, 1.0))
        W[idx[v], idx[u]] += w
        if u != v:
            W[idx[u], idx[v]] += w
    out = W.sum(axis=0)
    dangling = out == 0  # nodes with no edges: their walker teleports back to the seeds
    M = W / np.where(dangling, 1.0, out)

    p = s.copy()
    for _ in range(iters):
        new = (1 - alpha) * s + alpha * (M @ p + p[dangling].sum() * s)
        if np.abs(new - p).sum() < tol:
            p = new
            break
        p = new
    return {node: float(p[i]) for node, i in idx.items()}

Three choices matter. Seed weights are normalized, so you can pass raw scores such as {"MARLOW DYNAMICS": 0.5, "PROJECT TERN": 1.0}. Edge weights are used if present; in minigraphrag the weight is the summed relationship strength from extraction (Chapter 9), so relations stated often and strongly carry more walkers. And if no seed is found in the graph, the function falls back to global PageRank rather than returning zeros; a caller that prefers “no result” should check the seeds first.

Checking against NetworkX

Always test a from-scratch numerical routine against a reference. NetworkX's alpha uses the same convention as ours (probability of following an edge). In NetworkX 3, pagerank uses SciPy under the hood, so install scipy first.

import networkx as nx
import pytest
from minigraphrag.search.ppr import personalized_pagerank

def five_node_graph():
    G = nx.Graph()
    G.add_edges_from([("M", "D"), ("M", "K"), ("M", "P"), ("D", "K"), ("K", "T"), ("P", "T")])
    return G

def test_matches_hand_computation():
    r = personalized_pagerank(five_node_graph(), {"M": 1, "T": 1}, alpha=0.5, iters=200)
    expected = {"M": 0.328, "D": 0.080, "K": 0.152, "P": 0.132, "T": 0.308}
    assert all(abs(r[k] - v) < 1e-3 for k, v in expected.items())

def test_matches_networkx():
    pytest.importorskip("scipy")
    G = five_node_graph()
    ours = personalized_pagerank(G, {"M": 1, "T": 1}, alpha=0.5, iters=200)
    ref = nx.pagerank(G, alpha=0.5, personalization={"M": 1, "T": 1}, tol=1e-12)
    assert all(abs(ours[k] - ref[k]) < 1e-6 for k in G)
When NetworkX is enough

For production you rarely need your own PPR: nx.pagerank(G, alpha=0.5, personalization=seeds) is fine up to hundreds of thousands of edges, and graph databases ship PPR procedures (for example, Neo4j's Graph Data Science library has a Personalized PageRank mode through its source-nodes option). Write your own when you need to control dangling mass, reuse one sparse matrix across many queries, or run batched PPR for many questions at once as a matrix of seed vectors.

HippoRAG: a knowledge graph as an associative index

HippoRAG (Gutiérrez et al., NeurIPS 2024) turns PPR into a passage retriever. It has an offline indexing phase that builds a schemaless knowledge graph, and an online phase that runs one PPR per question.

Offline: OpenIE triples and synonym edges

HippoRAG does not fix a schema. For each passage it prompts an LLM in two steps: first extract the named entities, then extract OpenIE triples (subject, relation, object) whose subjects and objects should be those entities or other noun phrases. From the Kestrel press release about the acquisition you get triples like (Marlow Dynamics, acquired, Deepcast) and (Deepcast, ended outside contracts with, Kestrel Labs).

  • Every distinct noun phrase becomes a phrase node. Every triple becomes a relation edge.
  • A retrieval encoder (the paper uses Contriever or ColBERTv2) embeds every node. Pairs whose cosine similarity exceeds a threshold (τ = 0.8 in the paper) get a synonym edge. This is a soft form of entity resolution: “Sentinel drones” and “Project Sentinel” end up adjacent even if no merge step collapsed them, so walkers can cross between them.
  • A passage matrix P with one row per node and one column per passage records how often each node's phrase appears in each passage. This is provenance, the same idea as source_ids in Chapter 8.

Compare this with the GraphRAG-style index you built in Part 2: there are no entity types, no descriptions, no summaries and no communities. The graph exists only to be walked, so HippoRAG's indexing is cheaper per passage than a full GraphRAG index while still paying one or two LLM calls per passage.

Online: from query to passage scores

  1. Query NER. An LLM extracts the query's named entities: for “How is Marlow Dynamics related to Project Tern?”, Marlow Dynamics and Project Tern.
  2. Linking. Each query entity is embedded with the same retrieval encoder and linked to the graph node with the highest cosine similarity. These are the query nodes.
  3. Node specificity. Not all query nodes are equally informative. HippoRAG defines the specificity of node i as si = 1 / |Pi|, where Pi is the set of passages the node was extracted from. It multiplies each query node's reset probability by si. The paper motivates this as a cheap, local version of inverse document frequency: a node mentioned in every document tells you little about which document you want.
  4. PPR. Run Personalized PageRank with the specificity-weighted query nodes as the personalization vector and damping 0.5.
  5. Passage scores. Multiply the PPR vector by the passage matrix: score = Pᵀ r. Each passage gets the sum, over the nodes it mentions, of mention count times node probability. Take the top-k passages as context for the reader LLM.

Here is the whole online phase on the five-node example, with four passages from the Kestrel corpus. For simplicity each passage mentions each node at most once.

Look at the last step again. Passage p3 (Priya's profile) never mentions Project Tern, and p2 (the Tern blog) never mentions Marlow Dynamics. A dense retriever scores each passage against the query independently, so neither looks like a strong match. PPR ranks both highly because Priya Nair, whom both mention, sits between the two seeds.

Why one PPR run handles multi-hop questions

Iterative retrievers such as IRCoT answer multi-hop questions by alternating: retrieve, reason, write a follow-up query, retrieve again. Each hop costs an LLM call and a retrieval, and an early mistake derails the chain. PPR explores all hops at once, because the walk mixture ∑ αk Mk p already contains 1-hop, 2-hop and 3-hop contributions from every seed. A node that is two hops from one seed and one hop from another collects mass from both. Bridge entities come out of the linear algebra, not out of a reasoning chain.

The price is that PPR returns scores, not reasons. It tells you Priya Nair matters to this question, not why. The reader LLM still has to work out the chain from the retrieved passages, and if the bridge fact lives in a passage that did not make the top-k cut, the answer fails. Chapter 18's path retrieval makes the chain explicit.

Gutiérrez et al., HippoRAG (2024)

On multi-hop QA benchmarks (MuSiQue, 2WikiMultiHopQA and HotpotQA), HippoRAG's single-step retrieval outperformed strong dense retrievers by up to 20%, with the largest gains on 2WikiMultiHopQA, whose questions hinge on entities. The authors report that single-step HippoRAG is comparable to or better than iterative retrieval such as IRCoT while being 10–30 times cheaper and 6–13 times faster, and that the two approaches combine well. The LLM was GPT-3.5-turbo and the retrieval encoder Contriever or ColBERTv2.

Implementing HippoRAG search

minigraphrag implements the online phase on top of the index you built in Part 2. It deviates from the paper in three small, deliberate ways, noted in the comments: it adds literal name and alias matches to embedding linking, it reuses the LightRAG keyword prompt as query NER (so the offline MockLLM can run it), and it uses source_ids as a binary passage matrix.

import re
from ..context import format_table
from ..llm import LLM, parse_json_response
from .. import prompts as P
from ..tokens import count_tokens
from .common import DEFAULT_RESPONSE_TYPE, SearchResult, doc_ids_for_units, generate_answer

def link_query_to_nodes(index, query: str, llm: LLM | None = None, top_k: int = 5) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Seed weights for PPR: query entities linked to graph nodes, weighted by node specificity."""
    G = index.graph
    scores: dict[str, float] = {}
    q = query.upper()
    for n, d in G.nodes(data=True):                        # literal name or alias in the query
        if any(re.search(rf"(?<!\w){re.escape(s)}(?!\w)", q) for s in [n, *d.get("aliases", [])]):
            scores[n] = 1.0
    phrases = []
    if llm is not None:                                    # query NER via the keyword prompt
        data = parse_json_response(llm.complete(P.KEYWORD_EXTRACTION_PROMPT.format(query=query), json=True))
        phrases = [str(k) for k in data.get("low_level_keywords", [])]
    for phrase in phrases or [query]:                      # link each phrase to its nearest entity
        for name, sim, _ in index.entity_store.search(index.embedder.embed([phrase])[0], k=1 if phrases else top_k):
            scores[name] = max(scores.get(name, 0.0), sim)
    top = dict(sorted(scores.items(), key=lambda x: -x[1])[:top_k])
    # Node specificity: divide by the number of text units the entity was extracted from.
    return {n: w / max(1, len(G.nodes[n].get("source_ids", []))) for n, w in top.items() if w > 0}

def rank_passages(index, node_scores: dict[str, float]) -> list[tuple[str, float]]:
    """Score each text unit by the summed PPR score of the entities extracted from it (P^T r)."""
    totals: dict[str, float] = {}
    for n, score in node_scores.items():
        for uid in index.graph.nodes[n].get("source_ids", []):
            totals[uid] = totals.get(uid, 0.0) + score
    return sorted(totals.items(), key=lambda x: (-x[1], x[0]))

def hipporag_search(index, query: str, llm: LLM, top_k_passages: int = 5, alpha: float = 0.85,
                    max_context_tokens: int = 4000, response_type: str = DEFAULT_RESPONSE_TYPE) -> SearchResult:
    seeds = link_query_to_nodes(index, query, llm)
    ppr = personalized_pagerank(index.graph, seeds, alpha=alpha)
    rows, used = [], 0
    for uid, score in rank_passages(index, ppr)[:top_k_passages]:
        text = index.text_units[uid].text
        if used + count_tokens(text) > max_context_tokens:
            break
        rows.append([uid, text])
        used += count_tokens(text)
    top_nodes = sorted(ppr.items(), key=lambda x: -x[1])[:10]
    context = format_table("Sources", ["id", "text"], rows)
    answer = generate_answer(llm, query, context, response_type)
    return SearchResult(answer, "ppr", context,
                        {"seeds": seeds, "top_nodes": top_nodes, "sources": [{"id": r[0], "text": r[1]} for r in rows]},
                        doc_ids_for_units(index, [r[0] for r in rows]), llm_calls=2)
Default α is 0.85 here, 0.5 in the paper

hipporag_search defaults to alpha=0.85, the classic PageRank value, while HippoRAG uses 0.5. On the small, dense Kestrel graph the difference changes rankings noticeably (you saw Kestrel Labs overtake the seeds at 0.85). Pass alpha=0.5 to reproduce the paper's behavior, and tune it on your evaluation set (Chapter 25) rather than trusting either default.

Using it looks like every other search method in the package:

Print the seeds before anything else when debugging. Almost every bad PPR result traces back to linking: a query entity linked to the wrong node, or not linked at all. PPR faithfully spreads mass from wrong seeds.

HippoRAG 2: from retrieval to memory

HippoRAG 2 (Gutiérrez et al., ICML 2025, “From RAG to Memory: Non-Parametric Continual Learning for Large Language Models”) keeps the PPR core but addresses weaknesses the authors found in the original: it relied too heavily on entities, so questions whose meaning lives in context rather than names suffered, and simple factual questions sometimes did worse than plain dense retrieval. It adds three changes.

1. Passage nodes in the graph (dense-sparse integration)

Every passage becomes a node, connected by context edges to the phrase nodes extracted from it. The graph now holds both sparse concepts (phrases) and dense contexts (passages). The passage matrix disappears as a separate step: passages are ranked directly by their own PPR scores, and PPR mass can flow through a passage from one of its phrases to another.

2. Query-to-triple matching

Instead of running NER on the query and linking single entities, HippoRAG 2 embeds the whole query and matches it against embeddings of the indexed triples, keeping the top 5. A triple like (Priya Nair, formerly worked at, Marlow Dynamics) carries the relation, so a query about “the engineer who came from the competitor” can match it even though it names neither entity.

3. Recognition memory: LLM filtering of triples

The retrieved triples go to an LLM that filters out the ones irrelevant to the query, a step the paper calls recognition memory by analogy to how people recognize whether a cue matters. The phrase nodes of the surviving triples (at most five triples, so at most ten phrase nodes) become seeds, weighted by their triple scores. All passage nodes are also seeded, each with its dense similarity to the query multiplied by a small weight factor (0.05 by default), so passage similarity guides the walk without swamping the entity seeds. If the filter keeps no triples and no phrase node is available, the system falls back to plain dense passage retrieval. PPR then runs with damping 0.5, and passages are ranked by their PPR scores.

The paper evaluates retrieval as memory along three axes: factual memory (simple QA), sense-making (long, discourse-level understanding) and associativity (multi-hop). It reports that earlier structure-augmented RAG methods, including the original HippoRAG, often underperform a strong embedding retriever on factual and sense-making tasks, while HippoRAG 2 improves associative memory tasks by 7% over a leading embedding model, as the abstract puts it, without giving up factual performance. The ablations credit both the passage nodes and query-to-triple matching.

The cost profile is attractive. Indexing costs one or two LLM calls per passage, similar to extraction in any GraphRAG pipeline and with no summarization or community reports. Querying costs one small LLM call (NER or triple filtering), one sparse PPR and one generation call. There is no per-hop LLM loop, so latency stays flat as questions get more hops. The weaknesses are that PPR has no notion of relation semantics or time (“Who is the CTO?” scores Jonas Vehl and Priya Nair alike) and that it produces rankings, not explanations.

import networkx as nx
from minigraphrag.search.ppr import personalized_pagerank

def specificity_seeds(G, linked: dict[str, float]) -> dict[str, float]:
    """linked: {node: link similarity}. Returns normalized reset weights."""
    raw = {n: sim / max(1, len(G.nodes[n].get("source_ids", []))) for n, sim in linked.items() if n in G}
    total = sum(raw.values())
    return {n: w / total for n, w in raw.items()} if total else {}

G = nx.Graph([("M", "D"), ("M", "K"), ("M", "P"), ("D", "K"), ("K", "T"), ("P", "T")])
passages = {"p1": ["M", "D", "K"], "p2": ["T", "K", "D", "P"], "p3": ["P", "M", "K"], "p4": ["K"]}
for n in G:
    G.nodes[n]["source_ids"] = [p for p, ns in passages.items() if n in ns]

seeds = specificity_seeds(G, {"K": 1.0, "T": 1.0})
print(seeds)                                  # {'K': 0.2, 'T': 0.8}
r = personalized_pagerank(G, seeds, alpha=0.5, iters=200)
scores = {p: sum(r[n] for n in ns) for p, ns in passages.items()}
print({k: round(v, 3) for k, v in r.items()})
print({k: round(v, 3) for k, v in scores.items()})

import math
import networkx as nx
import numpy as np

edges = ("okafor-kestrel vehl-kestrel priya-kestrel okafor-twi priya-marlow ruiz-twi kestrel-sentinel "
         "twi-sentinel priya-sentinel sentinel-halden sentinel-bleach bleach-halden ruiz-halden bright-kestrel "
         "lena-bright lena-kestrel deepcast-kestrel marlow-deepcast marlow-kestrel kestrel-tern vehl-tern "
         "priya-tern tern-deepcast kestrel-port twi-port").split()
G = nx.Graph([e.split("-") for e in edges])
nodes = list(G); idx = {n: i for i, n in enumerate(nodes)}
A = nx.to_numpy_array(G, nodelist=nodes)
M = A / A.sum(axis=0)
p = np.zeros(len(nodes)); p[idx["marlow"]] = p[idx["tern"]] = 0.5

for alpha in (0.5, 0.85, 0.95):
    r, t = p.copy(), 0
    while True:
        new = (1 - alpha) * p + alpha * M @ r
        t += 1
        if np.abs(new - r).sum() < 1e-6:
            break
        r = new
    print(alpha, t, math.ceil(math.log(1e-6 / 2) / math.log(alpha)))

Output: α = 0.5 stops after 15 iterations (bound 21); α = 0.85 after 35 (bound 90); α = 0.95 after 50 (bound 283). The observed counts are well under the bound because the graph mixes quickly, but they still grow sharply with α. Two implications: at α = 0.5 a fixed iters=30 is plenty, and at high α a fixed small iteration count silently returns an unconverged vector, which biases scores toward the seeds. Use a tolerance check like minigraphrag does, and log the iteration count. (Note the stopping rule measures change between iterations, not distance to the true fixed point; the true error is at most α/(1−α) times the last change.)

from minigraphrag.search.ppr import personalized_pagerank

def lift_scores(G, seeds, alpha=0.85):
    ppr = personalized_pagerank(G, seeds, alpha=alpha, iters=200)
    glob = personalized_pagerank(G, {}, alpha=alpha, iters=200)   # empty seeds: uniform teleport
    return {n: ppr[n] / glob[n] for n in G}

lift = lift_scores(G, {"marlow": 1, "tern": 1})
for n, s in sorted(lift.items(), key=lambda x: -x[1])[:6]:
    print(f"{s:5.2f}  {n}")

At α = 0.85, raw PPR ranks Kestrel Labs first (0.198) because its global PageRank is also the highest (0.183). Its lift is only about 1.08: it is barely more relevant to this query than to any query. Marlow Dynamics (2.40) and Project Tern (2.13) lead, and Deepcast (1.56), Priya Nair (1.35) and Jonas Vehl (1.22) all rise above Kestrel Labs, while the reef-side nodes drop to lifts well below 1. The risk: lift over-rewards obscure nodes. A leaf adjacent to a seed has tiny global PageRank, so even modest PPR gives it a huge ratio. In practice people use a softened version such as ppr / globalβ with β around 0.5, or simply lower α, which is what HippoRAG does.

import networkx as nx
from minigraphrag.search.ppr import personalized_pagerank

def hipporag2_rank(index, query: str, passage_weight: float = 0.05, top_triples: int = 5, alpha: float = 0.5):
    G = nx.Graph(index.graph)                         # copy: do not mutate the index
    for uid, unit in index.text_units.items():
        G.add_node(("P", uid), kind="passage")
    for n, d in index.graph.nodes(data=True):
        for uid in d.get("source_ids", []):
            G.add_edge(n, ("P", uid), weight=1.0)      # context edges

    qvec = index.embedder.embed([query])[0]
    seeds: dict = {}
    # Query-to-triple matching: relationship embeddings stand in for triples.
    for _, sim, meta in index.relationship_store.search(qvec, k=top_triples):
        for n in (meta["source"], meta["target"]):
            seeds[n] = max(seeds.get(n, 0.0), float(sim))
    # Dense passage seeds, down-weighted so entities still steer the walk.
    for uid, sim, _ in index.text_unit_store.search(qvec, k=len(index.text_units)):
        seeds[("P", uid)] = passage_weight * max(float(sim), 0.0)

    ppr = personalized_pagerank(G, seeds, alpha=alpha, iters=100)
    ranked = sorted(((n[1], s) for n, s in ppr.items() if isinstance(n, tuple)), key=lambda x: -x[1])
    return ranked

for q in ["Why did Kestrel Labs start Project Tern?",
          "Which investor's board member oversees the company whose drones detected the Halden Reef bleaching?",
          "What connects Priya Nair to Deepcast?"]:
    top = hipporag2_rank(index, q)[:5]
    print(q, [index.text_units[u].doc_id for u, _ in top])

Check your understanding

In the equation r = (1 − α) p + α · M r as used in this course, what does α = 0.85 mean?

  1. The walker restarts at a seed with probability 0.85
  2. The walker follows an edge with probability 0.85 and restarts with probability 0.15
  3. 85% of the mass stays on the seed nodes
  4. The walk is truncated after 85 steps
α multiplies the edge-following term, so it is the damping (follow) probability. This matches NetworkX's alpha. Many random-walk-with-restart papers use the letter for the restart probability instead, so always check the equation.

Why does HippoRAG use damping 0.5 rather than the classic 0.85?

  1. 0.5 converges to the exact answer while 0.85 only approximates it
  2. 0.85 is only valid for directed graphs
  3. Shorter walks keep mass near the query entities, so hubs and far-away structure dominate less
  4. It halves the number of LLM calls
Walk lengths are geometric with mean α/(1−α): one step at 0.5 versus about 5.7 at 0.85. Retrieval answers are usually within a few hops, and long walks drift toward globally central nodes. Both values converge; 0.5 also converges faster.

Kestrel Labs appears in 40 of 50 passages; Project Tern in 2. A query names both. How does node specificity change the reset vector?

  1. It removes Kestrel Labs from the seeds
  2. It gives both seeds 0.5, because both were named in the query
  3. It boosts Kestrel Labs because it is better connected
  4. Kestrel Labs gets weight proportional to 1/40 and Project Tern 1/2, so Tern carries about 95% of the reset mass
Specificity is 1/|Pi|. Normalizing 0.025 and 0.5 gives about 0.048 and 0.952. The hub still seeds the walk, but weakly, like an IDF weight.

What lets a single PPR run retrieve evidence for a two-hop question that iterative retrievers need two rounds for?

  1. The PPR vector sums walks of every length from the seeds, so bridge nodes collect mass from several seeds at once
  2. PPR calls the LLM once per hop internally
  3. HippoRAG precomputes all two-hop paths offline
  4. The passage matrix contains multi-hop edges
r = (1−α) ∑ αk Mk p mixes 1-hop, 2-hop and longer contributions in one linear-algebra computation. No reasoning step is needed to discover the bridge entity, though the reader LLM still has to explain the chain.

Which change is part of HippoRAG 2 rather than the original HippoRAG?

  1. Synonym edges between similar phrase nodes
  2. Query named entity recognition with an LLM
  3. Passage nodes in the graph and an LLM filter over query-matched triples
  4. Community reports used as seeds
Synonym edges and query NER are in the original. HippoRAG 2 adds passage nodes with context edges, matches the query against triples, filters them with an LLM (recognition memory) and ranks passage nodes by PPR directly.

Your PPR retriever returns great passages for “What connects Priya Nair to Deepcast?” but misses the key passage for “Why did Kestrel Labs start Project Tern?”. What is the most likely reason?

  1. PPR did not converge
  2. The graph is undirected
  3. α is too low
  4. The passage that explains the cause mentions Marlow and Deepcast, not the query's entities, so it gets little mass relative to passages that name the seeds
PPR ranks by connectivity to named entities. A “why” question's evidence often sits one hop away in a passage that names neither seed. Fusing with dense or BM25 retrieval, or HippoRAG 2's passage seeds, helps.

Questions learners often ask

Is PPR just a fancy k-hop neighborhood?

No. A k-hop neighborhood is a binary set with a hard cut-off: every node within k hops is in, equally. PPR is a continuous score with a soft, geometric decay, and it rewards multiplicity: a node reachable by three short paths scores higher than one reachable by one. It also penalizes hubs automatically, because a hub splits its outgoing mass among many neighbors. You can threshold PPR scores to get a set, but it will be shaped by the question's seeds rather than a radius.

Should edge weights be used, and which weights?

Use them if they mean “strength of association”. minigraphrag's summed extraction strengths do: relations stated often and strongly carry more walkers. Do not use weights that mean distance or cost (higher = farther), which would invert the semantics. A useful query-time variant multiplies each edge weight by the similarity between the relation description and the query, which makes the walk prefer relevant relation types. That costs one embedding lookup per edge in the explored region.

How do I run PPR on a graph with millions of nodes per query?

Use a sparse matrix (CSR) built once at load time, and run power iteration with scipy.sparse; at α = 0.5 you need 15–30 iterations. For lower latency there are local push algorithms (approximate PPR such as the Andersen–Chung–Lang push method) that touch only nodes near the seeds and skip the rest of the graph, and graph databases offer PPR as a built-in procedure. Cache nothing per query, but do cache the matrix and the node index.

Can I use HippoRAG on the GraphRAG index I already built, or do I need OpenIE triples?

You can use the GraphRAG-style entity graph, which is what minigraphrag does. The differences are in coverage and granularity. OpenIE produces many more, finer-grained phrase nodes (including non-named concepts like “sonar module”), which helps associative questions. A typed entity graph is cleaner and smaller, with fewer noisy edges, which helps precision. If your questions mostly name entities, the existing graph works well; if they describe concepts, add phrase nodes or use HippoRAG 2's passage nodes.

Why rank passages at all? Why not give the LLM the top PPR entities?

Entity names and short descriptions lose the evidence. “Priya Nair” scoring 0.13 does not tell the reader that she previously worked at Marlow. Passages carry the actual sentences, dates and qualifiers, and they are what you cite. A good hybrid is to put the top passages in context and add a short table of top PPR entities with their descriptions, which Chapter 22 covers.

Key takeaways

  • Personalized PageRank is a random walk that restarts at the question's entities: r = (1 − α) p + α · M r, with α the probability of following an edge in this course.
  • Check the convention before copying hyperparameters: “damping”, “teleport” and “restart” names are used inconsistently.
  • Power iteration converges geometrically at rate α; α = 0.5 keeps retrieval local and converges in a couple of dozen iterations.
  • HippoRAG indexes OpenIE triples with synonym edges, seeds PPR with linked query entities weighted by specificity, and scores passages by the mass of the entities they mention.
  • HippoRAG 2 adds passage nodes, query-to-triple matching and LLM triple filtering, improving associative retrieval without losing factual accuracy.
  • One PPR run finds bridge entities for multi-hop questions without an LLM loop, but it ranks rather than explains, and it misses evidence that names none of the seeds.
