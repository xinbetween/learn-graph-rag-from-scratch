Community reports and hierarchical summaries

A community is just a list of entities until someone explains what it is about. This chapter builds that explanation: ranked context tables that fit a token budget, a structured report prompt, bottom-up generation through the hierarchy, and the checks that keep reports specific and grounded.

You will be able to

  • Describe the parts of a GraphRAG community report and why pre-computed reports make global questions answerable
  • Build report context from entity, relationship and claim tables ranked by degree and trimmed to a token budget
  • Generate reports bottom-up, substituting sub-community reports when a community is too large to fit
  • Parse and validate report JSON, including its data references, and embed reports for retrieval
  • Compare community reports with RAPTOR's recursive summary tree and choose between them
  • Estimate report cost and recognize generic, hallucinated and stale reports

What a report is and why it exists

At the end of Chapter 11 the Kestrel graph had a two-level hierarchy. Level 0 split it into “Kestrel business” and “reef and regulation”; level 1 split those into five tighter communities, including the supply-chain community C2 = {Deepcast, Marlow Dynamics, Priya Nair, Project Tern}. Canonical question 5, “What risks does Kestrel Labs face?”, cannot be answered from any single entity: the supply shock lives in C2, the dependency on regulation in the regulation community, the concentration on one reef and one partner in the reef science community.

A community report is an LLM-written summary of one community, produced at indexing time. Global search (Chapter 15) never reads the raw graph; it reads reports. The idea from the GraphRAG paper is a division of labor: pay once, offline, to summarize every part of the corpus at several granularities, so that a question about the whole corpus becomes a map-reduce over a few hundred summaries instead of an impossible read of millions of tokens.

Anatomy

Microsoft GraphRAG's report prompt asks for five things, returned as JSON. minigraphrag uses the same schema:

FieldContentUsed by
titleShort, specific name that includes representative entitiesHuman browsing, report lists, dynamic community selection
summaryExecutive summary of the community's structure and key factsParent-level context when substituting sub-community reports
ratingFloat 0 to 10: the “impact severity” or importance of the communityRanking and filtering reports in global search
rating_explanationOne sentence justifying the ratingAuditing the rating
findings5 to 10 insights, each with a short summary and a multi-paragraph explanation citing data referencesThe substance global search maps over

The citation format is part of the contract: “Points supported by data should list their data references as [Data: <dataset name> (record ids)]”, with no more than five ids per reference (adding “+more” when there are more). The ids are the short human-readable ids of the rows in the context tables, which is why every entity and relationship gets a stable human_id in add_rank_attributes.

Building the report context

The report prompt is only as good as the tables it receives. The context builder has one job: given a community and a token budget, choose the most informative rows and format them so the model can cite them.

Ranked tables

The rows come from the graph built in Chapter 9:

  • Entities: id, name, type, summarized description, degree. Ranked by degree, because hubs participate in the most relationships and usually carry the most information.
  • Relationships: id, source, target, description, combined degree (sum of the endpoints' degrees). Ranked by combined degree, so edges between central entities come first.
  • Claims (when extracted, Chapter 10): id, subject, type, status, dates, description. Attached to the entities they are about.

This is the exact context build_community_context produces for C2 with the default budget (243 tokens by count_tokens, which falls back to four tokens per three words when tiktoken is not installed). The pipe-delimited format is compact and makes ids easy to cite.

Degrees are computed on the whole graph, not inside the community: Priya Nair has degree 4 because she also connects to Kestrel Labs and Project Sentinel, which live in other communities. That is deliberate. Global degree measures importance in the corpus, and a community member that bridges to other communities is often the most interesting one to describe.

Fitting the token budget

For a four-entity community everything fits. A leaf community of ten entities with long summarized descriptions, dozens of relationships and claims can easily exceed the budget. GraphRAG's default report input budget (community_reports.max_input_length) is 8,000 tokens, with up to 2,000 output tokens per report; minigraphrag's IndexConfig.report_max_tokens is 4,000.

There are two common strategies for spending the budget, and the reference implementations differ:

  • Fixed split (minigraphrag): half the budget for entity rows and half for relationship rows, each filled greedily in rank order with fit_to_budget. Simple and predictable, but it can waste budget: a community with short descriptions leaves half its entity budget unused while relationships get cut.
  • Edge-driven growth (Microsoft GraphRAG's sort_context): sort relationships by combined degree, then add them one at a time together with the details of both endpoint entities and their claims, re-rendering the context after each addition and stopping as soon as it would exceed the budget. Entities appear only if they participate in an included relationship, and the tables stay mutually consistent: no relationship refers to an entity whose row was cut.
ENTITY_HEADER = ["id", "entity", "type", "description", "degree"]
RELATIONSHIP_HEADER = ["id", "source", "target", "description", "combined_degree"]


def _entity_rows(G: nx.Graph, nodes: list[str]) -> list[list]:
    ranked = sorted(nodes, key=lambda n: (-G.nodes[n].get("degree", 0), n))
    return [[G.nodes[n]["human_id"], n, G.nodes[n].get("type", ""), G.nodes[n].get("description", ""),
             G.nodes[n].get("degree", 0)] for n in ranked]


def _relationship_rows(G: nx.Graph, edges: list[tuple[str, str]]) -> list[list]:
    ranked = sorted(edges, key=lambda e: (-G.edges[e].get("combined_degree", 0), e))
    return [[G.edges[e]["human_id"], e[0], e[1], G.edges[e].get("description", ""),
             G.edges[e].get("combined_degree", 0)] for e in ranked]


def _fit_rows(rows: list[list], max_tokens: int) -> list[list]:
    """Keep the leading rows whose rendered lines fit in `max_tokens`."""
    lines = ["|".join(str(c) for c in row) for row in rows]
    return rows[: len(fit_to_budget(lines, max_tokens))]


def build_community_context(G: nx.Graph, community: Community, max_tokens: int = 4000) -> str:
    """Entity and relationship tables for one community, truncated to `max_tokens`.

    Half the budget goes to entities and half to relationships, each ranked by degree.
    """
    ent_rows = _fit_rows(_entity_rows(G, community.nodes), max_tokens // 2)
    rel_rows = _fit_rows(_relationship_rows(G, [tuple(e) for e in community.edges]), max_tokens // 2)
    return (format_table("Entities", ENTITY_HEADER, ent_rows) + "\n\n"
            + format_table("Relationships", RELATIONSHIP_HEADER, rel_rows))

Squeeze C2's budget to 150 tokens and the fixed split shows its edge: only Priya Nair's entity row fits in the 75-token entity half, while three of four relationships fit in the other half. The report would cite relationships about Project Tern and Deepcast with no entity rows describing them. Exercise 12.1 asks you to reason through this.

Bottom-up generation through the hierarchy

A leaf community's raw tables usually fit. A level-0 community in a large corpus can have hundreds of entities, and no ranking trick makes that fit in 8,000 tokens without discarding most of it. GraphRAG's solution uses the hierarchy: generate reports for the deepest level first, then, when a parent community's raw context is too large, substitute its sub-communities' reports for their raw rows.

Step through the Kestrel hierarchy from Chapter 11 in the order reports are written.

The substitution algorithm

For each level, from deepest to shallowest, and for each community at that level:

  1. Build the raw context. If it fits the budget, use it.
  2. Otherwise, if the community has children with reports, rank the children by size (largest first) and replace their raw rows with their report summaries, one child at a time, until the context fits. Entities and relationships not covered by a substituted child stay as raw rows.
  3. If there are no children (a leaf that is too large), fall back to truncating the ranked raw tables.

GraphRAG's build_mixed_context annotates step 2 with the comment “replace local context with sub-community reports, starting from the biggest sub-community”. Larger children free the most tokens per substitution, and their reports are the most information-dense. minigraphrag implements a simplified version that substitutes as many child reports as fit in half the budget in one go:

def _full_context_tokens(G: nx.Graph, community: Community) -> int:
    return count_tokens(build_community_context(G, community, max_tokens=10**9))


def _substituted_context(G: nx.Graph, community: Community, child_reports: list[CommunityReport],
                         children: list[Community], max_tokens: int) -> str:
    """Replace raw tables with sub-community reports (largest children first)."""
    sizes = {c.id: len(c.nodes) for c in children}
    ranked = sorted(child_reports, key=lambda r: -sizes.get(r.community_id, 0))
    rows = _fit_rows([[r.community_id, r.title, r.summary] for r in ranked], max_tokens // 2)
    used = {row[0] for row in rows}
    covered = {n for c in children if c.id in used for n in c.nodes}
    remaining = Community(community.id, community.level, community.parent,
                          [n for n in community.nodes if n not in covered],
                          [e for e in community.edges if not (e[0] in covered and e[1] in covered)])
    return (format_table("Reports", ["id", "title", "summary"], rows) + "\n\n"
            + build_community_context(G, remaining, max_tokens // 2))


def generate_all_reports(llm: LLM, G: nx.Graph, communities: list[Community], max_tokens: int = 4000,
                         min_size: int = 2) -> list[CommunityReport]:
    """Write a report for every community (with at least `min_size` nodes), deepest level first."""
    by_id = {c.id: c for c in communities}
    reports: dict[int, CommunityReport] = {}
    for c in sorted(communities, key=lambda c: (-c.level, c.id)):
        if len(c.nodes) < min_size:
            continue
        child_reports = [reports[ch] for ch in c.children if ch in reports]
        if child_reports and _full_context_tokens(G, c) > max_tokens:
            context = _substituted_context(G, c, child_reports, [by_id[ch] for ch in c.children], max_tokens)
        else:
            context = build_community_context(G, c, max_tokens)
        reports[c.id] = generate_report(llm, context, c.id, c.level)
    return [reports[k] for k in sorted(reports)]

The report prompt, parsing and embedding

The prompt

minigraphrag/prompts.py keeps the structure of GraphRAG's report prompt and drops its long worked example to save tokens:

COMMUNITY_REPORT_PROMPT = """You are an AI assistant that helps a human analyst to perform general information discovery.

# Goal
Write a comprehensive report of a community, given a list of entities that belong to the community as well as their relationships and optional associated claims. The report will be used to inform decision-makers about the community and its potential impact.

# Report Structure
- TITLE: community's name that represents its key entities; short but specific.
- SUMMARY: an executive summary of the community's overall structure and how its entities relate.
- IMPACT SEVERITY RATING: a float score between 0-10 representing the importance of the community.
- RATING EXPLANATION: a single sentence explaining the rating.
- DETAILED FINDINGS: a list of 5-10 key insights. Each has a short summary and a multi-paragraph explanation grounded in the data.

Return output as a well-formed JSON-formatted string with the following format:
{{
    "title": <report_title>,
    "summary": <executive_summary>,
    "rating": <impact_severity_rating>,
    "rating_explanation": <rating_explanation>,
    "findings": [
        {{"summary": <insight_1_summary>, "explanation": <insight_1_explanation>}}
    ]
}}

# Grounding Rules
Points supported by data should list their data references as [Data: <dataset name> (record ids)], e.g. [Data: Entities (5, 7); Relationships (23)]. Do not list more than 5 record ids in a single reference. Do not include information for which no evidence is given.

# Real Data
Use the following text for your answer. Do not make anything up.

Text:
{input_text}

Output:
"""

Three prompt decisions have a large effect on quality. “Impact severity” comes from GraphRAG's original intelligence-analysis framing; for a product knowledge base, “importance to understanding the corpus” may rank communities more sensibly, and GraphRAG's auto prompt tuning rewrites this rating description for your domain (Chapter 28). The grounding rules are what make findings citable, and they only work if the ids in the context are the ones you can resolve later. And “Do not make anything up” is necessary but not sufficient: you still need to check.

Parsing

Report JSON fails in predictable ways: code fences around the object, a trailing sentence after it, a rating written as "7.5/10", findings as strings instead of objects. The parser extracts the first JSON object and coerces or drops what does not fit, so one bad field never loses the whole report.

@dataclass
class CommunityReport:
    community_id: int
    level: int
    title: str
    summary: str
    rating: float
    rating_explanation: str
    findings: list[dict] = field(default_factory=list)

    @property
    def full_content(self) -> str:
        """Markdown rendering: what gets embedded and put into search contexts."""
        lines = [f"# {self.title}", "", self.summary, ""]
        for f in self.findings:
            lines += [f"## {f.get('summary', '')}", "", f.get("explanation", ""), ""]
        return "\n".join(lines).strip()


def generate_report(llm: LLM, context: str, community_id: int = -1, level: int = 0) -> CommunityReport:
    """Ask the LLM for a JSON report and parse it defensively."""
    data = parse_json_response(llm.complete(P.COMMUNITY_REPORT_PROMPT.format(input_text=context), json=True))
    try:
        rating = float(data.get("rating", 0))
    except (TypeError, ValueError):
        rating = 0.0
    findings = [f for f in data.get("findings", []) if isinstance(f, dict)]
    return CommunityReport(community_id, level, str(data.get("title", f"Community {community_id}")),
                           str(data.get("summary", "")), rating, str(data.get("rating_explanation", "")), findings)

A rating of "7.5/10" becomes 0.0 here, silently. That is a reasonable default for a teaching implementation and a bad one for production, where it would sink an important community to the bottom of every ranking. Log parse fallbacks and consider retrying once with the parse error in the prompt.

Embedding reports

Global search can map over every report at a level, but several retrieval methods need to find relevant reports first: dynamic community selection, DRIFT's primer step (Chapter 15) and hybrid designs. So the indexer embeds each report's full_content into its own vector store, with the level as metadata:

report_store = _embed_store(embedder, [str(r.community_id) for r in reports],
                            [r.full_content for r in reports], [{"level": r.level} for r in reports])

Embedding the full markdown works well when reports are short. For long reports, a single vector averages many topics; embedding the title plus summary, or each finding separately with a pointer back to its report, gives sharper matches.

RAPTOR: summarizing chunks instead of communities

GraphRAG is not the only way to build hierarchical summaries. RAPTOR (Sarthi et al., 2024) builds a tree directly from text chunks: embed the chunks, cluster them (with Gaussian mixture models over dimensionality-reduced embeddings, which allows soft membership), summarize each cluster with an LLM, then embed and cluster the summaries, and recurse until the top. At query time it either traverses the tree from the root down or searches all nodes of all levels at once (the “collapsed tree”), which the authors found worked better.

On Kestrel the difference is concrete. RAPTOR would likely cluster the two Brightwater press releases together (similar vocabulary) and the two Tidewater Institute posts together. GraphRAG puts the Marlow acquisition article, the board memo and the Tern engineering blog into one community's evidence, because their entities are linked, even though the documents read very differently. Question 1, “Why did Kestrel Labs start Project Tern?”, needs that cross-document link.

Sarthi et al., RAPTOR: Recursive Abstractive Processing for Tree-Organized Retrieval (2024)

RAPTOR recursively embeds, clusters and summarizes chunks into a tree, and retrieves from multiple levels of abstraction. The authors report that retrieval over the tree improves results on question-answering tasks that require integrating information across long documents, and that pairing RAPTOR with GPT-4 improved the best reported accuracy on the QuALITY benchmark by 20% absolute. Chapter 6 places tree indexes alongside other graph shapes.

Cost and quality

What reports cost

The number of reports is the number of communities with at least min_size members, summed over all levels. With hierarchical Leiden and max_cluster_size = 10, a graph with N entities has at least about N/10 leaf communities, plus their ancestors. Each report call sends up to the input budget and receives up to the output budget.

A worked estimate: a corpus that produces 20,000 entities gives, say, 2,500 leaf communities and 600 communities at higher levels, about 3,100 reports. If the average context uses 5,000 of the 8,000 input tokens and reports average 1,200 output tokens, that is 3,100 × 5,000 ≈ 15.5 million input tokens and 3,100 × 1,200 ≈ 3.7 million output tokens. Output tokens are typically several times more expensive than input tokens, so reports are a meaningful slice of indexing cost, though usually smaller than extraction, which runs over every chunk with gleaning. Three levers matter most:

  • Skip tiny communities. A two-entity community rarely deserves a report; raising min_size to 3 or 4 can cut report count substantially on noisy graphs.
  • Cap the output. Ask for 5 findings rather than 10 at deep levels, where communities are small.
  • Defer. LazyGraphRAG and similar approaches (Chapter 26) postpone summarization to query time and only summarize what a query touches.

Checking data references is cheap and catches the most damaging problem. The ids a report may cite are exactly the ids in the tables it was given:

import re
from minigraphrag.context import parse_tables

REF = re.compile(r"\[Data:([^\]]*)\]")
PART = re.compile(r"(Entities|Relationships|Claims|Reports)\s*\(([^)]*)\)")

def validate_report(report, context: str) -> list[str]:
    """Problems with a report's data references and rating, given the context it was generated from."""
    tables = parse_tables(context)
    known = {name: {row.get("id", "").strip() for row in rows} for name, rows in tables.items()}
    problems = []
    if not 0 <= report.rating <= 10:
        problems.append(f"rating {report.rating} outside 0-10")
    for i, f in enumerate(report.findings):
        refs = REF.findall(f.get("explanation", ""))
        if not refs:
            problems.append(f"finding {i}: no data references")
        for ref in refs:
            for dataset, ids in PART.findall(ref):
                id_list = [x.strip() for x in ids.split(",") if x.strip() and x.strip() != "+more"]
                if len(id_list) > 5:
                    problems.append(f"finding {i}: more than 5 ids for {dataset}")
                missing = [x for x in id_list if x not in known.get(dataset, set())]
                if missing:
                    problems.append(f"finding {i}: {dataset} ids {missing} not in context")
    return problems

Run on the supply-chain report above against C2's context, this returns an empty list. Change one reference to Relationships (18), an edge that exists in the graph but not in C2's context, and it reports finding 0: Relationships ids ['18'] not in context. A model citing a real id it was never shown is a strong sign it is drawing on memory rather than the data.

C0's context: the Reports table holds C1 (5 nodes) then C2 (4 nodes); both summaries fit in 250 tokens. All 9 nodes are covered, so the remaining Entities table is empty, and every edge with both endpoints covered is removed. C0 has 14 edges: 5 inside C1, 4 inside C2, and 5 bridging edges, all dropped: Kestrel Labs–Priya Nair, Kestrel Labs–Deepcast, Kestrel Labs–Marlow Dynamics, Kestrel Labs–Project Tern and Jonas Vehl–Project Tern. The first nine are at least represented by the child reports; the five bridges appear in no context of any report. In this run the substituted context is only about 148 tokens: most of the 500-token budget goes unused.

Question 1, “Why did Kestrel Labs start Project Tern?”, suffers most. Its answer runs Marlow Dynamics → Deepcast → Kestrel Labs → Project Tern, and the Deepcast–Kestrel and Kestrel–Tern links are exactly the dropped bridges. Global search would see a leadership report and a supply-chain report without the sentence connecting them. (Local search, which reads the graph directly, is unaffected.)

from minigraphrag.communities import Community
from minigraphrag.context import format_table
from minigraphrag.reports import RELATIONSHIP_HEADER, _fit_rows, _relationship_rows, build_community_context

def substituted_context_with_bridges(G, community, child_reports, children, max_tokens):
    """Child reports first, then the relationships that cross between children, then leftovers."""
    sizes = {c.id: len(c.nodes) for c in children}
    ranked = sorted(child_reports, key=lambda r: -sizes.get(r.community_id, 0))
    rows = _fit_rows([[r.community_id, r.title, r.summary] for r in ranked], max_tokens // 2)
    used = {row[0] for row in rows}
    owner = {n: c.id for c in children if c.id in used for n in c.nodes}
    covered = set(owner)
    # Edges whose endpoints sit in two different summarized children: the story between the reports.
    bridges = [tuple(e) for e in community.edges
               if e[0] in covered and e[1] in covered and owner[e[0]] != owner[e[1]]]
    bridge_rows = _fit_rows(_relationship_rows(G, bridges), max_tokens // 4)
    remaining = Community(community.id, community.level, community.parent,
                          [n for n in community.nodes if n not in covered],
                          [e for e in community.edges if not (e[0] in covered and e[1] in covered)])
    return (format_table("Reports", ["id", "title", "summary"], rows) + "\n\n"
            + format_table("Bridging relationships", RELATIONSHIP_HEADER, bridge_rows) + "\n\n"
            + build_community_context(G, remaining, max_tokens // 4))

Wiring it in. generate_all_reports calls the module-level _substituted_context, so the least invasive option is a small copy of the loop in your own code that calls the new function, which also lets you add the retry-on-parse-failure and validation steps from this chapter. Monkeypatching minigraphrag.reports._substituted_context works for an experiment but couples you to a private name. The bridges table introduces a new dataset name, so extend the grounding rules in the prompt (“Bridging relationships” ids are cited as Relationships) and the validator's dataset map.

Check your understanding

Why does GraphRAG generate community reports at indexing time rather than summarizing communities when a question arrives?

  1. Because LLMs cannot summarize graphs at query time
  2. Because reports are needed to build the graph
  3. So that global questions become a map-reduce over a bounded set of prepared summaries instead of reading the whole corpus per query
  4. Because community detection is too slow to run at query time
Pre-computation moves the expensive summarization offline and amortizes it over all future global queries. Deferred alternatives exist (LazyGraphRAG), but they trade indexing cost for query cost.

In minigraphrag's report context, relationships are ranked by combined degree. What is combined degree?

  1. The number of chunks that mention the relationship
  2. The sum of the degrees of the relationship's two endpoints in the whole graph
  3. The summed extraction strength of the relationship
  4. The number of communities the relationship crosses
Combined degree favors relationships between central entities. Edge weight (summed strength) is a different attribute and is not used for this ranking.

A level-0 community's raw context is 30,000 tokens and the budget is 8,000. Its children already have reports. What does GraphRAG's context builder do?

  1. Splits the community again with Leiden
  2. Truncates the raw tables to the first 8,000 tokens
  3. Skips the report for that community
  4. Replaces sub-communities' raw rows with their reports, starting from the largest sub-community, until the context fits
Reports are generated bottom-up precisely so parents can reuse children's reports. Truncation is only the fallback for leaves that have no children.

A finding says “Deepcast was acquired for $200 million [Data: Relationships (5)]”, and relationship 5 is in the context but mentions no price. Which check catches this?

  1. Checking that cited ids exist in the context
  2. Checking that the rating is between 0 and 10
  3. Verifying the cited sentence against the text of the cited rows, for example with an LLM or NLI check
  4. Limiting references to five ids
The id is valid, so structural checks pass. Only a content check that compares the claim with the cited evidence can flag an unsupported detail.

What is the main difference between how RAPTOR and GraphRAG decide what gets summarized together?

  1. RAPTOR uses an LLM to group chunks; GraphRAG uses embeddings
  2. RAPTOR clusters chunks by embedding similarity; GraphRAG groups extracted entities by graph structure
  3. RAPTOR summarizes only the top level; GraphRAG summarizes only leaves
  4. There is no difference; both cluster entities
RAPTOR's clusters contain text that sounds alike; GraphRAG's communities contain entities that interact. That is why GraphRAG can put the acquisition article and the Tern blog in one community's evidence.

After adding the 2026 podcast to the corpus, the C1 report still calls Jonas Vehl the CTO. What is the most targeted fix?

  1. Regenerate every report in the index
  2. Increase the report output budget
  3. Lower the rating of C1
  4. Detect that C1's member descriptions changed (for example by a content hash), regenerate C1 and its ancestors, and include dated facts in the context
Stale reports come from changed inputs. Hashing member descriptions finds affected communities, ancestors must follow because they may substitute the child report, and dated claims keep the new report from repeating the old fact.

Questions learners often ask

Should reports include original text units as well as entity and relationship descriptions?

It can help specificity, since descriptions are already summaries. GraphRAG has a variant that builds report context from text units. The cost is budget: a few chunks consume what dozens of relationship rows would. A good compromise is to include the one or two chunks that contribute the most source ids to the community's top relationships.

What does the rating actually do?

It orders reports. Global search and dynamic community selection can prioritize or filter by it, and humans browsing an index start with the highest-rated communities. Treat it as a relative ranking signal generated with the same prompt for every community, not as a calibrated score, and adapt its definition (“impact severity”) to your domain.

Why do I get only one level of reports when I index the Kestrel corpus with minigraphrag?

With the default max_cluster_size = 10, no Kestrel community exceeds ten entities, so nothing is split and every community is level 0. The two-level hierarchy in this chapter uses max_cluster_size = 5. Also, the offline MockLLM extracts relationships from sentence co-occurrence, so its graph has more, noisier edges than the curated one here, and its communities differ (for example it groups Deepcast with Marlow Dynamics as a pair). Run with a real LLM to see communities closer to the running example.

Can I write reports in parallel?

Within a level, yes: communities at the same level don't depend on each other, so batch them concurrently up to your rate limit. Across levels, no: a parent may need its children's reports for substitution. GraphRAG processes level by level for exactly this reason.

Are community reports useful without global search?

Yes. Local search (Chapter 14) adds the reports of the communities its seed entities belong to, giving the model a zoomed-out view next to the entity details. They also make an excellent human-facing table of contents for a corpus, and a quick way to sanity-check what your extraction and clustering actually produced.

Key takeaways

  • A community report has a title, summary, rating, rating explanation and findings with data references; pre-computed reports are what make global questions tractable.
  • Report context is ranked tables: entities by degree, relationships by combined degree, plus claims, trimmed to an input budget. How you spend the budget changes what the report can say.
  • Reports are generated bottom-up; large parents substitute their children's reports for raw rows, largest children first.
  • Substitution can drop the relationships that connect sub-communities; keep bridging edges explicitly.
  • Parse defensively, validate every data reference against the context, and embed report content for retrieval.
  • RAPTOR summarizes clusters of similar chunks; GraphRAG summarizes communities of interacting entities. Pick by whether your questions are about topics or about actors and their relations.
