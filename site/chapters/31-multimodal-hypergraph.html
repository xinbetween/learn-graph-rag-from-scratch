Multimodal and hypergraph RAG

Real facts often tie together more than two things, and real documents are full of photos, tables and section structure. This chapter shows how to keep n-ary facts intact as hyperedges, how to store and search them in an ordinary property graph, and how to make images, tables and document structure first-class nodes.

You will be able to

  • Explain why binary edges lose n-ary facts, and compare edge properties, reification and hyperedges
  • Store a hypergraph as a bipartite graph of entity nodes and fact nodes, in NetworkX or Cypher
  • Extract n-ary facts with an LLM and retrieve them with embeddings plus Personalized PageRank
  • Model images, tables and table rows as graph nodes with cross-modal edges and provenance
  • Choose between captioning, shared image-text embeddings and late-interaction scoring for visual evidence
  • Describe what recent hypergraph, multimodal and document-structure GraphRAG systems add, and what they cost

Facts with more than two participants

Every graph you built so far has binary edges: one source, one target, a description. That fits “Lena Park is a partner at Brightwater Capital” perfectly. Now read the first sentence of the Series B press release in the Kestrel corpus:

Kestrel Labs today announced a $40 million Series B financing led by Brightwater Capital, which also led the company's Series A in 2022. Existing investors participated alongside two new infrastructure funds.

That sentence states one fact with at least five parts: a company (Kestrel Labs), a lead investor (Brightwater Capital), other participants (existing investors, two new infrastructure funds), an amount ($40M), a date (23 September 2025) and a round (Series B). The Series A press release has the same shape: $12M, led by Brightwater, with two regional angel groups, 15 March 2022. A fact that relates n things at once is called an n-ary fact.

What binary extraction does to it

The extractor from Chapter 8 can only emit pairs, so it splits the fact into edges such as BRIGHTWATER CAPITAL → KESTREL LABS (“led the Series B”), INFRASTRUCTURE FUNDS → KESTREL LABS (“invested in”), and a description on some edge that mentions $40M. Three things break:

  • Merging collapses the rounds. build_graph keys edges by their endpoint pair. Both rounds produce a Brightwater–Kestrel edge, so they land on one edge whose description list mixes “led the Series A” with “led the $40M Series B” and whose weight is the sum. Nothing structural says which amount belongs to which round.
  • Joins become ambiguous. If you model amounts and dates as nodes, Kestrel Labs has edges to $12M, $40M, March 2022 and September 2025. Pairing “amount” with “date” by walking through Kestrel gives 2 × 2 = 4 combinations, only 2 of which are real. Add the round name and the participant and it becomes 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 combinations with 2 true ones.
  • Participants lose their context. “Infrastructure funds invested in Kestrel Labs” is true, but the edge no longer says in which round, so “who joined Kestrel's cap table in 2025?” cannot be answered from structure.

Step through the visualization. The first three steps are the binary graph; the last three replace the tangle with two fact nodes, one per round, each connected to its participants by edges labeled with a role.

The graph from Chapter 8 already has one partial defense: extracting the event as an entity, as Exercise 8.1 did with the 2024 Halden Reef bleaching event. That is exactly the move this chapter makes systematic. A fact node is an event node you create on purpose, for every n-ary fact, with typed roles.

Hypergraphs and the bipartite trick

Definitions

A hypergraph H = (V, E) has vertices V and hyperedges E, where each hyperedge is any subset of V. The Series B fact is the hyperedge eB = {Kestrel Labs, Brightwater Capital, infrastructure funds, $40M, 2025-09-23}. Its size |eB| = 5 is called its arity or cardinality. An ordinary graph is the special case where every hyperedge has size 2.

The natural matrix for a hypergraph is the incidence matrix B, with one row per vertex and one column per hyperedge: Bv,e = 1 if v ∈ e, else 0. For the two Kestrel rounds (columns eA, eB) and eight vertices, Brightwater's row is [1, 1], the $40M row is [0, 1], and the column sums are the arities, 5 and 5. Vertex degree d(v) is a row sum: Brightwater and Kestrel have degree 2, everything else degree 1.

Two ways to flatten a hypergraph into a graph

Graph databases, NetworkX and every algorithm in this course expect pairwise edges, so you must turn hyperedges into ordinary edges. There are two standard expansions, and only one of them is lossless.

  • Clique expansion connects every pair of vertices inside each hyperedge. Its adjacency is A = B W BT − Dv, where W is the diagonal matrix of hyperedge weights and Dv the diagonal of weighted vertex degrees, subtracted to remove self-loops. A hyperedge of size k becomes k(k−1)/2 edges: 10 for the Series B. Worse, the Series A clique also contains the Brightwater–Kestrel pair, so the two cliques share an edge and you cannot tell afterwards which vertices formed which fact. Binary extraction produced the same blur.
  • Star expansion (also called the bipartite or incidence graph) adds one new node per hyperedge and connects it to each member. A hyperedge of size k becomes 1 node and k edges: 5 for the Series B. The graph is bipartite (entity nodes on one side, fact nodes on the other), its bi-adjacency matrix is exactly B, and the original hypergraph can be read back without loss.

Reification, events and hyperedges are the same idea

Knowledge-graph practitioners have handled this problem for decades under other names. In RDF, reification turns a statement into a resource so that you can say things about it. Wikidata attaches qualifiers (start time, point in time) to statements. Event-centric schemas model a funding round, an acquisition or an appointment as an event node. A “hyperedge stored as a fact node” is structurally identical to all of these. The differences are in emphasis:

In a property graph database

In Cypher the star expansion is a node label for facts and one relationship type with a role property. Literal values like amount and date usually become properties of the fact node rather than nodes of their own; making every date a node would create hub nodes that connect unrelated facts.

MERGE (k:Entity {name: "KESTREL LABS"})
MERGE (b:Entity {name: "BRIGHTWATER CAPITAL"})
MERGE (i:Entity {name: "INFRASTRUCTURE FUNDS"})
MERGE (f:Fact {id: "FACT:FUNDING_ROUND:KESTREL LABS:SERIES B"})
  SET f.type = "FUNDING_ROUND", f.round = "Series B",
      f.amount_usd = 40000000, f.date = date("2025-09-23"),
      f.description = "Kestrel Labs raised a $40 million Series B led by Brightwater Capital ...",
      f.source_ids = ["10_brightwater_series_b-000"]
MERGE (f)-[:HAS_ARG {role: "company"}]->(k)
MERGE (f)-[:HAS_ARG {role: "lead"}]->(b)
MERGE (f)-[:HAS_ARG {role: "participant"}]->(i);

// Which rounds did Brightwater lead, and who else joined each one?
MATCH (b:Entity {name: "BRIGHTWATER CAPITAL"})<-[:HAS_ARG {role: "lead"}]-(f:Fact {type: "FUNDING_ROUND"})
OPTIONAL MATCH (f)-[:HAS_ARG {role: "participant"}]->(p)
RETURN f.round, f.amount_usd, f.date, collect(p.name) AS participants
ORDER BY f.date;

The query walks two edges and cannot mix up rounds, because the amount, the date and the participants all hang off the same fact node. The same pattern works in Postgres with a facts table and a fact_args(fact_id, entity_id, role) table, which is the incidence matrix B stored as rows.

Hypergraph RAG: extraction and retrieval

Once facts are nodes, a GraphRAG pipeline changes in three places: the extractor emits facts with roles, the store holds a bipartite graph, and retrieval scores fact nodes as well as entities.

Luo et al., HyperGraphRAG (2025)

HyperGraphRAG argues that graph-based RAG is constrained by binary relations and represents n-ary relational facts as hyperedges. The method has three parts: knowledge hypergraph construction, retrieval and generation. The authors report experiments in medicine, agriculture, computer science and law, where it outperforms standard RAG and earlier graph-based RAG methods on answer accuracy, retrieval efficiency and generation quality.

Extracting n-ary facts

The extraction prompt asks for one self-contained sentence per fact plus a role map. The sentence matters as much as the roles: it is what you embed, and what the answer model reads. Literal values go into attributes, entity-valued arguments into roles. JSON output is the right choice here because roles are a nested map, which delimited tuples express badly (see the trade-off table in Chapter 8).

import networkx as nx
from minigraphrag.llm import parse_json_response

FACT_PROMPT = """-Goal-
Extract n-ary facts from the text. A fact is one event or statement that ties
together several participants, such as a funding round, an acquisition,
a contract or an appointment.

-Output-
Return JSON: {"facts": [{"type": ..., "text": ..., "roles": {...}, "attributes": {...}}]}
- type: one of FUNDING_ROUND, ACQUISITION, CONTRACT, APPOINTMENT, DETECTION
- text: one self-contained sentence stating the fact, with names in full
- roles: role name -> list of entity names (organizations, people, projects, places)
- attributes: literal values such as amount, date (YYYY-MM-DD or YYYY-MM), round
Use only information stated in the text. Omit roles and attributes you cannot fill.

-Example-
text: In April 2025 Marlow Dynamics acquired Deepcast.
output: {"facts": [{"type": "ACQUISITION", "text": "Marlow Dynamics acquired Deepcast in April 2025.",
  "roles": {"acquirer": ["Marlow Dynamics"], "target": ["Deepcast"]}, "attributes": {"date": "2025-04"}}]}

-Real data-
text: {input_text}
output:"""


def extract_facts(llm, unit) -> list[dict]:
    # str.replace, not str.format: the JSON example is full of braces.
    raw = llm.complete(FACT_PROMPT.replace("{input_text}", unit.text), json=True)
    facts = parse_json_response(raw).get("facts", [])
    for f in facts:
        f["source_id"] = unit.id
        f["roles"] = {r: [n.upper() for n in (names if isinstance(names, list) else [names])]
                      for r, names in f.get("roles", {}).items()}
    return [f for f in facts if f.get("type") and len(sum(f["roles"].values(), [])) >= 1]


def add_hyperedge(G: nx.Graph, fact_id: str, fact: dict) -> None:
    """Store one n-ary fact as a fact node plus one role-labelled edge per participant."""
    if fact_id not in G:
        G.add_node(fact_id, kind="fact", type=fact["type"], description=fact["text"],
                   attributes={}, source_ids=[])
    d = G.nodes[fact_id]
    d["attributes"].update(fact.get("attributes", {}))
    if fact.get("source_id") and fact["source_id"] not in d["source_ids"]:
        d["source_ids"].append(fact["source_id"])
    for role, names in fact["roles"].items():
        for name in names:
            if name not in G:
                G.add_node(name, kind="entity", type="", description="", source_ids=[])
            G.add_edge(fact_id, name, role=role)


def participants(G: nx.Graph, fact_id: str) -> list[tuple[str, str]]:
    return sorted((G.edges[fact_id, n]["role"], n) for n in G.neighbors(fact_id))


def facts_of(G: nx.Graph, entity: str, fact_type: str | None = None) -> list[str]:
    return [f for f in G.neighbors(entity)
            if G.nodes[f].get("kind") == "fact" and fact_type in (None, G.nodes[f]["type"])]

Two choices in this code are deliberate. Entity names are uppercased to match the node names build_graph produces, so fact nodes can be added to the same nx.Graph as your existing entity graph, and entity resolution from Chapter 9 can run over role arguments too. And kind distinguishes the two sides of the bipartite graph, which every downstream step needs.

Mixing fact nodes into the entity graph changes other algorithms

If you run Leiden or degree-based ranking on a graph that contains fact nodes, a busy entity's degree now counts facts rather than neighbors, and communities may form around fact nodes. Either run community detection on the entity-only projection (connect two entities if they share a fact) or keep facts in a separate layer that points into the entity graph.

Retrieving over hyperedges

A query can match a fact in two ways: its wording resembles the fact's sentence (“How much was the Series B?”), or it names participants (“Brightwater”). So retrieval seeds from both sides, then spreads relevance through the bipartite structure with Personalized PageRank (PPR) from Chapter 17:

r = (1 − α) s + α PT r

Here s is the normalized seed vector and P is the random-walk transition matrix of the bipartite graph (from any node, move to a uniformly random neighbor). A walk alternates sides: entity → fact → entity. Two bipartite steps equal one hypergraph step, whose transition matrix is PH = Dv−1 B W De−1 BT: pick one of your hyperedges, then a uniform member of it. A fact node that is similar to the query and connected to query entities collects mass from both.

from minigraphrag.embeddings import HashingEmbedder, cosine_top_k
from minigraphrag.search.ppr import personalized_pagerank   # power iteration, alpha = follow-edge probability
from hypergraph import participants


def hyper_search(G, query: str, embedder, k: int = 3, alpha: float = 0.85, top: int = 3) -> list[dict]:
    facts = [n for n, d in G.nodes(data=True) if d["kind"] == "fact"]
    ents = [n for n, d in G.nodes(data=True) if d["kind"] == "entity"]
    q = embedder.embed([query])[0]
    fact_vecs = embedder.embed([G.nodes[f]["description"] for f in facts])
    ent_vecs = embedder.embed([f"{n} {G.nodes[n]['description']}" for n in ents])

    seeds: dict[str, float] = {}
    for ids, vecs in ((facts, fact_vecs), (ents, ent_vecs)):      # dual seeding
        for i, score in cosine_top_k(q, vecs, k):
            if score > 0:
                seeds[ids[i]] = seeds.get(ids[i], 0.0) + score
    if not seeds:
        return []

    rank = personalized_pagerank(G, seeds, alpha)
    best = sorted(facts, key=lambda f: -rank[f])[:top]
    return [{"fact": f, "score": round(rank[f], 3), "text": G.nodes[f]["description"],
             "attributes": G.nodes[f]["attributes"], "roles": participants(G, f),
             "sources": G.nodes[f]["source_ids"]} for f in best]

On a graph holding just the two funding facts, with HashingEmbedder, this ranks the Series B fact first with a PPR score of about 0.265 and the Series A second at about 0.245. (These scores use one-sentence fact descriptions like the ones in Exercise 31.3; different wording moves them slightly.) The margin is small because both facts share two participants and the hashing embedder only sees word overlap. That is acceptable: each hit carries its full role map and attributes, so the answer model reads “amount_usd: 40000000, round: Series B” as a unit and has no structural path to pairing one round's date with the other round's amount. Here a correct structure matters more than a wide ranking margin.

For context construction (Chapter 22), render each fact as one row: the sentence, the roles, the attributes and the source ids. A fact table is typically shorter than the equivalent relationship table because one row replaces several edges.

Two lessons carry over to your own system. First, extraction quality limits everything downstream: an n-ary fact has more slots to get wrong than a pair, which is why self-consistency (sample several extractions, keep what agrees) fits well. Second, the incidence structure is itself a ranking signal, which both the PPR code above and HyCE-RAG's confidence propagation exploit.

Images, tables and figures as nodes

Kestrel's documents are text files, but the organizations behind them produce far more than text. Tidewater Institute's research note RN-2024-07 says the temperature, optical and acoustic data for the bleaching event are published as dataset HR-2024-08. A real version of that note would carry photos of the reef crest; a real Project Tern blog would carry a spec table. For this section, imagine two hypothetical additions to the corpus:

  • Figure 1 of RN-2024-07: a Sentinel drone photo of pale branching coral on the northern reef-crest transect, captioned “Onset of bleaching, August 2024”.
  • Table 1 of the Project Tern blog post: a two-row comparison of sonar modules. The Deepcast module: made by Deepcast, no longer available to Kestrel. The Tern module: built by Kestrel Labs, sea trials on two Sentinel drones in Q1 2026, phased fleet replacement Q2 to Q4 2026, designed to fit the existing hull. Every cell comes from sentences already in the blog post; only the table layout is invented.

A text-only pipeline sees neither. The photo has no words at all, and a table flattened into a chunk loses the header that tells you what each cell means.

A multimodal graph schema

The schema adds three node kinds and a handful of edge types:

  • Figure nodes store a reference to the image bytes, the caption, the page or bounding box it came from, a generated text description, and one or more embeddings.
  • Table nodes store the caption and header. Row nodes store the cells together with the header, so “Q1 2026” is always read as “sea trials: Q1 2026”. Rows are the right retrieval grain for most questions; whole tables are too big and single cells too small.
  • Cross-modal edges: DEPICTS (figure → entity), ABOUT (row → entity), MENTIONS (text unit → entity), and REFERS_TO (text unit → figure, when the prose says “see Figure 1”). Structural edges CONTAINS and NEXT link documents, sections and their children.

A table row is itself a small n-ary fact: “the Tern module, made by Kestrel Labs, sea trials Q1 2026, fleet replacement Q2–Q4 2026”. You can store rows with exactly the fact-node machinery from the previous section, with column names as roles or attributes.

Building it

The “describe in context” step does more for quality than any other. A vision-language model shown only the photo might write “underwater scene with white coral”. Shown the caption and the Detection section of RN-2024-07, it can write “Sentinel drone photo of paling branching coral on the Halden Reef northern crest transect at bleaching onset, August 2024”, which names three linkable entities: Project Sentinel, Halden Reef and the 2024 bleaching event.

Wu et al., Multi-granularity context-enhanced RAG over multimodal knowledge graphs (2026)

This work observes that multimodal KG pipelines usually process modalities independently before fusing them, so text context is underused when extracting knowledge from images. Its context-enhanced multimodal KG enriches each image with local context (sentences semantically related to the image, including surrounding text at several granularities) and global context (a summary of the whole passage). The authors report gains on a vision-centric dataset and across different multimodal-KG RAG methods.

Three ways to embed visual evidence

Once figures are nodes, you need a way to decide which figures match a query. There are three families, in increasing fidelity and cost.

  1. Textualize. Embed the caption plus generated description with your normal text embedder. Cheap, works with any vector store, and everything downstream stays text. It is only as good as the description: anything the vision model did not mention is invisible.
  2. Shared image-text embedding. Models trained contrastively on image-caption pairs (CLIP is the classic example) map an image and a text query to one vector each in a shared space. Retrieval is a single cosine, as in Chapter 2. One vector must summarize the whole image, so small details get averaged away.
  3. Late interaction (multi-vector). Keep one vector per query token and one per image patch. ColBERT introduced this for text and ColPali applied it to page images. The score is MaxSim: for each query token, take its best-matching patch, then sum.

score(q, d) = Σi ∈ query tokens maxj ∈ patches qi · dj

The visualization below uses tiny 4-dimensional vectors (axes: pale, coral, crest structure, open water) for three candidate photos. Switch between scoring modes and watch the ranking change. With a single mean-pooled vector, the healthy deep-reef photo wins because its every patch is “coral”. With MaxSim, the bleaching photo wins: one patch strongly matches “bleached” and another matches “crest”, and the two water patches cost it nothing.

The cost is storage and compute. With illustrative sizes of 1,000 patch vectors of 128 dimensions in float32, one page image needs 1,000 × 128 × 4 bytes = 512 KB, against 3 KB for a single 768-dimensional vector: about 170 times more. Multi-vector indexes are usually compressed or pooled to cut that cost, and a common pattern uses cheap single-vector search to shortlist candidates and late interaction only to rerank them.

Kim, ColGraphRAG (2026)

ColGraphRAG isolates exactly this choice inside a multimodal GraphRAG pipeline. It replaces only the ranking operator for graph-linked image nodes with MaxSim-style multi-vector scoring, keeping graph construction, text and table retrieval, extraction and reasoning unchanged. On MultimodalQA it reports better retrieval-stage estimates for image candidates and downstream QA gains, with larger movement where visual evidence matters most and mixed trends on text-dominant questions. The author frames this as mechanism-level evidence that calls for broader validation.

Retrieval across modalities on the Kestrel graph

With figure and row nodes linked into the entity graph, the local search from Chapter 14 works unchanged: seed entities, expand, collect evidence. The difference is that some evidence items are images or rows. Step through two questions.

He et al., HVM-GraphRAG (2026)

HVM-GraphRAG targets two weaknesses of multimodal GraphRAG on complex documents: unreliable cross-modal evidence indexing and expensive traversal. It uses a holistic view of the document to guide graph construction, which reduces noisy and conflicting graph updates, and builds indices from concept-level nodes to supporting multimodal chunks. At query time it searches a compact concept-level graph and jumps to evidence through that index instead of traversing a dense entity graph, then regroups retrieved chunks by modality before answering.

HVM-GraphRAG's index closely resembles the source_ids list on every node in minigraphrag: a small graph of concepts, each pointing at the chunks that support it. The multimodal version lets those chunks be images and tables. EvoGraph-R1, described earlier, works over a multimodal hypergraph, so it combines both halves of this chapter.

Document structure as a graph

Documents have a skeleton before you extract a single entity: titles, sections, paragraphs, figures, tables, footnotes and cross-references. The research note RN-2024-07 has sections Summary, Detection, Extent, Discussion, Recommendations and Data availability. A document-structure graph (sometimes called a lexical graph, see Chapter 6) makes that skeleton explicit: CONTAINS edges from document to section to paragraph, NEXT edges between consecutive paragraphs, and REFERS_TO edges for “see Table 1” or “as described in the Detection section”.

You already have an entity graph, but many questions are about where something is said rather than who is related to whom. “What did Tidewater recommend after the bleaching?” is answered by one section, Recommendations. Its three recommendations name only entities that appear all over the corpus (the Sentinel fleet, Halden Reef, Port Avalon), and nothing in the entity graph marks them as recommendations. Entity-anchored retrieval from “Tidewater” pulls in every Tidewater fact first; navigating document → section → paragraphs goes straight to the answer.

Xie et al., DocNavRAG (2026)

DocNavRAG argues that fixed GraphRAG traversal is too rigid and that agentic RAG over weakly structured interfaces keeps searching from scratch. It organizes document hierarchies and cross-region relations into a navigable graph, exposes graph operations for locating, navigating, expanding and fetching, and keeps an evolving evidence state that guides retrieval until enough evidence is collected. Across four long- and multi-document QA benchmarks it reports average improvements of 7.8% in answer quality and 17.7% in context sufficiency over the strongest baseline.

Financial filings are the domain where this structure is most regular. HC-RAG, covered in Chapter 32, builds a typed evidence graph of documents, sections, text units, table units and metadata, and routes queries along document–section–unit paths. Structure graphs and entity graphs are complementary: link them with MENTIONS edges and a query can enter through either.

Exercises

Exercise 31.1 Warm-up

Treat the two Kestrel funding rounds as hyperedges, with amounts and dates as vertices as in the first visualization: eA = {Kestrel, Brightwater, angel groups, $12M, Mar 2022} and eB = {Kestrel, Brightwater, infra funds, $40M, Sep 2025}. (a) How many nodes and edges does the star expansion have? (b) How many distinct edges does the clique expansion have? (c) Given only the clique expansion, can you recover eA and eB?

Show answer

(a) Vertices: Kestrel, Brightwater, angel groups, $12M, Mar 2022, infra funds, $40M, Sep 2025 = 8 entity nodes, plus 2 fact nodes = 10 nodes. Edges: one per membership, 5 + 5 = 10 edges.

(b) Each 5-member clique has 5 × 4 / 2 = 10 edges, so 20 in total, but the pair Kestrel–Brightwater appears in both, so there are 19 distinct edges (one of them with weight 2 if you count multiplicity).

(c) Not reliably. The clique expansion is two 5-cliques sharing an edge. You might guess the maximal cliques, but a different hypergraph produces the same graph: for example, ten separate binary facts covering the same pairs, or three-member hyperedges that happen to cover them. Maximal cliques are also not always the original hyperedges when hyperedges overlap in more than one pair. The star expansion is unambiguous because each fact node lists exactly its members.

Exercise 31.2 Core

Use the hypergraph random walk PH from the retrieval section on the hypergraph from Exercise 31.1: from a vertex, choose one of its hyperedges uniformly, then a uniform member of that hyperedge (which may be the vertex itself). Start with all probability on infra funds. Compute the distribution after one step and after two steps. Which vertex other than the start is most likely after two steps, and why does that matter for retrieval?

Hint

Infra funds belongs to one hyperedge. Kestrel and Brightwater belong to two, so from them the walk splits 50/50 between eA and eB.

Show answer

Step 1. Infra funds is only in eB, so the walk moves to eB and picks each of its 5 members with probability 0.2: Kestrel 0.2, Brightwater 0.2, infra funds 0.2, $40M 0.2, Sep 2025 0.2.

Step 2. Propagate each 0.2:

  • From infra funds, $40M and Sep 2025 (each in eB only): 3 × 0.2 = 0.6 goes to eB, spread 0.12 to each member.
  • From Kestrel: 0.1 to eA and 0.1 to eB, spread 0.02 to each member of each.
  • From Brightwater: the same, 0.02 to each member of eA and of eB.

So eB receives 0.6 + 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.8, giving each eB member 0.16; eA receives 0.2, giving each eA member 0.04. Kestrel and Brightwater are in both: 0.16 + 0.04 = 0.20 each. Infra funds, $40M and Sep 2025: 0.16 each. Angel groups, $12M and Mar 2022: 0.04 each. Check: 2 × 0.20 + 3 × 0.16 + 3 × 0.04 = 0.40 + 0.48 + 0.12 = 1.00.

Kestrel and Brightwater are most likely (0.20) because they sit in both hyperedges and collect mass from both. That is the familiar hub effect from PPR: shared participants rise. But the Series B's literal values ($40M, Sep 2025 at 0.16) stay four times more likely than the Series A's (0.04), so a walk seeded on the Series B side stays mostly inside its own fact. In a clique expansion, where Kestrel–Brightwater is a single shared edge, that separation would be weaker.

The Series B press release also mentions the Series A: “Brightwater Capital, which also led the company's Series A in 2022”. An extractor will produce a partial Series A fact from that chunk (lead, company, round, date “2022”, no amount) in addition to the full one from the Series A press release. Write fact_key(fact) and merge_fact(G, fact) so that both extractions become one fact node with the most specific date, both source ids, the fuller sentence, and a conflicts list when two values genuinely disagree.

import networkx as nx
from hypergraph import add_hyperedge, participants

def fact_key(fact: dict) -> str | None:
    """Identity of a fact, independent of which document mentioned it."""
    roles, attrs = fact["roles"], fact.get("attributes", {})
    if fact["type"] == "FUNDING_ROUND" and roles.get("company") and attrs.get("round"):
        return f"FACT:FUNDING_ROUND:{roles['company'][0]}:{attrs['round'].upper()}"
    return None

def merge_fact(G: nx.Graph, fact: dict) -> str:
    fact_id = fact_key(fact) or f"FACT:{fact['source_id']}:{G.number_of_nodes()}"
    if fact_id in G:
        d = G.nodes[fact_id]
        for k, new in fact.get("attributes", {}).items():
            old = d["attributes"].get(k)
            if old is None or str(new).startswith(str(old)):
                d["attributes"][k] = new                      # new value is missing or more specific
            elif not str(old).startswith(str(new)):
                d.setdefault("conflicts", []).append((k, old, new, fact["source_id"]))
        fact = {**fact, "attributes": {}}                    # attributes already merged above
        if len(fact.get("text", "")) <= len(d["description"]):
            fact["text"] = d["description"]                  # keep the most complete sentence
        d["description"] = fact["text"]
    add_hyperedge(G, fact_id, fact)
    return fact_id

G = nx.Graph()
partial_a = {"type": "FUNDING_ROUND", "source_id": "10_brightwater_series_b-000",
             "text": "Brightwater Capital led the Kestrel Labs Series A in 2022.",
             "roles": {"company": ["KESTREL LABS"], "lead": ["BRIGHTWATER CAPITAL"]},
             "attributes": {"round": "Series A", "date": "2022"}}
full_a = {"type": "FUNDING_ROUND", "source_id": "02_brightwater_series_a-000",
          "text": "Kestrel Labs raised a $12 million Series A led by Brightwater Capital on 15 March 2022.",
          "roles": {"company": ["KESTREL LABS"], "lead": ["BRIGHTWATER CAPITAL"],
                    "participant": ["REGIONAL ANGEL GROUPS"]},
          "attributes": {"round": "Series A", "amount_usd": 12_000_000, "date": "2022-03-15"}}
for f in (partial_a, full_a):
    merge_fact(G, f)

d = G.nodes["FACT:FUNDING_ROUND:KESTREL LABS:SERIES A"]
print(d["attributes"])   # {'round': 'Series A', 'date': '2022-03-15', 'amount_usd': 12000000}
print(d["source_ids"])   # ['10_brightwater_series_b-000', '02_brightwater_series_a-000']
print(d.get("conflicts"))  # None

Design notes. The key uses the company and round, not the lead investor, because a later chunk might omit or misname the lead. Date strings compare by prefix, so “2022” and “2022-03-15” are compatible and the more specific one wins, while “2021-11” vs “2022-03-15” would be logged as a conflict rather than silently overwritten. Facts without a usable key fall back to a per-source id: better an unmerged duplicate than a wrong merge. Real systems add more keys per fact type (acquirer + target for ACQUISITION) and run entity resolution on role arguments first so “Kestrel” and “Kestrel Labs” key identically.

Take the row maxima and sum them.

Photo A: bleached 0.85 (pale coral), coral 0.68 (pale coral), crest 0.99 (crest rock). Score = 0.85 + 0.68 + 0.99 = 2.52.

Photo B: bleached 0.19, coral 0.99, crest 0.93. Score = 0.19 + 0.99 + 0.93 = 2.11.

Photo A ranks higher by 0.41, almost entirely because of the “bleached” token (0.85 vs 0.19).

Mean pooling averages A's two water patches into its single vector, which drags it away from the query, while B's four coral-and-ridge patches all point roughly in the query's “coral” and “crest” directions, so B's pooled vector is closer to the pooled query (0.83 vs 0.62 cosine with these vectors). MaxSim lets each query token pick its own best patch, so irrelevant patches cost nothing and one strong local match counts in full.

Nodes. Document (id, title, date, publisher), Section (doc_id, heading, order), TextUnit (existing fields plus section_id), Figure (id, doc_id, section_id, page, bbox, image_path, caption, description, description_model, description_prompt_version, image_sha256), Table (id, doc_id, section_id, page, caption, header), Row (table_id, row_index, cells as a header-to-value map, rendered_text). Questions learners often ask

Is a hypergraph really different from adding event nodes to my knowledge graph?

Structurally, no. A hypergraph stored by star expansion is a bipartite graph of entities and event-like fact nodes. What the hypergraph framing adds is discipline and algorithms: you extract every multi-participant statement as a fact, you treat entity–fact incidence as the core structure, and you can use hypergraph walks, incidence-based propagation or hypergraph neural networks on it. If your schema already has well-designed event nodes, you have most of the benefit.

Should role names be free text or a fixed list?

Start with a small fixed list per fact type (company, lead, participant for funding rounds; acquirer, target for acquisitions) and let the extractor add an other role with a description. Free-text roles drift quickly (“lead investor”, “led by”, “primary backer”) and break Cypher queries. Normalize roles after parsing exactly as you normalize entity types in Chapter 8.

Do I still need binary relationships if I have fact nodes?

Usually yes. Many facts really are pairwise (“Lena Park is a partner at Brightwater Capital”), and binary edges make neighborhood expansion, community detection and path retrieval simple. A practical hybrid: keep the binary entity graph as is, add fact nodes for statements with three or more arguments or with dates and amounts, and derive the entity-only projection when an algorithm needs a plain graph.

When should I pass the image itself to the answer model instead of its description?

When the question depends on visual detail the description might have missed (extent of paling, reading a chart value) and your answer model is multimodal. Images cost many more input tokens than a sentence, so a common pattern is to retrieve with descriptions and embeddings, then include only the top one or two images as pixels alongside their captions, with the rest as text.

How do I index tables that span many rows?

Make one node per row, rendered with its header (“Module: Tern; Maker: Kestrel Labs; Sea trials: Q1 2026”), plus a table node holding the caption and a short summary. Retrieve rows, then include the header once and the matching rows in the context. For numeric questions over whole columns (sums, trends), route to a structured query over the table instead of asking the LLM to add numbers from text.

Key takeaways

  • N-ary facts such as funding rounds lose their grouping when split into binary edges, especially once facts share endpoints.
  • A hyperedge stored as a fact node with role edges (star expansion) is lossless and fits any property graph or relational store; clique expansion is not.
  • Hypergraph retrieval seeds on fact sentences and on entities, then spreads relevance through entity–fact incidence, for example with Personalized PageRank.
  • Images, tables and table rows become nodes with cross-modal edges and full provenance; contextual descriptions make them linkable and searchable.
  • Textual descriptions, shared image-text vectors and late-interaction MaxSim trade cost for visual fidelity; rerank with the expensive one.
  • Document-structure graphs complement entity graphs for questions about where something is said.
