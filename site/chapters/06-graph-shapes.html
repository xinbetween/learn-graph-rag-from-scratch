Graph shapes for retrieval

“Build a graph” is not a design. Before spending a single LLM call, decide what the nodes are, what the edges mean, and which questions the shape must answer. This chapter maps the design space, from free lexical graphs to LLM-built community hierarchies, with the build cost and retrieval patterns of each.

You will be able to

  • Describe eight graph shapes used for retrieval and draw each one for the Kestrel Labs corpus
  • Estimate the indexing cost of each shape in LLM calls and embeddings as a function of corpus size
  • Name the retrieval patterns each shape enables, such as window expansion, entity hops, Personalized PageRank, map-reduce over summaries and collapsed-tree search
  • Choose a shape, or a combination, from the question types your users actually ask
  • Build a lexical graph with PART_OF, NEXT and SIMILAR_TO edges in NetworkX from minigraphrag text units

Why shape comes before building

Chapter 3 showed three ways vector RAG fails on Kestrel Labs: multi-hop questions (“Why did Kestrel start Project Tern?”), global questions (“What are the main themes?”), and relationships that live between chunks rather than inside one. Chapters 4 and 5 gave you the tools: graph algorithms and knowledge-graph modeling. It is tempting to jump straight to “extract entities with an LLM”, because that is what Microsoft GraphRAG does. But that is one point in a larger design space, and not always the right one.

Every graph for retrieval answers three design questions:

  1. What is a node? A piece of the source text (document, section, chunk, sentence), a piece of extracted knowledge (entity, fact, event), or a piece of generated abstraction (summary, community report).
  2. What is an edge, and who creates it? Document structure (free and exact), embedding similarity (cheap and fuzzy), string matching or NER (cheap and shallow), or an LLM (expensive and descriptive).
  3. How will retrieval move? Expand a window, hop between entities, spread scores with PageRank, or read summaries top-down. A shape that does not support your retrieval pattern is dead weight.

The cost differences are large. For the Kestrel corpus (12 documents, about 5,400 words), a lexical graph takes zero LLM calls. An entity graph with one gleaning round takes about two extraction calls per chunk plus summarization. A GraphRAG-style community hierarchy adds one report call per community. At 12 documents none of this matters; at 100,000 documents it decides whether the project ships.

Shapes built from the text itself

(a) The lexical graph

A lexical graph represents the documents, not the world they describe. Nodes are documents, sections and chunks; edges record where text sits:

  • PART_OF: chunk → section → document. Sometimes called a parent-child structure.
  • NEXT: chunk → following chunk in reading order.
  • SIMILAR_TO (optional): chunk ↔ chunk when their embeddings are close, typically keeping the top k neighbors above a threshold.

The graphrag.com pattern catalog calls these Lexical Graph, Parent-Child Lexical Graph and Lexical Graph with Sibling Structure, and adds variants with a section hierarchy or with LLM-generated hypothetical questions attached to chunks.

What it is good at. Fixing the “chunk boundary” problem: a vector hit on the middle of an explanation can be widened to the previous and next chunks, or to its whole section. Navigating long, structured documents (manuals, contracts, filings) by section. Finding related passages in other documents through SIMILAR_TO without any LLM.

Build cost. Zero LLM calls. One embedding per chunk, which you already paid for in vector RAG, plus O(N·k) similarity edges if you add them. Computing exact top-k neighbors for every chunk is O(N²) dot products, which is fine for tens of thousands of chunks and needs an approximate nearest-neighbor index beyond that.

Retrieval patterns. Window expansion (hit ± n NEXT hops), parent-child retrieval (match small chunks for precision, return their larger parent for context), similarity hops (one SIMILAR_TO hop from each hit), and metadata filters by document date or source.

What it cannot do. It knows nothing about entities. Doc 07 never mentions Kestrel Labs, so no lexical edge connects the acquisition to Kestrel's decision, unless the embeddings happen to be close. Multi-hop questions over named things still fail.

(b) The entity graph extracted from text

This is the shape most people mean by GraphRAG. An LLM reads each chunk and emits entities and relationships (Chapter 8); duplicates are merged (Chapter 9); descriptions are summarized. The graphrag.com catalog calls a graph of real-world things a Domain Graph. Microsoft GraphRAG and LightRAG both build one, and each entity and relationship keeps the ids of the chunks it came from.

What it is good at. Multi-hop questions over named things: the Marlow → Deepcast → Kestrel → Tern chain becomes four edges. Aggregating everything said about one entity across documents into a single description. Community detection, which needs entity-level structure.

Build cost. N × (1 + g) extraction calls for N chunks and g gleaning rounds, plus summarization calls for entities and relationships with many descriptions. Output tokens are the expensive part: extraction writes long structured records. Quality depends on the extraction model, and errors (missed entities, invented relations, unmerged aliases) are baked into the index until you rebuild.

Retrieval patterns. Entity-anchored neighborhood expansion (local search, Chapter 14), relation matching by keywords (LightRAG, Chapter 16), path finding between query entities (Chapter 18).

(c) Lexical graph linked to entities

Put shapes (a) and (b) in one graph and connect them with MENTIONS edges from each chunk to the entities extracted from it. The graphrag.com catalog calls this Lexical Graph with Extracted Entities. It is what you get for free if you keep provenance ids from extraction: each extracted mention carries a source_id, build_graph collects them into a source_ids list on the node, and that list is the node's MENTIONS edges, stored inside out.

What it is good at. Everything (b) does, plus grounding: every fact can be traced to text, every retrieved entity can bring its best supporting chunks into the prompt, and vector hits on chunks can be expanded through entities. This is the shape minigraphrag builds, and local search's context (entities, relationships and source text units) depends on it.

Build cost. The same LLM calls as (b). The extra edges are bookkeeping.

Retrieval patterns. Chunk → entity → chunk expansion, entity-to-evidence ranking (which chunks mention the most retrieved entities), and graph-enhanced vector search: start from vector hits, then add chunks that share entities with them.

Default choice

If you are unsure, build (c). It degrades gracefully: with extraction turned off it is still a lexical graph, and with it on you can always drop the chunk layer from a query. Shapes that throw away the link to source text are hard to debug and cannot cite.

Shapes that add summaries

Global questions such as “What are the main themes in this corpus?” have no single answer passage. Neither chunks nor entities answer them; summaries do. Two families of shapes add summary nodes on top of the text, and they differ in what gets clustered.

(d) Tree indexes: RAPTOR and HiRAG

RAPTOR (Recursive Abstractive Processing for Tree-Organized Retrieval) builds a tree bottom-up directly from chunks, with no entity extraction at all:

At query time RAPTOR supports two modes. Tree traversal starts at the root layer, picks the most similar nodes, descends into their children, and repeats. Collapsed tree ignores the hierarchy and puts every node, leaves and summaries alike, into one pool, then takes the most similar nodes until the token budget is full. The paper found collapsed tree consistently performed better, because it lets each question pick its own mix of detail and abstraction.

What trees are good at. Questions that need to integrate information spread across a long document or many related passages, at a chosen level of detail: “How did Kestrel's supply situation evolve from 2022 to 2025?”. Corpora where entities are not the natural unit, like narrative text, meeting transcripts or opinion pieces.

Build cost. Embeddings for every chunk and summary, plus one LLM summary per cluster. If clusters average c members, layer sizes shrink roughly geometrically (N, N/c, N/c², …), so the total number of summaries is about N/(c − 1). Soft clustering adds some duplication. That is usually far fewer calls than per-chunk extraction, but each summary call has a long input. Updates are awkward: adding documents can change the clusters above them.

Retrieval patterns. Collapsed-tree search (top-k over all levels), tree traversal, and zoom: retrieve a summary, then pull its children for evidence.

HiRAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation with Hierarchical Knowledge) brings the tree idea to entity graphs. Starting from an LLM-extracted knowledge graph, it clusters entity embeddings with Gaussian mixture models, asks an LLM to write summary entities for each cluster, and repeats on the new layer, stopping when the clusters stop getting sparser (the paper uses a 5% threshold on the change in cluster sparsity). Retrieval then assembles three levels of context: local (descriptions of the entities matched to the query), global (reports for the communities those entities belong to) and bridge (the triples on paths that connect the local entities to their communities). It combines shapes (b), (d) and (e).

Sarthi et al., RAPTOR (2024)

RAPTOR recursively embeds, clusters and summarizes chunks into a tree, and retrieves across its levels. The paper reports substantial gains over retrieving only contiguous chunks on question answering that needs information integrated across a long document; with GPT-4 as the reader, it improves the best QuALITY accuracy by 20 points absolute. It also compares tree traversal with the collapsed tree and finds the collapsed tree better. Chapter 12 implements a RAPTOR-style tree next to GraphRAG's community reports so you can compare them on Kestrel.

(e) The community hierarchy

Microsoft GraphRAG's distinctive shape starts from the linked entity graph (c), runs hierarchical Leiden community detection over it, and asks an LLM to write a community report for every community at every level: a title, a summary, a rating of importance and a list of findings. The result is a hierarchy with entities at the bottom and reports above them. The graphrag.com catalog calls it Lexical Graph with Extracted Entities and Community Summaries.

For Kestrel Labs you would expect communities close to the running example's themes: leadership and Project Tern; reef science around Tidewater and Halden Reef; Marlow and Deepcast; Brightwater and Lena Park; the council and the Ocean Health Act. Their reports are exactly the raw material for “What are the main themes?”.

What it is good at. Global sensemaking over a whole corpus: themes, risks, “what is going on here”. Local questions too, because community reports can be added to entity-anchored context.

Build cost. Everything in (b), plus one report call per community across all levels. Reports have long inputs (entity and relationship tables) and long outputs. This is the most expensive shape in this chapter; LazyGraphRAG and other cost-cutting variants (Chapter 26) exist largely to avoid paying for it upfront.

Retrieval patterns. Map-reduce global search over reports at a chosen level (Chapter 15), dynamic community selection that walks the hierarchy top-down and prunes irrelevant branches, and DRIFT-style search that starts from community reports and follows up with local search.

Passage graphs, hypergraphs and curated graphs

(f) Passage graphs and knowledge-indexing graphs

A different family keeps passages as first-class nodes and uses entities mainly as connectors between them. The goal is a better index for finding passages; the graph is a means, never the product. Three examples show the range.

  • HippoRAG and HippoRAG 2. HippoRAG extracts open information-extraction triples into a phrase graph and ranks passages by running Personalized PageRank from the entities in the question (Chapter 17). HippoRAG 2 adds passage nodes to the graph, connected to the phrases extracted from them, keeps the synonym edges between phrases with similar embeddings, and seeds PageRank from both query-related phrases and passages.
  • LinearRAG. LinearRAG builds a relation-free “Tri-Graph” with three node types: entities, sentences and passages. Entities are found with a lightweight named-entity recognizer such as spaCy, not an LLM, and edges record which sentences mention and which passages contain each entity. There are no entity-to-entity relations at all. Retrieval first activates relevant entities by propagating query similarity through sentences (a semantic bridge from one entity to others that co-occur with it), then ranks passages with Personalized PageRank over the entity–passage graph. Its authors argue this avoids the noise of LLM relation extraction and makes indexing scale linearly with corpus size without LLM token cost.
  • KAG. KAG (Knowledge Augmented Generation) targets professional domains. Among its components is mutual indexing between a knowledge graph and the original chunks: graph elements point to the chunks they came from, and chunks point to the graph elements they contain, so reasoning can move between symbolic structure and text in both directions. It pairs this with a logical-form-guided hybrid reasoning engine.

What passage graphs are good at. Multi-hop passage retrieval at low indexing cost: the question's entities activate passages that share entities with them, several hops away. They produce ranked passages, which slot straight into an existing RAG prompt.

Build cost. Ranges from no LLM calls (NER plus embeddings, as in LinearRAG) to per-chunk LLM triple extraction (HippoRAG). No summaries.

Retrieval patterns. Personalized PageRank from query entities, score propagation along mention edges, entity-overlap reranking of vector hits. They are weak on global questions, since nothing summarizes the corpus.

(g) Hypergraphs

A binary edge connects two nodes. A hyperedge connects any number. Recall the Series B from Chapter 5: Kestrel Labs, Brightwater Capital, two new funds, $40 million, 23 September 2025. As binary edges it shatters into pairs that lose the fact that they are one event; as a hyperedge it stays whole. HyperGraphRAG, for example, extracts n-ary facts as hyperedges over the entities they involve and retrieves by matching both entities and hyperedges to the question.

Good at domains dense with multi-party facts: medicine (drug, dose, condition, outcome), law, finance. Build cost is similar to (b), with harder extraction. Retrieval matches hyperedges as units, and many graph algorithms need hypergraph-specific versions. Chapter 31 covers them properly; until then, the event-node pattern from Chapter 5 is the practical approximation in an ordinary graph.

(h) Existing curated knowledge graphs

Sometimes the graph already exists: Wikidata, a biomedical ontology, a product catalog, or an enterprise graph of customers, contracts and tickets. The shape is whatever its schema says, usually a typed, directed multigraph like Chapter 5's. Good at precise, structured and temporal questions (“Who is the CTO?”, “Which contracts renew in Q3?”). Build cost is zero for the graph, but you must link your documents to it: recognize mentions and resolve them to the graph's ids. Retrieval patterns are structured queries, often generated by an LLM (Text2Cypher, Chapter 20), and path reasoning over typed edges (Think-on-Graph, Chapter 18). Combined with a lexical graph via MENTIONS edges, a curated KG becomes shape (c) with an authoritative entity layer.

The shape you choose limits the answers you can get

No retriever can recover structure the index never stored. A lexical graph cannot answer Q2 reliably however clever the traversal; an entity graph without dates cannot answer Q6 without guessing. Write down your top question types first, and check each one against the table above before you build.

Most production systems combine shapes, and there is research on choosing per question. StructRAG, for instance, routes each task to the structure type best suited to it and converts documents into that structure at inference time. Benchmark studies of graph-based RAG, such as the unified comparison in DIGIMON, find that no single graph method wins on every dataset and question type. Treat shape as a per-workload decision and evaluate it (Chapter 25).

Building a lexical graph in NetworkX

Here is shape (a) over the Kestrel corpus, built from minigraphrag's real chunking and embedding modules. A MultiDiGraph allows a NEXT edge and a SIMILAR_TO edge between the same two chunks, and keys each edge by type.

import networkx as nx

from minigraphrag.chunking import TextUnit, chunk_documents, load_documents
from minigraphrag.embeddings import HashingEmbedder, cosine_top_k


def doc_title(text: str, doc_id: str) -> str:
    first = text.lstrip().splitlines()[0] if text.strip() else ""
    return first.lstrip("# ").strip() or doc_id


def build_lexical_graph(docs: dict[str, str], units: list[TextUnit], embedder=None,
                        k: int = 3, min_similarity: float = 0.3) -> nx.MultiDiGraph:
    """Documents and chunks as nodes; PART_OF, NEXT and SIMILAR_TO as edges."""
    G = nx.MultiDiGraph()
    for doc_id, text in docs.items():
        G.add_node(doc_id, kind="document", title=doc_title(text, doc_id))

    by_doc: dict[str, list[TextUnit]] = {}
    for u in units:
        G.add_node(u.id, kind="chunk", doc_id=u.doc_id, text=u.text,
                   n_tokens=u.n_tokens, start=u.start, end=u.end)
        G.add_edge(u.id, u.doc_id, key="PART_OF", type="PART_OF")
        by_doc.setdefault(u.doc_id, []).append(u)

    for doc_units in by_doc.values():
        doc_units.sort(key=lambda u: u.start)             # reading order
        for a, b in zip(doc_units, doc_units[1:]):
            G.add_edge(a.id, b.id, key="NEXT", type="NEXT")

    if embedder is not None and units:
        vectors = embedder.embed([u.text for u in units])
        for i, u in enumerate(units):
            for j, score in cosine_top_k(vectors[i], vectors, k=k + 1):  # +1: the chunk itself
                v = units[j]
                if j == i or v.doc_id == u.doc_id or score < min_similarity:
                    continue                               # skip self, same-document pairs and weak links
                G.add_edge(u.id, v.id, key="SIMILAR_TO", type="SIMILAR_TO", score=round(score, 3))
    return G


if __name__ == "__main__":
    docs = load_documents("data/corpus")
    units = chunk_documents(docs, size=300, overlap=50)
    G = build_lexical_graph(docs, units, HashingEmbedder(), k=3, min_similarity=0.3)

    kinds = nx.get_node_attributes(G, "kind")
    print(sum(1 for k in kinds.values() if k == "document"), "documents,",
          sum(1 for k in kinds.values() if k == "chunk"), "chunks")
    for t in ("PART_OF", "NEXT", "SIMILAR_TO"):
        print(t, sum(1 for *_, d in G.edges(data=True) if d["type"] == t))

    # Retrieval pattern: expand a hit to its neighbors in reading order
    hit = "07_marlow_acquires_deepcast-001"
    window = [u for u, _, d in G.in_edges(hit, data=True) if d["type"] == "NEXT"] + [hit] + \
             [v for _, v, d in G.out_edges(hit, data=True) if d["type"] == "NEXT"]
    print("window:", window)
    print("similar:", [(v, d["score"]) for _, v, d in G.out_edges(hit, data=True) if d["type"] == "SIMILAR_TO"])

Read the numbers. Each document is short enough to become two 300-word chunks, so there are 24 chunks, 24 PART_OF edges and one NEXT edge per document. The 46 directed SIMILAR_TO edges come from 39 distinct chunk pairs; some pairs chose each other. The strongest pairs are what you would hope for: the Tidewater launch post and the bleaching research note, the Series A and Series B press releases. The chunk about Marlow's acquisition links to the 2022 Deepcast supply agreement, the start of the story it ends.

These similarities are lexical, not semantic

HashingEmbedder hashes words into buckets, so it measures word overlap. It is deterministic and offline, which makes it good for tests. Swap in SentenceTransformerEmbedder or OpenAIEmbedder and the scores, the right threshold and some of the links will change; always re-tune min_similarity for the embedder you use, for example by looking at the score distribution and keeping the top few percent of pairs.

Design decisions in that code

  • Skipping same-document similarity. Chunks of one document are already connected by NEXT and PART_OF, and they would otherwise dominate every chunk's top-k. Cross-document links are the ones structure cannot give you.
  • Top-k plus a threshold. Top-k alone links every chunk to something, even when nothing is related. A threshold alone gives hub chunks (boilerplate, “About Kestrel Labs” paragraphs) hundreds of edges. Use both.
  • Edges keyed by type. Retrieval code filters by type constantly (“follow NEXT only”). Keying makes that explicit and prevents a SIMILAR_TO edge from overwriting a NEXT edge.
  • Text stored on nodes. Fine for 24 chunks. At scale, store text in a document store or Parquet and keep only ids and offsets in the graph (Chapter 13).

from collections import Counter

def add_mentions(G, mentions: dict[str, list[str]]):
    for unit_id, names in mentions.items():
        for name in names:
            if name not in G:
                G.add_node(name, kind="entity")
            G.add_edge(unit_id, name, key="MENTIONS", type="MENTIONS")
    return G

def expand_via_entities(G, hit_unit_ids, max_units=5):
    hits = set(hit_unit_ids)
    shared = Counter()
    for hit in hits:
        entities = [e for _, e, d in G.out_edges(hit, data=True) if d["type"] == "MENTIONS"]
        for e in entities:
            for unit, _, d in G.in_edges(e, data=True):
                if d["type"] == "MENTIONS" and unit not in hits:
                    shared[unit] += 1
    return shared.most_common(max_units)

Result: [("03_…-000", 2), ("05_…-000", 2), ("02_…-000", 1), ("07_…-000", 1)]. Ties keep first-counted order in Counter.most_common, so the order among ties depends on the order of MENTIONS edges, not on relevance. Notice the problem the hub creates: the Series A chunk scores the same as the acquisition article only because it mentions Kestrel Labs, which appears nearly everywhere. A better score divides each shared entity by how many chunks mention it (an IDF weight): KESTREL LABS would count 1/5, DEEPCAST 1/3, and the acquisition article (1/3) would outrank the Series A release (1/5). This is the hub problem from Chapter 4 showing up in retrieval.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture

from minigraphrag.chunking import chunk_documents, load_documents
from minigraphrag.embeddings import HashingEmbedder, cosine_top_k
from minigraphrag.tokens import count_tokens

def fit_gmm(X, max_k=8, seed=0):
    best, best_bic = None, np.inf
    for k in range(2, min(max_k, len(X) - 1) + 1):
        gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=k, covariance_type="diag", random_state=seed).fit(X)
        bic = gm.bic(X)
        if bic < best_bic:
            best, best_bic = gm, bic
    return best

def build_tree(texts, embedder, threshold=0.2, max_k=8):
    nodes = [{"text": t, "level": 0, "children": []} for t in texts]
    layer = list(range(len(nodes)))
    level = 0
    while len(layer) > 3:
        X = embedder.embed([nodes[i]["text"] for i in layer])
        gm = fit_gmm(X, max_k)
        probs = gm.predict_proba(X)
        level += 1
        new_layer = []
        for c in range(gm.n_components):
            members = [layer[i] for i in range(len(layer)) if probs[i, c] >= threshold]  # soft membership
            if not members:
                continue
            summary = " ".join(nodes[m]["text"].split(". ")[0] + "." for m in members)
            nodes.append({"text": summary, "level": level, "children": members})
            new_layer.append(len(nodes) - 1)
        if len(new_layer) >= len(layer):
            break                                    # not shrinking: stop
        layer = new_layer
    return nodes

def collapsed_tree_search(query, nodes, embedder, budget=1000):
    M = embedder.embed([n["text"] for n in nodes])
    q = embedder.embed([query])[0]
    picked, used = [], 0
    for i, _ in cosine_top_k(q, M, k=len(nodes)):
        t = count_tokens(nodes[i]["text"])
        if used + t > budget:
            continue
        picked.append(i)
        used += t
    return picked

docs = load_documents("data/corpus")
units = chunk_documents(docs, size=100, overlap=0)
emb = HashingEmbedder()
tree = build_tree([u.text for u in units], emb)
picked = collapsed_tree_search("What are the main themes?", tree, emb)
print(sum(tree[i]["level"] > 0 for i in picked), "summaries,", sum(tree[i]["level"] == 0 for i in picked), "leaves")

With a hashing embedder, expect the selection to be dominated by leaves, and the result to vary with the seed. Two reasons. First, “What are the main themes?” shares almost no words with any node, so lexical similarity is close to noise; the ranking reflects accidents of word overlap. Second, the fake summaries are long concatenations, so each consumes a large part of the budget. With a real embedder and real LLM summaries, abstract questions tend to match higher-level summaries, which is the behavior the RAPTOR paper relies on. The exercise's point is to see both mechanics (BIC model selection, soft membership, budgeted collapsed search) and why they depend on semantic embeddings and genuine abstraction.

Questions learners often ask

Is a lexical graph really “GraphRAG”?

By the broad definition used in the surveys, any retrieval that uses graph structure counts, and window expansion and similarity hops are graph traversals. The label matters less than the capability: a lexical graph fixes boundary and neighboring-passage problems and nothing more. It is a good baseline to beat, and a layer every other shape benefits from.

Can I start with one shape and add another later?

Yes, if you keep stable chunk ids (Chapter 7). A lexical graph can gain entities by adding MENTIONS edges; an entity graph can gain communities and reports on top. What is hard to add later is anything that needed information you discarded, such as offsets into the source text or the dates of documents.

Should chunks and entities live in the same database?

It is convenient, and graph databases handle it well (the graphrag.com patterns assume one graph). But it is not required: many systems keep chunk text in a document or vector store and only ids in the graph, joined by id at query time. The shape is a logical design; storage is a separate decision covered in Chapter 13.

Which shape gives the best accuracy?

No shape wins everywhere. Comparative studies of graph-based RAG methods report that results depend strongly on the dataset and question type, and vector RAG remains strong on single-hop lookups. Pick candidates with the decision table, build the cheapest one that could work, and evaluate on your own questions before paying for more structure.

Where do hypothetical questions fit?

The graphrag.com catalog includes a lexical graph variant where an LLM generates questions each chunk can answer, stored as nodes linked to the chunk. Queries are matched against those questions instead of the raw text, which often matches user phrasing better. It adds one LLM call per chunk, so it sits between shapes (a) and (b) in cost.

Key takeaways

  • A retrieval graph is defined by what its nodes are, who creates its edges, and how retrieval moves over it; decide these before building.
  • Lexical graphs cost no LLM calls and fix boundary and neighboring-passage problems, but know nothing about entities.
  • Entity graphs make multi-hop chains explicit; linking them to chunks with MENTIONS edges adds grounding and citations for free, which makes the hybrid a strong default.
  • Tree indexes (RAPTOR) and community hierarchies (GraphRAG) add summaries for broad questions; they differ in clustering embeddings versus graph structure.
  • Passage graphs such as HippoRAG 2 and LinearRAG use entities as connectors to rank passages, sometimes with no LLM at indexing time.
  • Hypergraphs keep n-ary facts whole; curated KGs give precise, typed, temporal answers if you can link your text to them.
  • Match shapes to question types and cost, and expect to combine shapes.
