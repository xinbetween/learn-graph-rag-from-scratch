Where vector RAG breaks

The baseline from Chapter 2 answers “who supplied the sonar?” well. This chapter runs the harder canonical questions through the same pipeline, shows chunk by chunk what retrieval returns and what it misses, and names each failure precisely enough that the rest of the course can fix it.

You will be able to

  • Trace a multi-hop question through vector retrieval and identify the missing bridge evidence
  • Explain why global “sensemaking” questions have no good nearest neighbor, using the query-focused summarization framing
  • Recognize alias, split-fact, staleness and dilution failures in retrieved context
  • Implement iterative retrieval with follow-up queries and explain why it is only a partial fix
  • Map each failure type to the GraphRAG technique and chapter that addresses it

The setup: same index, harder questions

Every number in this chapter comes from the Chapter 2 baseline, unchanged: the 12 Kestrel documents cut into 24 text units of 300 words with 50 words of overlap, embedded with HashingEmbedder, searched with exact cosine top-k, k = 5 unless stated. You can reproduce all of them with build_vector_index from Chapter 2 and store.search.

Because the hashing embedder only sees shared words, you might suspect the failures are just a weak embedder. Some are. The important ones are not, and for each failure this chapter separates the part a better embedding model would fix from the part it structurally cannot. The test is simple: does any single chunk contain what the question needs, in words or meaning close to the question? If not, no similarity function over single chunks can rank the right evidence first.

Here is the retrieval footprint of the seven canonical questions at a glance. Each row is a question; each column is a document. The supporting documents come from data/questions.jsonl.

Multi-hop questions: the evidence does not look like the question

Question 1: “Why did Kestrel Labs start Project Tern?”

The answer is a chain of three facts from three documents:

  1. Marlow Dynamics acquired Deepcast in April 2025 and ended outside supply contracts (document 07, a trade-press article).
  2. Deepcast stopped supplying Kestrel Labs: “Deepcast will not renew our supply agreement” (document 08, the board memo).
  3. Kestrel built its own sonar: “Project Tern will replace the Deepcast sonar in Project Sentinel” (document 09, the engineering blog).

A capable model given this context will write something like “Kestrel started Project Tern to stop depending on an outside sonar supplier after Deepcast units became unavailable.” That is fluent, cited and missing the actual cause. It is the worst kind of wrong answer: a plausible partial one.

Why the missing chunks score so low

After stopword removal the question is five words: kestrel, labs, start, project, tern. The Marlow article shares exactly one of them, labs, and only by accident: it never mentions Kestrel Labs or Project Tern at all. It is a story about a defense contractor buying a sonar maker. The board memo does share kestrel, labs, start, project, but its first chunk is 300 words of supply-chain detail, so those four words are a small part of its vector. It also never names the buyer: it says “Deepcast's change of ownership” and “the new owner”.

A neural embedder would rank the memo higher, since “sonar supply disruption and proposed response” is semantically close to “why start an in-house sonar project”. It would not rescue the Marlow article, because that article is not about Kestrel, Tern or why anyone started anything. The evidence for hop 1 resembles the evidence for hop 2, not the question. The link between them is a shared entity, Deepcast, and cosine similarity between the question and each chunk has no way to follow a shared entity.

Larson and Truitt, the GraphRAG announcement (2024)

The blog post that introduced GraphRAG named this failure directly: baseline RAG struggles to connect the dots when answering requires traversing disparate pieces of information through their shared attributes to produce new synthesized insights. The chain Marlow → Deepcast → Kestrel → Tern is that kind of traversal.

Question 2: a fragile success

“Which investor's board member oversees the company whose drones detected the Halden Reef bleaching?” needs three hops: the bleaching was detected by Sentinel drones (document 06), Kestrel Labs runs Sentinel (document 04), and Lena Park of Brightwater Capital sits on Kestrel's board (document 02). Top-5 retrieval covers all three facts anyway: the research note ranks 1st, the Sentinel launch post 2nd, and at rank 4 the Series B press release contributes “Lena Park, partner at Brightwater Capital and a member of the Kestrel Labs board”, a sentence that sits in the same chunk as a mention of the 2024 bleaching event.

That is luck, not capability. Remove the Series B press release from the corpus and rerun the query: the top 5 become the research note, the launch post, Priya Nair's profile, the founding article and the research note again. The Series A release, the only other source of the board fact, ranks 11th. Nothing about the question changed; a redundant document disappeared. Vector RAG answers multi-hop questions reliably only when some document already states the connection in one place.

Question 3: someone already connected the dots

“What connects Priya Nair to Deepcast?” is answered almost entirely by the top-ranked chunk, the first part of the 2026 leadership interview. In it the host says Priya “joined in 2023 from Marlow Dynamics”, and Mira Okafor says “Marlow Dynamics bought Deepcast last year”. The Marlow article ranks 3rd. The retrieved context supports the chain Priya → Marlow → Deepcast. What is missing is the second path, Priya co-leads Project Tern, which replaces Deepcast sonar: the Tern blog ranks 7th.

The lesson generalizes. On real corpora, multi-hop questions often seem to work in demos because someone wrote a summary, an FAQ or an interview that connects the facts. The questions your users care about most are frequently the ones nobody has written up yet.

Global questions: no chunk is about the whole corpus

Question 4: “What are the main themes in this corpus?”

This question has no nearest neighbor. Its content words after tokenization are main, theme, corpu (the crude plural stripper eats the s of “corpus”). The best chunk scores 0.078; from rank 6 on, every chunk scores zero or slightly below. The “top 5” is noise: the Series A release twice, Priya Nair's profile, the Deepcast supply deal and the bleaching note, chosen because a word like “main” happens to appear.

A better embedder produces nonzero scores and a different noisy top 5, but it cannot fix the underlying problem. The reference answer names four themes: reef science and monitoring; supply-chain risk and vertical integration; funding and growth; regulation creating demand. Evidence for them is spread over eight documents. No single chunk is about “themes”, and five chunks cannot cover eight documents. Even with perfect ranking, the model would see a sample and summarize the sample.

Edge et al., From Local to Global (2024)

The GraphRAG paper frames questions like “What are the main themes in the dataset?” as query-focused summarization (QFS) over an entire corpus, a sensemaking task rather than a lookup. It argues that RAG fails on such global questions because they are not answered by retrieving a few relevant records, and proposes community summaries built at indexing time and combined with map-reduce at query time. You will build that pipeline in Chapters 11, 12 and 15.

Question 5: “What risks does Kestrel Labs face?”

This one sits between local and global. There is a chunk about risk, and retrieval finds it: the 2022 Deepcast supply deal (rank 4) quotes an analyst on single-source components. But the full answer is spread across four documents written over four years: that 2022 analysis, the Marlow acquisition in 2025 (rank 19), the board memo calling supply “the most serious operational risk” (rank 9), and the 2026 interview in which the CEO lists execution risk on Tern, competition from Marlow, dependence on the Ocean Health Act and concentration on one reef and one partner (rank 10). Top-5 context yields a one-risk answer. The chunks that list the most risks rank 9th and 10th because the question's other words, “Kestrel Labs”, are everywhere in the corpus and dominate the similarity.

Lost relationships: aliases, split facts, stale facts and dilution

Aliases and indirect references

The same entity appears under different surface forms, and some references never use a name at all:

  • The bleaching research note says “the Sentinel program” and “Sentinel drones”, never “Project Sentinel” and never “Kestrel Labs”.
  • The council minutes call Tidewater Institute “TWI” and Kestrel's CEO “the vendor's CEO”.
  • The board memo calls Marlow Dynamics “the new owner”. No embedding model can resolve that without reading another document.
  • The 2026 interview says “Tern”, “Okafor”, “Vehl” and “Nair”.

Neural embedders handle easy aliases (“TWI” near “Tidewater” may or may not work, depending on the model). Coreference across documents, such as “the new owner” meaning Marlow Dynamics, needs the fact to be resolved once, at indexing time, and stored. That is what entity extraction and resolution do in Chapters 8 and 9.

Facts split across chunk boundaries

With 120-word chunks, the Series B press release is cut in the middle of Okafor's quote. Chunk 10_brightwater_series_b-002 ends mid-sentence: “Project Tern, our in-house sonar program, is the”. Chunk -003 starts 20 words earlier, at “outside supplier for the most important part of our drones,” and carries the quote through “is the answer to that”, but it has lost the opening “Spring 2025 taught us that we cannot depend on a single”. One vector holds the cause without the conclusion; the other holds the conclusion without its time anchor. And neither names the supplier, which appears only in other documents. Overlap protects short facts; it cannot protect relationships that span more than the overlap.

The effect on question 1 is stark. Count a fact as present when any retrieved chunk states it, whichever document it comes from:

At 300 words, k = 3 already gives two facts, both from the Tern blog's first chunk: Tern replaces the Deepcast sonar, and Deepcast units are no longer available. The third arrives at k = 10, and not from the acquisition article, which is still 21st. It comes from the 2026 interview at rank 6, where Okafor mentions in passing that “Marlow Dynamics bought Deepcast last year”. At 120 words, even ten chunks contain none of the three: the top ranks go to fragments of the Series B and Series A releases, the Sentinel launch post and Priya Nair's profile, which repeat “Kestrel Labs” and “Project”, plus the Tern blog's first fragment, which announces the project and stops before the sentence about Deepcast (that sentence is in the blog's third fragment, at rank 18). Smaller chunks sharpen single-fact matching and shred multi-fact reasoning.

Stale facts

Question 6, “Who is the CTO of Kestrel Labs?”, retrieves the 2026 interview chunk at rank 5, which says Priya Nair has been CTO since March 2026. At rank 6 comes the 2022 supply deal article, which calls Jonas Vehl the chief technology officer, and the 2021 founding article and the 2025 board memo say the same. Cosine similarity has no notion of time: an old statement of a fact scores as high as a new one, and more old statements mean more chances to outrank the new one. With k = 5 a careful model reads the datelines carried by each document's first chunk and answers correctly. With a slightly different embedder, or k = 6 and a less careful model, it answers “Jonas Vehl”. Question 15 in questions.jsonl, “Who was the CTO when the bleaching event was detected?”, needs time-aware reasoning on purpose. Temporal facts are the subject of Chapter 10 and Chapter 27.

Top-k dilution

Two kinds of noise crowd the top-k. Redundancy: overlapping chunks of the same document, like the two Series B chunks at ranks 2 and 3 for question 7, spend two slots on one piece of evidence. Hub words: “Kestrel Labs” appears in almost every document, so any question that names Kestrel rewards chunks that repeat the name, which is why the Series A boilerplate keeps appearing in the top 5 for questions 1, 5 and 6.

The instinctive fix is a bigger k. Chapter 2's smoke test showed multi-hop recall rising from 0.60 at k = 5 to 0.88 at k = 10. But on this corpus k = 10 means reading 42% of all chunks, precision drops, the evidence that matters lands in the middle of a long context, and on a corpus of 100,000 chunks the same k would still be a tiny sample. Raising k converts a retrieval problem into a long-context problem; it does not solve it.

Partial fixes: rewriting, decomposition and iterative retrieval

Before graphs, the RAG community attacked multi-hop failures by making retrieval a loop. Self-Ask (Press et al., 2022) prompts the model to ask itself follow-up questions. IRCoT (Trivedi et al., 2022) interleaves retrieval with chain-of-thought, using each reasoning step as the next query. ReAct (Yao et al., 2022) generalizes this to tool use. The shared idea: read what you retrieved, notice the bridge entity, and search for it.

The numbers in that animation are real hashing-embedder results. Iterative retrieval works here. A reference implementation looks like this:

from minigraphrag.context import format_table
from minigraphrag.search.common import generate_answer

FOLLOW_UP_PROMPT = """You are answering a question by searching a document collection one query at a time.

Question: {question}

Evidence so far:
{evidence}

If the evidence fully answers the question, reply with exactly DONE.
Otherwise reply with ONE short search query for the most important missing fact.
Use specific names that appear in the evidence."""

def iterative_search(index, question: str, llm, max_hops: int = 3, k: int = 3):
    seen: list[str] = []
    query, calls = question, 0
    for hop in range(max_hops):
        qvec = index.embedder.embed([query])[0]
        for unit_id, _, _ in index.text_unit_store.search(qvec, k=k):
            if unit_id not in seen:
                seen.append(unit_id)
        if hop == max_hops - 1:
            break
        evidence = "\n---\n".join(index.text_units[u].text[:800] for u in seen)
        reply = llm.complete(FOLLOW_UP_PROMPT.format(question=question, evidence=evidence)).strip()
        calls += 1
        if not reply or reply.upper().startswith("DONE"):
            break
        query = reply.splitlines()[0]
    context = format_table("Sources", ["id", "text"], [[u, index.text_units[u].text] for u in seen])
    return generate_answer(llm, question, context), seen, calls + 1

This needs a real model: MockLLM does not recognize the follow-up prompt and replies OK, which becomes a useless query. Exercise 3.3 shows how to test the loop offline with scripted follow-ups.

Why iterative retrieval is only a partial fix

LimitationWhat happens on Kestrel
The model must spot the bridge entity in raw textHop 1 must surface a chunk that names Deepcast. If the Series B chunk alone had been retrieved (“a single outside supplier”), there would be no name to follow.
Errors compoundA bad follow-up (“Kestrel Labs sonar history”) retrieves the 2022 supply deal and another Series B chunk, and the loop drifts. Each hop multiplies the chance of going off track.
Cost and latency scale with hopsEach hop adds an embedding call, a search and an LLM call that reads the growing evidence. Latency grows roughly with the number of hops, and each LLM call reads a longer context than the last.
Aliases still break queriesA follow-up for “who owns Deepcast now?” works; a follow-up built from the memo's “the new owner” does not.
Global questions do not decompose“Main themes” has no natural follow-up query. Covering eight documents by hopping means reading most of the corpus, one slow step at a time.
Nothing is learnedThe link Marlow → Deepcast → Kestrel is rediscovered, at full cost, for every user who asks a related question.

GraphRAG moves that work from query time to indexing time. Entities and relationships are extracted once, aliases are resolved once, communities are summarized once. At query time, following a bridge entity is a dictionary lookup instead of an LLM call. Iterative, agentic retrieval does not disappear; in Chapter 18 and Chapter 30 the agent walks a graph instead of guessing search strings.

from ch02_vector_rag import build_vector_index

class ScriptedLLM:
    def __init__(self, follow_ups):

import collections, json, math
from ch02_vector_rag import build_vector_index
from minigraphrag.embeddings import HashingEmbedder
from minigraphrag.llm import find_mentions

index = build_vector_index("data/corpus", HashingEmbedder())
store, units = index.text_unit_store, index.text_units
mentions = {uid: {m[2].upper() for m in find_mentions(u.text)} for uid, u in units.items()}
df = collections.Counter(e for ents in mentions.values() for e in ents)
N = len(units)

def expand(question, k=3, extra=4, idf=False):
    qvec = index.embedder.embed([question])[0]
    seeds = [uid for uid, _, _ in store.search(qvec, k)]
    q_ents = {m[2].upper() for m in find_mentions(question)}
    bridge = set().union(*(mentions[s] for s in seeds)) - q_ents
    weight = (lambda e: math.log(N / df[e])) if idf else (lambda e: 1.0)
    score = {uid: sum(weight(e) for e in mentions[uid] & bridge) for uid in units if uid not in seeds}
    extra_ids = sorted((u for u in score if score[u] > 0), key=lambda u: -score[u])[:extra]
    return seeds + extra_ids

def recall(fn):
    by_type = collections.defaultdict(list)
    for q in map(json.loads, open("data/questions.jsonl")):
        got = {units[u].doc_id for u in fn(q["question"])}
        need = set(q["supporting_docs"])
        by_type[q["type"]].append(len(got & need) / len(need))
    return {t: round(sum(v) / len(v), 2) for t, v in by_type.items()}

print("top-7 ", recall(lambda q: [u for u, _, _ in store.search(index.embedder.embed([q])[0], 7)]))
print("expand", recall(expand))
print("idf   ", recall(lambda q: expand(q, idf=True)))
print(expand("Why did Kestrel Labs start Project Tern?", idf=True))

Question 1 still does not retrieve the Marlow article. The expanded set is 10-001, 09-000, 05-000 plus 12-001, 12-000, 10-000, 08-000: the memo arrives, the acquisition article does not. The seeds mention seventeen bridge entities, most of them common (Port Avalon, Sentinel, Jonas Vehl and Halden Reef each appear in 13 or 14 of the 24 chunks), and chunks that mention many of them outrank the Marlow article, which shares only Deepcast, Marlow and Marlow Dynamics with the seeds, the last two being one company the gazetteer has not merged. With IDF weights it scores 4.3; the weakest chunk that made the cut scores 5.2. IDF helps but cannot overcome a pile of moderately common entities.

What this tells you about the design of real GraphRAG: linking chunks by co-mention is too coarse. You want entities as nodes with typed, described relationships (Marlow Dynamics —acquired→ Deepcast), retrieval that starts from the entities the question names, and scoring that spreads relevance along edges while discounting hubs. That is local search (Chapter 14) and Personalized PageRank (Chapter 17).

Questions learners often ask

Aren't these failures just artifacts of the toy hashing embedder?

Some are: vocabulary mismatch (“CTO” vs. “chief technology officer”) and some alias cases improve a lot with a neural embedder. The structural ones do not. The Marlow article is not about Kestrel or Tern in any embedding space; “main themes” is not answered by any single chunk; similarity has no notion of time; and a chunk boundary splits a relationship regardless of how the halves are embedded. Exercise: rerun this chapter's tables with get_embedder("st:all-MiniLM-L6-v2") or "openai" and see which rows change.

Would a long-context model with the whole corpus in the prompt avoid all this?

For a 12-document corpus, mostly yes, and Chapter 1 recommends trying it. Every fact is present, so the retrieval failures disappear, leaving the reasoning ones: connecting facts across documents, preferring the newest statement, synthesizing themes. Strong models do reasonably well at those on 7,000 tokens. The approach stops working when the corpus outgrows the window or the budget, which is the normal situation for the private data RAG is built for.

Can a reranker fix multi-hop retrieval?

A cross-encoder reranker, which reads the question and a chunk together, ranks the candidates it is given more accurately than cosine similarity does. It still scores each chunk against the question on its own, and it only reranks what the first stage retrieved. The Marlow article at rank 21 is neither in the candidate set nor relevant to the question on its own. Rerankers help precision; they do not create bridges.

How common are multi-hop and global questions in practice?

It depends heavily on the application. Customer support over product docs is dominated by lookups. Research, due diligence, investigations, incident analysis and “what's going on in these reports?” tasks are dominated by multi-hop and sensemaking questions. Benchmarks such as HotpotQA and MultiHop-RAG were built to test questions whose evidence sits in several documents, which single-hop benchmarks do not exercise. Log your users' real questions and classify a sample before deciding.

If a document already connects the dots, like the 2026 interview, why build a graph?

Because you cannot rely on it. Such documents cover a few popular connections, go stale, and are absent for exactly the new questions that make analysis valuable. A graph built from all documents contains the connections whether or not anyone wrote them up, and it can show the path it used, which a summary document cannot.

Key takeaways

  • Vector retrieval scores each chunk against the question independently, so evidence linked only through a shared entity is invisible.
  • Multi-hop successes often depend on some document having already written the connection down. Remove it and retrieval fails.
  • Global questions are query-focused summarization over the corpus; no chunk is their nearest neighbor.
  • Aliases, chunk boundaries, stale statements and hub words each lose relationships in their own way.
  • Raising k and iterating queries help partially, at growing cost and fragility, and do not help sensemaking.
  • GraphRAG moves linking, resolution and summarization to indexing time. Use the taxonomy to decide which techniques your questions need.
