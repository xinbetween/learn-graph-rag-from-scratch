Hybrid retrieval and seven Graph RAG designs

No single retriever wins on every question. This chapter builds the hybrid layer that production systems actually ship: BM25 from scratch, dense and graph rankings, reciprocal rank fusion, reranking and query routing. Then it walks through seven reusable Graph RAG designs, each tied to the Kestrel Labs graph and a short code sketch.

You will be able to

  • Fuse keyword, dense and graph rankings with reciprocal rank fusion, and say why it beats naive score normalization
  • Add a cross-encoder or graph-aware reranker and estimate what it costs
  • Route a question to local, path, global or Text2Cypher retrieval
  • Apply seven Graph RAG designs (neighborhood expansion, metapaths, subgraph assembly, temporal windows, hybrid rerankers, panels and provenance) and name the pitfall of each
  • Combine a metapath and a time window into one event-centered retrieval on the Kestrel graph

Why one retriever is not enough

By now you have built several retrievers. Vector RAG ranks chunks by embedding similarity. Local search anchors on entities and fans out. Personalized PageRank spreads relevance through the graph. Global search map-reduces community reports. Text2Cypher turns a question into a database query. Each was motivated by a question the previous one got wrong.

In production you rarely get to pick one, because users do not announce what kind of question they are asking. Look at how differently the Kestrel questions fail:

QuestionWhat makes it hardRetriever that tends to win
“What did the Deepcast letter say about the second quarter?”An exact phrase and a rare nameBM25: exact term matching on “Deepcast” and “quarter”
“Which sonar vendor was bought by a defense company?”No names at all; the corpus says “acquired” and “Marlow Dynamics”Dense vectors: paraphrase matching
“Why did Kestrel Labs start Project Tern?”The answer spans three documents joined by entitiesGraph: paths and PPR through Deepcast and Marlow Dynamics

A hybrid retrieval layer has three jobs: run more than one retriever, merge their rankings into one list, and spend a more expensive model on the short list that survives. An optional fourth job, routing, decides which retrievers to run at all.

BM25 from scratch

Dense embeddings are good at meaning and bad at identity. An embedding of “Deepcast” sits close to “sonar supplier”, which helps with paraphrases and hurts when the user typed the exact name and wants that name. Keyword retrieval is the opposite. BM25 (“Best Match 25”; the number is a version label from the Okapi retrieval system at City University London) is still the default keyword ranker in Lucene, Elasticsearch and OpenSearch because it is cheap, needs no training and is hard to beat on exact-term queries.

Three intuitions

  • Rare terms matter more. A match on “supplied”, which appears in one passage, says more than a match on “Kestrel”, which appears everywhere. That is inverse document frequency (IDF).
  • Repetition helps, with diminishing returns. A passage that says “sonar” twice is probably more about sonar than one that says it once, but the tenth mention adds almost nothing. That is term-frequency saturation, controlled by k1.
  • Long passages match everything a little. A term hit in a short passage is stronger evidence than the same hit in a long one. That is length normalization, controlled by b.

The formula

For a query q with terms t, a document d of length |d| tokens, average document length avgdl, and term frequency f(t, d):

score(d, q) = Σ over t in q of   IDF(t) · f(t,d) · (k1 + 1) / ( f(t,d) + k1 · (1 − b + b · |d| / avgdl) )

IDF(t) = ln( (N − n(t) + 0.5) / (n(t) + 0.5) + 1 )

Here N is the number of documents and n(t) the number that contain t. The “+1” inside the logarithm is the Lucene variant: it keeps IDF positive even for a term that appears in most documents, where the classic Robertson–Spärck Jones form goes negative. Typical settings are k1 between 1.2 and 2.0 and b = 0.75. minigraphrag defaults to k1 = 1.5.

Worked numbers on five Kestrel passages

Take five short passages, tokenized with simple_tokenize from minigraphrag/embeddings.py (lowercase, stopwords removed, a crude plural strip):

idPassageTokens |d|

N = 5 and avgdl = (8 + 12 + 12 + 9 + 11) / 5 = 10.4. The query “Who supplied sonar to Kestrel?” tokenizes to supplied, sonar, kestrel (“who” and “to” are stopwords).

  1. IDF. “supplied” is in 1 passage: ln((5 − 1 + 0.5)/(1 + 0.5) + 1) = ln(4) = 1.386. “sonar” is in 3 passages (d1, d2, d3): ln(2.5/3.5 + 1) = ln(1.714) = 0.539. “kestrel” is also in 3 (d2, d3, d5): 0.539.
  2. Length factor for d2. k1 · (1 − b + b · 12/10.4) = 1.5 × (0.25 + 0.865) = 1.673.
  3. Saturated term frequency for one hit in d2. 1 × 2.5 / (1 + 1.673) = 0.935.
  4. Score of d2. 1.386 × 0.935 + 0.539 × 0.935 + 0.539 × 0.935 = 1.297 + 0.504 + 0.504 = 2.305.
  5. Score of d3. “sonar” appears twice: 2 × 2.5 / (2 + 1.673) = 1.361, times 0.539 = 0.734. Plus “kestrel” once: 0.504. Total 1.238.
  6. d1 (8 tokens, shorter than average) gets a smaller length factor, 1.5 × (0.25 + 0.75 × 0.769) = 1.240, so its single “sonar” hit is worth 0.539 × 2.5/2.240 = 0.601. d5 scores 0.525 for “kestrel”. d4 scores 0.

The ranking is d2 (2.305), d3 (1.238), d1 (0.601), d5 (0.525). Two lessons hide in these numbers. First, the one rare term “supplied” contributes more than half of d2's score. Second, d3 mentions “sonar” twice but gets only 1.361 rather than 2 × 0.935 = 1.870 for it: that is saturation.

What the knobs do, in numbers

Setting b = 0 switches length normalization off: d2 rises to 2.464 and d1 drops to 0.539, because the short passage no longer gets a bonus. Pushing k1 toward 0 makes BM25 binary: d1, d2 and d3 all score exactly 0.539 for the query “sonar”, since two mentions count the same as one. Large k1 (say 3) lets repetition count for longer before saturating.

Here is the whole ranker as it ships in minigraphrag. Everything is vectorized over documents with NumPy, but it is the formula above line for line.

from ..embeddings import simple_tokenize


def tokenize(text: str) -> list[str]:
    return simple_tokenize(text)


class BM25:
    def __init__(self, documents: list[str], k1: float = 1.5, b: float = 0.75,
                 tokenizer: Callable[[str], list[str]] = tokenize):
        self.k1, self.b, self.tokenizer = k1, b, tokenizer
        self.doc_tfs = [Counter(tokenizer(d)) for d in documents]
        self.doc_lens = np.array([sum(tf.values()) for tf in self.doc_tfs], dtype=float)
        self.avgdl = float(self.doc_lens.mean()) if len(documents) else 0.0
        df = Counter(t for tf in self.doc_tfs for t in tf)
        n = len(documents)
        # The +1 inside the log keeps IDF positive even for terms in most documents.
        self.idf = {t: math.log((n - f + 0.5) / (f + 0.5) + 1) for t, f in df.items()}

    def get_scores(self, query: str) -> np.ndarray:
        scores = np.zeros(len(self.doc_tfs))
        for term in set(self.tokenizer(query)):
            if term not in self.idf:
                continue
            tf = np.array([d.get(term, 0) for d in self.doc_tfs], dtype=float)
            norm = self.k1 * (1 - self.b + self.b * self.doc_lens / (self.avgdl or 1))
            scores += self.idf[term] * tf * (self.k1 + 1) / (tf + norm)
        return scores

    def search(self, query: str, top_k: int = 5) -> list[tuple[int, float]]:
        """(document index, score) pairs, best first; zero-score documents are omitted."""
        scores = self.get_scores(query)
        order = np.argsort(-scores, kind="stable")[:top_k]
        return [(int(i), float(scores[i])) for i in order if scores[i] > 0]
Tokenization decides what BM25 can match

simple_tokenize turns “Dynamics” into “dynamic” and “Series” into “serie”, and it has no idea that “Kestrel's” and “Kestrel” are the same word beyond stripping punctuation. Whatever you choose, use the same tokenizer for documents and queries. For identifier-heavy corpora (part numbers, ticket ids) keep a second field with no stemming at all.

Robertson and Zaragoza, The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond (2009)

This survey derives BM25 from a probabilistic model of relevance and explains the saturation and length-normalization components as approximations to that model. It also covers BM25F, which weights fields such as title and body separately: a useful idea when your text units carry a document title or section heading.

Dense and graph rankings

For fusion, every retriever only needs to produce a ranked list of the same kind of item. In minigraphrag that item is a text unit id, which keeps citations simple.

  • Dense: embed the question with index.embedder, then index.text_unit_store.search(vec, k=20) returns (id, cosine, metadata) tuples. It catches “sonar vendor bought by a defense company” without any shared keywords.
  • Graph: link_query_to_nodes maps the question to seed entities, personalized_pagerank spreads mass through the entity graph, and rank_passages sums node mass over the text units each entity was extracted from (Chapter 17). It surfaces the Marlow acquisition article for “Why did Kestrel start Tern?” even though that article never mentions Tern.

Graph retrieval can rank more than passages. Local search ranks entities and relationships, global search ranks community reports, and path retrieval ranks paths. You can fuse those too, as long as each list contains comparable items. Mixing passages and entities in one fused list works poorly because their “goodness” is not on the same scale; give each kind its own section of the context instead (Chapter 22).

Fusing rankings

You now have three ranked lists for the same question, and they have to become one.

Why adding scores fails

BM25 scores are unbounded and depend on the query: 2.3 for our three-term query, perhaps 11 for a longer one. Cosine similarities from a sentence embedder cluster in a narrow band like 0.35 to 0.45. PPR masses are probabilities that sum to 1 across the whole graph. Adding them lets BM25 dominate for no principled reason.

The usual patch is min-max normalization per list, then a weighted sum (called CombSUM in the information retrieval literature):

def min_max(scores: dict[str, float]) -> dict[str, float]:
    lo, hi = min(scores.values()), max(scores.values())
    return {d: (s - lo) / (hi - lo) if hi > lo else 1.0 for d, s in scores.items()}

def combsum(runs: dict[str, dict[str, float]], weights: dict[str, float]) -> list[tuple[str, float]]:
    """Normalize each retriever's scores to [0, 1], then add them with weights (CombSUM)."""
    fused: dict[str, float] = {}
    for name, scores in runs.items():
        for doc, s in min_max(scores).items():
            fused[doc] = fused.get(doc, 0.0) + weights.get(name, 1.0) * s
    return sorted(fused.items(), key=lambda x: -x[1])

Normalization has its own failure. Suppose BM25 gives passages a, b, c scores 2.3, 1.2, 0.6 and the dense retriever gives them 0.39, 0.40, 0.41. The dense retriever is saying “these are all about equally relevant.” Min-max stretches that 0.02 spread to the full 0-to-1 range, so c jumps from last to a tie with a (both 1.0), overriding a clear BM25 preference on the strength of noise. Z-score normalization has the same problem with short lists, and both are sensitive to a single outlier score.

Reciprocal rank fusion

Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) throws scores away and uses only positions. Each list votes for each item with a weight that decays with rank:

RRF(d) = Σ over retrievers r of   w_r / (k + rank_r(d))        rank starts at 1; items missing from a list contribute 0

Because only ranks matter, RRF is immune to score scale, needs no training data and has one parameter, k.

Why k is about 60

k controls how much being first is worth compared with being present. With k = 0, rank 1 earns 1.0 and rank 2 earns 0.5: the top of any single list dominates. With k = 60, rank 1 earns 1/61 = 0.01639 and rank 2 earns 1/62 = 0.01613, only 1.6% less, while rank 10 still earns 1/70 = 0.01429. Presence in several lists now counts for much more than exact position within one.

Work through three lists: BM25 ranks A, C, B; dense ranks B, D, C; graph ranks E, F, C. Item C is never first but appears in all three lists.

Itemk = 0k = 60
A (BM25 rank 1)1/1 = 1.0001/61 = 0.0164
B (BM25 rank 3, dense rank 1)1/3 + 1/1 = 1.3331/63 + 1/61 = 0.0323
C (ranks 2, 3, 3)1/2 + 1/3 + 1/3 = 1.1671/62 + 1/63 + 1/63 = 0.0479

At k = 0, B wins because one retriever loved it. At k = 60, C wins because all three retrievers found it. The value 60 comes from the original RRF paper, where it was fixed after pilot experiments and worked well across collections; it is also the default constant in Elasticsearch's RRF and in several other search engines. Treat 60 as a good default rather than a law; the script in Exercise 21.3 makes sweeping it a one-line change.

Look at where “07 Marlow buys Deepcast” lands. It is the article that explains the root cause, and only the graph retriever found it. With k = 60 it scores 1/61 = 0.0164 and finishes fifth, behind documents that two retrievers agreed on. This is the main weakness of equal-weight RRF for GraphRAG: evidence that only the graph can find is exactly the evidence you added a graph for, and fusion buries it. Two fixes are common. Weight the graph list higher (weights=[1, 1, 2] gives 07 a score of 2/61 = 0.0328, which lifts it from fifth to fourth, ahead of the leadership interview), or reserve a few context slots for each retriever's top items before filling the rest from the fused list.

from ..context import format_table
from ..llm import LLM
from ..tokens import count_tokens
from .bm25 import BM25
from .common import DEFAULT_RESPONSE_TYPE, SearchResult, doc_ids_for_units, generate_answer
from .ppr import link_query_to_nodes, personalized_pagerank, rank_passages


def reciprocal_rank_fusion(rankings: list[list[str]], k: int = 60, weights: list[float] | None = None) -> list[tuple[str, float]]:
    """Fuse several ranked id lists (best first). Returns (id, fused score), best first."""
    weights = weights or [1.0] * len(rankings)
    scores: dict[str, float] = {}
    for ranking, w in zip(rankings, weights):
        for rank, item in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            scores[item] = scores.get(item, 0.0) + w / (k + rank)
    return sorted(scores.items(), key=lambda x: (-x[1], x[0]))


def hybrid_search(index, query: str, llm: LLM, top_k: int = 5, rrf_k: int = 60, candidates: int = 20,
                  max_context_tokens: int = 4000, response_type: str = DEFAULT_RESPONSE_TYPE) -> SearchResult:
    """Fuse BM25, vector and PPR-graph rankings of text units, then answer."""
    unit_ids = list(index.text_units)
    bm25 = BM25([index.text_units[u].text for u in unit_ids])
    keyword = [unit_ids[i] for i, _ in bm25.search(query, candidates)]
    vector = [uid for uid, _, _ in index.text_unit_store.search(index.embedder.embed([query])[0], k=candidates)]
    ppr = personalized_pagerank(index.graph, link_query_to_nodes(index, query))
    graph = [uid for uid, _ in rank_passages(index, ppr)[:candidates]]

    fused = reciprocal_rank_fusion([keyword, vector, graph], k=rrf_k)
    rows, used = [], 0
    for uid, score in fused[:top_k]:
        text = index.text_units[uid].text
        if used + count_tokens(text) > max_context_tokens:
            break
        rows.append([uid, text])
        used += count_tokens(text)
    context = format_table("Sources", ["id", "text"], rows)
    answer = generate_answer(llm, query, context, response_type)
    data = {"rankings": {"bm25": keyword, "vector": vector, "graph": graph}, "fused": fused[:top_k],
            "sources": [{"id": r[0], "text": r[1]} for r in rows]}
    return SearchResult(answer, "hybrid", context, data, doc_ids_for_units(index, [r[0] for r in rows]), llm_calls=1)

Reranking the short list

Retrievers use a bi-encoder or keyword index: the query and each passage are encoded separately, so passage vectors can be precomputed and search is fast. A cross-encoder feeds the query and a passage into one transformer together, letting every query token attend to every passage token. That makes it much more accurate at judging relevance and much more expensive: one forward pass per candidate, at query time. The standard compromise is to fuse cheaply, then cross-encode only the top 20 to 50.

from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder   # pip install sentence-transformers

reranker = CrossEncoder("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2")

def cross_encoder_rerank(index, query: str, fused: list[tuple[str, float]], top_n: int = 30, keep: int = 8):
    """Score (query, passage) pairs jointly for the fused top-n; keep the best `keep`."""
    candidates = [uid for uid, _ in fused[:top_n]]
    pairs = [(query, index.text_units[uid].text) for uid in candidates]
    scores = reranker.predict(pairs)                 # one transformer forward pass per pair
    ranked = sorted(zip(candidates, scores), key=lambda x: -x[1])
    return [(uid, float(s)) for uid, s in ranked[:keep]]

Cost reasoning: a MiniLM-sized cross-encoder scores somewhere between tens and a few hundred short pairs per second on a CPU, and far more on a GPU, so reranking 30 candidates adds roughly tens to hundreds of milliseconds. A 7B LLM used as a listwise reranker is far slower and costs tokens; use one only when a cross-encoder demonstrably fails on your questions.

A cross-encoder sees one passage at a time, so it cannot tell that passage A matters because it connects to passage B. That is exactly the multi-hop signal GraphRAG cares about, and it motivates graph-based reranking.

Dong et al., Don't Forget to Connect! Improving RAG with Graph-based Reranking (2024)

G-RAG builds a graph over the retrieved documents, connecting documents that share concepts from their Abstract Meaning Representation (AMR) parses, and trains a graph neural network to rerank them. Documents that are only partially relevant on their own can be lifted by their connections to relevant ones. The authors also report that using PaLM 2 directly as a reranker performed considerably worse than their GNN reranker, a useful caution against assuming a big LLM is the best reranker.

Sarmah et al., HybridRAG: Integrating Knowledge Graphs and Vector Retrieval Augmented Generation (2024)

HybridRAG retrieves context from both a vector database and a knowledge graph built from financial earnings call transcripts, and passes both to the generator. On their question set the combination outperformed either VectorRAG or GraphRAG alone on retrieval and answer metrics. The design is simple, concatenating the two contexts rather than fusing rankings, which makes it a good baseline to beat before you build anything fancier.

Query routing

Running every retriever on every question wastes latency and money, and some retrievers actively hurt certain questions: global search on “Who is the CTO?” produces a vague essay. A router classifies the question first. The categories that matter for GraphRAG map directly onto earlier chapters:

RouteSignalsKestrel exampleRetrieval

Time is better handled as a modifier than as a route. “Who supplied Kestrel's sonar in 2024?” is a local question with a time constraint, so the router extracts as_of and the temporal-window design below applies it.

import re
from minigraphrag.llm import parse_json_response

ROUTER_PROMPT = """Classify the question for a retrieval router. Categories:
- local: about specific named entities and their direct facts
- path: asks how entities are connected, or why something happened through a chain of events
- global: asks for themes, trends, risks or summaries across the whole corpus
- text2cypher: asks to count, filter, sort or aggregate typed records
Return JSON: {{"route": "<category>", "as_of": "<YYYY or YYYY-MM if the question names a time, else null>"}}
Question: {question}"""

GLOBAL_CUES = re.compile(r"\b(themes?|trends?|overall|main|risks?|summar\w+|across)\b", re.I)
CYPHER_CUES = re.compile(r"\b(how many|count|list all|average|top \d+|most)\b", re.I)
PATH_CUES = re.compile(r"\b(connect\w*|why|whose|link\w*|path|between)\b", re.I)
YEAR = re.compile(r"\b(20\d\d)\b")

def route_query(question: str, llm=None) -> dict:
    """Pick a retrieval strategy. Uses the LLM when given, with regex rules as a cheap fallback."""
    if llm is not None:
        out = parse_json_response(llm.complete(ROUTER_PROMPT.format(question=question), json=True))
        if out.get("route") in {"local", "path", "global", "text2cypher"}:
            return {"route": out["route"], "as_of": out.get("as_of")}
    year = YEAR.search(question)
    as_of = year.group(1) if year else None
    if GLOBAL_CUES.search(question):
        return {"route": "global", "as_of": as_of}
    if CYPHER_CUES.search(question):
        return {"route": "text2cypher", "as_of": as_of}
    if PATH_CUES.search(question):
        return {"route": "path", "as_of": as_of}
    return {"route": "local", "as_of": as_of}
Misroutes are silent

A wrong route still returns an answer, just a worse one, so routing errors do not show up in logs. Keep a labeled set of 50 to 100 questions with their correct route, measure router accuracy separately from answer quality, and when the router is unsure (for instance the LLM and the regex rules disagree) run both candidate routes and fuse.

Seven Graph RAG designs

The first half of this chapter was about combining retrievers. The second half is about shaping what graph retrieval returns. Thinking Loop's Medium article “7 Graph RAG Designs That Outperform Keyword Search” (October 2025) lists seven designs: neighborhood expansion, metapaths, subgraph assembly, temporal windows, hybrid rerankers, panels and provenance. We use that list as a checklist. The article is paywalled, so everything below is our own description of each idea as a general pattern, with definitions, trade-offs and code written for this course rather than quoted from the article.

Each design gets the same treatment: what it is, when to use it, what goes wrong, what it looks like on the Kestrel graph, and a code sketch. Where the course covers a design in depth, we link there.

1. Neighborhood expansion

What it is. Start from seed entities matched to the question and pull in their neighbors, up to a hop limit, then use the entities, relationships and source passages of that neighborhood as context. It is the core move of local search.

When to use it. Entity-centric questions where the answer is one or two hops from something the user named: “Who leads Project Sentinel?”, “Who is on Kestrel's board?”

Pitfalls. Hubs explode. Kestrel Labs touches most of the graph, so a 2-hop expansion from it returns nearly everything, and the context fills with loosely related facts. Cap the fan-out per node, rank neighbors by edge weight or query similarity, and cap total nodes.

Kestrel example. Seeding on Project Tern with one hop and a fan-out cap of three keeps its heaviest three edges. Without the cap, a second hop through Kestrel Labs would pull in Brightwater Capital, the City Council and Tidewater Institute, none of which help explain Tern.

import networkx as nx

def expand_neighborhood(G: nx.Graph, seeds: list[str], hops: int = 1, max_per_node: int = 5,
                        max_nodes: int = 30) -> list[str]:
    """Breadth-first expansion that follows only the heaviest `max_per_node` edges of each node."""
    selected = list(dict.fromkeys(s for s in seeds if s in G))
    frontier = list(selected)
    for _ in range(hops):
        nxt = []
        for node in frontier:
            nbrs = sorted(G.neighbors(node), key=lambda n: -G.edges[node, n].get("weight", 1.0))
            for n in nbrs[:max_per_node]:
                if n not in selected and n not in nxt:
                    nxt.append(n)
        room = max_nodes - len(selected)
        if room <= 0:
            break
        selected += nxt[:room]
        frontier = nxt[:room]
    return selected

2. Metapaths

What it is. A metapath is a sequence of relation types and node types, such as Organization →ACQUIRED→ Organization →SUPPLIED→ Organization →RUNS→ Project. Instead of expanding blindly, you only follow edges that match the next step of the pattern. The idea comes from heterogeneous information network research, where metapaths define which kinds of connection count as meaningful.

When to use it. You have a typed graph and recurring question shapes. “Which projects were affected when a supplier was acquired?” is always the same metapath, whatever the company names. Metapaths are also a clean way to express domain knowledge that an analyst can review.

Pitfalls. They need a typed schema: the minigraphrag index stores free-text descriptions on undirected edges, so metapaths require a typed view such as the one below (Chapter 5 discusses schemas). Metapaths are brittle to extraction drift: if half the supply edges came out as SUPPLIES_TO, the walk silently finds nothing. And hand-written metapaths do not cover novel questions; pair them with a fallback.

import networkx as nx

def follow_metapath(G: nx.MultiDiGraph, start: str, metapath: list[tuple[str, str]],
                    max_fanout: int = 25) -> list[list[tuple[str, str, str]]]:
    """All walks from `start` that match `metapath`, a list of (RELATION, TARGET_TYPE) steps.

    Prefix a relation with "^" to traverse it backwards, e.g. ("^RUNS", "ORGANIZATION")
    goes from a project to the organization that runs it.
    """
    walks = [[]]                      # each walk is a list of (source, relation, target) hops
    frontier = [start]
    for relation, target_type in metapath:
        backwards = relation.startswith("^")
        rel = relation.lstrip("^")
        next_walks, next_frontier = [], []
        for walk, node in zip(walks, frontier):
            edges = G.in_edges(node, data=True) if backwards else G.out_edges(node, data=True)
            matches = []
            for u, v, d in edges:
                other = u if backwards else v
                if d["relation"] == rel and G.nodes[other]["type"] == target_type:
                    matches.append((other, (u, rel, v)))
            for other, hop in sorted(matches)[:max_fanout]:   # cap fan-out per node
                if any(other in (h[0], h[2]) for h in walk) or other == start:
                    continue                                   # keep walks simple (no cycles)
                next_walks.append(walk + [hop])
                next_frontier.append(other)
        walks, frontier = next_walks, next_frontier
    return walks

3. Subgraph assembly

What it is. Collect candidate evidence from several sources (seed entities, neighborhood expansion, metapath walks, top passages' entities) and assemble it into one connected, size-bounded subgraph before serializing it. The connection requirement is the point: it keeps the bridge entities that link relevant facts, even when a bridge is not similar to the query itself.

When to use it. Multi-hop questions whose evidence is spread over several seeds, and whenever you want the context to read as one coherent story rather than a bag of facts. Chapter 19 covers the prize-collecting Steiner tree formulation that G-Retriever uses.

Pitfalls. Exact Steiner trees are NP-hard, so you rely on heuristics that can drop a relevant but weakly connected node. Prizes from different sources need balancing; if name matches get a huge prize, the tree bends around them. And a connected subgraph can still be too big to serialize, so the node cap must be derived from your token budget.

Kestrel example. For “Why did Kestrel Labs start Project Tern?”, name matching seeds Kestrel Labs and Project Tern, the metapath walks add Marlow Dynamics and Deepcast, and the tree keeps the edges that connect them.

import networkx as nx
from minigraphrag.search.local import map_query_to_entities
from minigraphrag.search.subgraph import similarity_prizes, pcst_subgraph

def assemble_subgraph(index, question: str, metapath_walks: list[list[tuple]] = (), max_nodes: int = 12) -> nx.Graph:
    """Union three evidence sources, then keep one connected, budget-sized piece."""
    G = index.graph
    prizes = similarity_prizes(index, question, top_k=10)             # semantic relevance
    for name in map_query_to_entities(index, question, top_k=3):       # named in the question
        prizes[name] = prizes.get(name, 0.0) + 5.0
    for walk in metapath_walks:                                        # typed paths found earlier
        for u, _, v in walk:
            for n in (u.upper(), v.upper()):
                if n in G:
                    prizes[n] = prizes.get(n, 0.0) + 3.0
    return pcst_subgraph(G, prizes, edge_cost=1.0, max_nodes=max_nodes)

4. Temporal windows

What it is. Give every fact a validity interval and filter retrieval to facts that were true during the time the question cares about. The window can be a point (“as of March 2026”) or a span (“around the bleaching event”).

When to use it. Any corpus where facts change: roles, suppliers, prices, ownership. Without a window, “Who is the CTO of Kestrel Labs?” retrieves both Jonas Vehl and Priya Nair, and the model picks one, often the one mentioned more. Chapter 10 extracts dated claims and Chapter 27 builds bi-temporal edges properly.

For point-in-time questions, minigraphrag/temporal.py already provides a bi-temporal graph with add_fact, as_of and holders. The window helper below handles the other case, a span of time, over the typed view.

Pitfalls. Undated facts: you must decide whether a fact with no dates passes every window (recall) or none (precision). Granularity: a fact dated only “2023” overlaps any window that touches 2023. Boundaries: with month granularity, a window of March 2026 contains both Vehl's CTO role (ends 2026-03) and Nair's (starts 2026-03). And document date is not fact date: an interview recorded in 2026 can describe 2022.

from datetime import date

def _to_date(s: str | None, default: date, end: bool = False) -> date:
    """'2025-06' -> 1 June 2025; '2023' -> 1 Jan 2023 (or 1 Dec 2023 when used as an end)."""
    if s is None:
        return default
    month = int(s[5:7]) if len(s) >= 7 else (12 if end else 1)
    return date(int(s[:4]), month, 1)

def overlaps(d: dict, start: str, end: str) -> bool:
    """Does the fact's validity [valid_from, valid_to] overlap the window [start, end]?"""
    f = _to_date(d.get("valid_from"), date.min)
    t = _to_date(d.get("valid_to"), date.max, end=True)
    return f <= _to_date(end, date.max, end=True) and t >= _to_date(start, date.min)

def facts_in_window(G, nodes: set[str], start: str, end: str) -> list[tuple]:
    """Edges touching `nodes` whose validity overlaps the window, oldest first."""
    rows = []
    for u, v, d in G.edges(data=True):
        if (u in nodes or v in nodes) and overlaps(d, start, end):
            rows.append((d.get("valid_from") or "????", u, d["relation"], v, d.get("valid_to") or "open"))
    return sorted(rows)

5. Hybrid rerankers

What it is. A reranker that scores each candidate with several signals at once: text relevance (a cross-encoder or the fused rank), graph signals (distance to the question's seed entities, PPR mass, whether the passage supports an edge on a found path), and metadata (recency, source authority). The simplest version is a hand-weighted linear blend; the next step is a small learned model such as logistic regression or gradient-boosted trees trained on labeled pairs.

When to use it. After fusion, when a text-only reranker keeps demoting passages that matter for structural reasons. The Marlow acquisition article is the Kestrel example: a cross-encoder sees little overlap with “Why did Kestrel start Tern?”, but the graph says it sits two hops from both seeds on the only path between them.

Pitfalls. Unscaled features (the same trap as score fusion), weights tuned on five questions, and graph features that reward hubs: every passage mentions Kestrel Labs, so “distance to Kestrel Labs” is always 0. Prefer features computed from the less common seed entities, and hold out questions when tuning weights.

import networkx as nx

def graph_proximity(G: nx.Graph, seeds: list[str], passage_entities: list[str], cutoff: int = 3) -> float:
    """1.0 if the passage mentions a seed entity, 0.5 one hop away, 0.33 two hops away, 0 beyond cutoff."""
    best = None
    for s in seeds:
        if s not in G:
            continue
        dist = nx.single_source_shortest_path_length(G, s, cutoff=cutoff)
        for e in passage_entities:
            if e in dist and (best is None or dist[e] < best):
                best = dist[e]
    return 0.0 if best is None else 1.0 / (1 + best)

def hybrid_rerank(candidates: list[str], features: dict[str, dict[str, float]],
                  weights: dict[str, float]) -> list[tuple[str, float]]:
    """Linear blend of per-candidate features that are already scaled to [0, 1]."""
    scored = [(c, sum(w * features[c].get(name, 0.0) for name, w in weights.items())) for c in candidates]
    return sorted(scored, key=lambda x: -x[1])

6. Panels

What it is (our interpretation). The list names “panels” without a definition we can read, so this is our reading of it: present evidence to the model as several labeled context panels, each answering a different sub-question, assembled per query. Typical panels are an entity panel (who and what is involved), a relationship-path panel (how they connect), a timeline panel (what was true when) and a source-passages panel (the original text to quote). Each panel has its own ranking and its own token budget, so no single kind of evidence can crowd out the others. GraphRAG's local search, with its separate Reports, Entities, Relationships and Sources tables, is an early instance of the same idea.

When to use it. Questions that need several kinds of evidence at once, and any setting where you want the model to cite the kind of evidence as well as the item: “[Paths: 1] [Sources: 08_kestrel_board_memo_supply-000]”.

Pitfalls. Duplicate content across panels wastes budget (the same fact as a path and as a sentence in a source); deduplicate across panels as well as within them. Fixed shares waste space when a panel is empty; redistribute unused tokens. Too many panels fragment attention, so four or five is a practical maximum.

from minigraphrag.context import fit_to_budget
from minigraphrag.search.local import map_query_to_entities
from minigraphrag.search.paths import find_relational_paths, paths_to_text

PANEL_SHARES = {"Entities": 0.15, "Paths": 0.25, "Timeline": 0.15, "Sources": 0.45}

def build_panels(index, question: str, timeline_rows: list[str], budget: int = 4000) -> str:
    """Evidence as labeled panels. Each panel answers one kind of sub-question and has its own budget."""
    G = index.graph
    seeds = map_query_to_entities(index, question, top_k=3)
    rows = {
        "Entities": [f"{G.nodes[n]['human_id']}|{n}|{G.nodes[n].get('type', '')}|{G.nodes[n].get('description', '')[:300]}"
                     for n in seeds],
        "Paths": paths_to_text(G, find_relational_paths(G, seeds[:1], seeds[1:], max_hops=3)).splitlines(),
        "Timeline": timeline_rows,                       # e.g. from facts_in_window, oldest first
        "Sources": [f"{uid}|{index.text_units[uid].text}"
                    for uid in dict.fromkeys(u for n in seeds for u in G.nodes[n].get("source_ids", []))],
    }
    headers = {"Entities": "id|entity|type|description", "Paths": "path", "Timeline": "from|subject|relation|object|to",
               "Sources": "id|text"}
    panels = []
    for title, share in PANEL_SHARES.items():
        kept = fit_to_budget(rows[title], int(budget * share), header=headers[title])
        if kept:                                          # an empty panel is noise; leave it out
            panels.append("\n".join([f"-----{title}-----", headers[title], *kept]))
    return "\n\n".join(panels)

7. Provenance

What it is. Every fact that reaches the prompt carries a pointer back to the text that states it: source text unit ids, ideally a short supporting quote, and the extraction run that produced it. Answers cite those pointers, and anything without a pointer is either dropped or clearly flagged.

When to use it. Always, but it is non-negotiable when users make decisions from answers, when the graph is built by an LLM that can invent edges, and when you need to delete a document and everything derived from it. Chapter 7 created the ids, Chapter 8 attached them to every record, and Chapter 23 verifies paths hop by hop against them.

Pitfalls. Merged descriptions lose provenance granularity: after summarization, which sentence came from which source is gone, so keep source_ids lists through every merge. Citations to ids that exist but do not support the claim are worse than no citations, because they look trustworthy. And provenance must survive incremental updates; a deleted document should remove its edges or at least their support.

from dataclasses import dataclass, field

@dataclass
class EvidenceFact:
    subject: str
    relation: str
    object: str
    source_ids: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)   # text unit ids that state the fact
    quote: str = ""                                        # the shortest supporting span
    valid_from: str | None = None
    valid_to: str | None = None

    def render(self, fact_id: int) -> str:
        when = f" [{self.valid_from or '?'} to {self.valid_to or 'now'}]" if (self.valid_from or self.valid_to) else ""
        return (f"F{fact_id}: {self.subject} {self.relation} {self.object}{when} "
                f"(sources: {', '.join(self.source_ids) or 'NONE'}; quote: \"{self.quote}\")")

def facts_from_edges(index, edges: list[tuple[str, str]]) -> list[EvidenceFact]:
    """Wrap minigraphrag edges as facts with their source ids and a short supporting quote."""
    facts = []
    for u, v in edges:
        d = index.graph.edges[u, v]
        quote = ""
        for uid in d.get("source_ids", []):
            text = index.text_units[uid].text if uid in index.text_units else ""
            for sentence in text.replace("\n", " ").split(". "):
                if u.split()[0].lower() in sentence.lower() and v.split()[0].lower() in sentence.lower():
                    quote = sentence.strip()[:200]
                    break
            if quote:
                break
        facts.append(EvidenceFact(u, "RELATED_TO", v, list(d.get("source_ids", [])), quote))
    return facts

def drop_unsupported(facts: list[EvidenceFact]) -> tuple[list[EvidenceFact], list[EvidenceFact]]:
    kept = [f for f in facts if f.source_ids and f.quote]
    return kept, [f for f in facts if f not in kept]

Putting designs together: an event-centered briefing

Three of the designs compose into one retrieval that deserves a name of its own: seed on an event node, expand along a metapath, then keep only the facts that were true inside a time window around the event. It answers the kind of question an analyst asks after something happens. On Kestrel: “Brief me on the 2024 Halden Reef bleaching detection: who was involved and what was the situation at the time?”

  1. Seed on the event node 2024 Halden Reef bleaching event.
  2. Expand two hops along a metapath: Event ←DETECTED− Project ←RUNS− Organization. This reaches Project Sentinel and Kestrel Labs and ignores everything else adjacent to the reef.
  3. Pull a time window of six months either side of August 2024 (2024-02 to 2025-02) over all facts touching those three nodes.
from examples.kestrel_kg import typed_graph
from examples.metapaths import follow_metapath
from examples.temporal_window import facts_in_window

def event_briefing(G, seed: str, metapath: list[tuple[str, str]], start: str, end: str) -> list[str]:
    """Seed on an event, expand along a metapath, then keep facts valid in the window."""
    walks = follow_metapath(G, seed, metapath)
    nodes = {seed} | {n for walk in walks for u, _, v in walk for n in (u, v)}
    return [f"{frm}|{u}|{rel}|{v}|{to}" for frm, u, rel, v, to in facts_in_window(G, nodes, start, end)]

G = typed_graph()
rows = event_briefing(G, "2024 Halden Reef bleaching event",
                      [("^DETECTED", "PROJECT"), ("^RUNS", "ORGANIZATION")],   # event <- project <- organization
                      start="2024-02", end="2025-02")                          # six months either side
print(len(rows))
print("\n".join(rows))

def rankings(q: str, n: int = 20) -> dict[str, list[str]]:
    return {
        "bm25": [unit_ids[i] for i, _ in bm25.search(q, n)],
        "vector": [u for u, _, _ in index.text_unit_store.search(index.embedder.embed([q])[0], k=n)],

    }

    docs = {index.text_units[u].doc_id for u in units[:k]}
    return len(docs & gold) / len(gold)

           "rrf equal": [1.0, 1.0, 1.0], "rrf graph x2": [1.0, 1.0, 2.0], "rrf bm25 x2": [2.0, 1.0, 1.0]}

Interpretation matters more than the exact numbers, which depend on your embedder and extractor. With the offline HashingEmbedder and MockLLM, the “dense” retriever is really another bag-of-words matcher and the graph is noisy, so do not be surprised if equal-weight RRF lands below BM25 alone. Fusion helps when retrievers are individually decent and make different mistakes. If one retriever is much weaker, equal weights let it push good items down; weighting the strong retriever up, or dropping the weak one, fixes that. Repeat with a real embedder (get_embedder("st")) and an LLM-built graph, and the dense and graph lists usually become strong enough for fusion to pay off, especially on the multi-hop questions. Always break results down by question type: fusion that helps multi-hop questions and hurts simple ones may call for routing rather than more fusion.

Exercise 21.5 Stretch

Using facts_in_window, answer canonical questions 6 and 7 for the windows 2024-01 to 2024-12 (supplier), 2026-03 (CTO) and 2026-04 (CTO). One of these gives an ambiguous result. Explain why, and change the interval semantics so that each month has exactly one CTO. What does your change cost for facts dated only by year?

Show answer

2024: the only SUPPLIED fact touching Kestrel Labs is Deepcast (2022-08 to 2025-06), so the answer is Deepcast. 2026-04: only Priya Nair's CTO_OF (from 2026-03, open) overlaps. 2026-03 is ambiguous: Vehl's role has valid_to = "2026-03" and Nair's has valid_from = "2026-03", and the inclusive overlap test keeps both.

Fix: treat intervals as half-open, [valid_from, valid_to), so a fact is valid up to but not including its end month:

def overlaps_half_open(d, start, end):
    f = _to_date(d.get("valid_from"), date.min)
    t = _to_date(d.get("valid_to"), date.max, end=False)       # first month it is no longer valid
    return f <= _to_date(end, date.max, end=True) and t > _to_date(start, date.min)

Now March 2026 returns only Priya Nair. This is the semantics minigraphrag/temporal.py uses: its is_valid tests valid_from <= at < valid_to, and holders(kestrel_timeline(), "CTO_OF", "Kestrel Labs", "2026-03") returns only Priya Nair. The cost shows up with year-only dates. Mira Okafor's FORMERLY_WORKED_AT Tidewater has valid_to = "2021", which _to_date(..., end=False) reads as January 2021, so the half-open test says she had left by February 2021, which the corpus does not say. Either store an explicit precision next to each date and treat a year-only end as “end of that year”, or keep dates at the precision the source gives and report uncertainty (“Vehl until March 2026, Nair from March 2026”) instead of forcing one answer. Chapter 27's bi-temporal model stores both when a fact was true and when the system learned it, which also handles corrections.

Check your understanding

In BM25, what happens to a passage's score for a term as that term's frequency in the passage grows from 1 to 10?

  1. It grows linearly, ten times the single-hit score
  2. It stays constant after the first occurrence regardless of k1
  3. It grows with diminishing returns and approaches IDF × (k1 + 1)
  4. It decreases because long passages are penalized
The tf component f(k1 + 1)/(f + k1 · norm) saturates toward k1 + 1 as f grows. Only as k1 approaches 0 does it become constant after the first occurrence.

Why does min-max normalization followed by a weighted sum often fuse badly?

  1. It stretches tiny, meaningless score differences in one list to the full 0 to 1 range
  2. It can only fuse two lists
  3. It ignores items that appear in only one list
  4. It requires the lists to have the same length
A retriever that scores three passages 0.39, 0.40, 0.41 is saying “no idea”, but min-max turns that into 0, 0.5 and 1, as confident as a list with a clear winner.

With RRF and k = 60, an item ranked first by only the graph retriever competes with an item ranked second by both BM25 and dense. Which scores higher, and what is the GraphRAG-specific concern?

  1. The graph item, because first place always wins
  2. They tie, because RRF ignores position
  3. The graph item, because graph retrievers get a bonus by default
  4. The item found by two retrievers (about 0.032 vs 0.016), which can bury multi-hop evidence only the graph finds
1/62 + 1/62 = 0.0323 beats 1/61 = 0.0164. That is usually a feature, but graph-only evidence is often the reason you added a graph, so weight the graph list or reserve slots for it.

Why is a cross-encoder applied only to the top 20 to 50 fused candidates rather than to the whole corpus?

  1. Cross-encoders cannot read passages longer than 50 tokens
  2. It needs one joint forward pass per (query, passage) pair at query time, so its cost grows with the number of candidates
  3. Cross-encoders are less accurate than BM25 on large sets
  4. Passage vectors from a cross-encoder must be recomputed every day
A bi-encoder precomputes passage vectors once; a cross-encoder cannot, because it encodes query and passage together. That accuracy costs a model call per candidate.

The metapath Organization -ACQUIRED-> Organization -SUPPLIED-> Organization suddenly returns nothing after a re-index, although the corpus did not change. What is the most likely cause?

  1. RRF weights were reset
  2. The temporal window is too narrow
  3. Extraction produced different relation labels (for example SUPPLIES_TO) or types, so no edge matches the pattern
  4. The graph has too many hubs
Metapaths match labels exactly. They depend on a stable schema, so normalize relation labels after extraction and test metapaths as part of indexing.

In the event-centered briefing, three undated facts (LOCATED_IN, BOARD_MEMBER_OF, COMPETES_WITH) appeared in a 2024 window. What does that reveal?

  1. The metapath expanded too far
  2. The window treats a missing date as “always valid”, a recall-over-precision choice you must make explicitly
  3. The facts were extracted with wrong dates
  4. Temporal windows cannot be combined with metapaths
Defaulting a missing date to date.min or date.max makes undated facts overlap every window. Sometimes that is right (location rarely changes), sometimes it leaks stale facts; you can flag undated facts in the panel instead.

Questions learners often ask

Should I use a vector database's built-in hybrid search instead of writing RRF myself?

In production, often yes: Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Weaviate, Qdrant and others offer BM25 plus vector search with RRF or weighted fusion in one query, which saves a network round trip and keeps pagination correct. Write it yourself once, as here, so you know what the knobs do, and keep your own fusion when one of the lists comes from outside that database, as graph rankings usually do.

Does BM25 still matter when embeddings keep getting better?

Yes, for the queries embeddings are structurally bad at: exact identifiers, rare proper names, codes, numbers and quoted phrases. It is also a strong baseline that costs nothing to run and catches embedding regressions. Learned sparse retrievers such as SPLADE sit between the two, but plain BM25 remains the usual keyword leg of a hybrid system.

How do I pick RRF weights without labeled data?

Start equal. Then write 30 to 50 questions with the documents that answer them; that small set is enough to see whether a retriever is dragging results down (Exercise 21.3). Weight changes that do not move recall on that set are not worth keeping. If you truly have no labels, a safer move than weights is slot reservation: guarantee each retriever its top one or two items in the context.

Are metapaths just Cypher queries?

A metapath is a pattern; a Cypher MATCH clause is one way to execute it. The distinction matters for where it comes from. Metapaths are usually curated per question type and reused, which makes them reviewable and fast. Text2Cypher generates a pattern per question, which covers novel questions but can hallucinate relation types. A good combination is to give the Text2Cypher prompt your curated metapaths as examples.

Is “panels” an established term?

Not as a standard research term. We took the name from the Thinking Loop list and gave it the interpretation described above, because structuring context into labeled evidence sections is a well-established practice (GraphRAG local search's tables are one example). If you read a different definition elsewhere, the underlying engineering question is the same: how to split a token budget across kinds of evidence.

Key takeaways

  • Keyword, dense and graph retrievers fail on different questions; hybrid systems run several and merge them.
  • BM25 is IDF times saturated, length-normalized term frequency; k1 controls saturation and b length normalization.
  • RRF fuses by rank, needs no score calibration, and with k ≈ 60 rewards agreement across lists. Weight or reserve slots for graph-only evidence so fusion does not bury it.
  • Cross-encoders and graph-aware rerankers are affordable only on a short list; routing avoids running retrievers that will hurt.
  • The seven designs (neighborhood expansion, metapaths, subgraph assembly, temporal windows, hybrid rerankers, panels, provenance) are composable; each has a characteristic failure you should test for.
  • Seed on an event, walk a metapath, apply a time window: that combination turns a sprawling graph into a focused, dated briefing.
