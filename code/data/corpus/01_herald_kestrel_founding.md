# Two Scientists Bet on Robot Swimmers for Port Avalon's Waters

*Port Avalon Herald, Business section. Published 4 October 2021.*

A converted boat shed on the north quay is the unlikely headquarters of Port Avalon's newest technology company. Kestrel Labs, founded this year by marine scientist Dr. Mira Okafor and engineer Jonas Vehl, wants to build small autonomous underwater drones that can watch over coral reefs for months at a time without a crew on the surface.

"Most of what we know about reefs comes from a few divers on a few good-weather days," said Okafor, who serves as chief executive of the new company. "The ocean does not wait for good weather. We want instruments that stay in the water."

Before starting the company, Okafor spent six years as a senior researcher at Tidewater Institute, the marine research institute that sits a short walk from the quay. From 2015 to 2021 she ran long-term water-quality studies there, and she says the frustration of losing data every time a research vessel went into dry dock is what pushed her toward robotics. She left Tidewater Institute in the spring of 2021 to start Kestrel Labs, but she describes the institute as "the reason Port Avalon is the right place for this company."

Her co-founder brings the hardware. Jonas Vehl, the company's chief technology officer, spent a decade building acoustic instruments for offshore survey firms before the two met at a conference on ocean observation. As CTO, Vehl leads a team of four engineers and is responsible for the drone's navigation and sensing systems. He is blunt about the difficulty.

"Underwater, GPS doesn't work, radio barely works, and the light is gone after thirty metres," Vehl said. "Everything is sound. If you get the sonar right, the rest of the drone follows."

Kestrel Labs is registered in Port Avalon and currently employs nine people, most of them former students of the regional university. The founders say they chose to stay in Port Avalon rather than move to a larger technology hub because the city offers quick access to open water and to the scientists who will use their instruments.

The company has so far been funded by the founders' savings and a small innovation grant. Okafor said Kestrel Labs will begin talking to venture investors next year, once a prototype has completed sea trials.

Not everyone is convinced that a startup can compete in underwater robotics, a field dominated by large contractors that build vehicles for navies and oil companies. But Okafor argues that environmental monitoring is a different market. "Defense vehicles are designed to be expensive and rare," she said. "Reef monitoring needs drones that are cheap enough to lose."

The first Kestrel Labs prototype, a torpedo-shaped vehicle roughly the length of a surfboard, is expected to be tested in the sheltered waters of the harbour before the end of the year.
